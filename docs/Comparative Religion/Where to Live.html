Methodology

228 regions × 16 factors. Three views in one tool: numeric ratings, descriptive cells, and user-weighted rankings.

Why does maxing one weight not change rankings as much as expected?

With 16 factors and uniform default weights, each factor contributes ~6% to the total. Maxing one slider to 10 brings it to about 40% — meaningful, but not dominant. If you want a single factor to actually veto regions that score poorly on it, you have two stronger tools:

Minimum thresholds: Drop any region scoring below your floor on a chosen factor. Set, say, State Conduct ≥ 60 to filter out complicit states entirely.

Ethics penalty (in the Scoring section): Subtract (100 − state-conduct-rating) × intensity from every region's total. At intensity 1.0, a region with state conduct of 20 loses 80 points off its score, regardless of what its other factors look like. This is closer to the ethical claim "you can't outweigh complicity in atrocities with good schools." Adjust intensity to taste — at 0.5, the penalty is half-strength; at 2.0, double.

Aggregate view

The View → Aggregate toggle rolls the 228 regions up into 13 continental groups (US, Canada, Western Europe & Nordics, MENA, Sub-Saharan Africa, etc.) by averaging the ratings of their constituents. Useful for a high-altitude comparison. The group rating is the simple arithmetic mean of its members; the descriptive cell for each factor shows the range across constituents.

Rating direction

All ratings are normalized so higher = more of the named trait. For factors where high raw values are usually undesirable (Cost of Living, Tax Burden), the rating is inverted — high cost gives a low affordability rating, high taxes give a low tax-efficiency rating. This makes the weighted total intuitive: bigger total = stronger fit for your weights.

State conduct framework

Per the user's instruction, funding or supplying states that commit genocide is weighted as a major negative — applied consistently.

The United States receives a state-conduct rating of approximately 33–35 due to: military aid to Israel during the Gaza war (ICJ provisional measures in force, ICC arrest warrants for Netanyahu and Gallant, ~45,000+ Palestinian deaths per Gaza Health Ministry), strikes on Iran's nuclear sites in June 2025, capture of Maduro in January 2026, continuing Houthi strikes, drug-boat strikes in the Caribbean/Pacific. Defensive aid to Ukraine is treated as more neutral but does not offset the above.

Israel itself receives ~20 as direct perpetrator of the conduct in question. UAE receives ~22 for alleged RSF arms supply during Sudan's RSF-perpetrated genocide. Iran ~15 (Hamas/Hezbollah/Houthi support + 2022-23 protest killings). China ~18 (Xinjiang, Tibet, Hong Kong, support of Myanmar junta). Russia ~8 (invasion of Ukraine). Myanmar ~5 (junta atrocities + Rohingya). North Korea ~3 (totalitarian, troops to Russia). Sudan/South Sudan ~12 (RSF-controlled regions of active genocide).

Conversely, states with low/zero foreign military adventurism and no domestic atrocity record score higher: Switzerland ~90 (armed neutrality), Iceland ~92, Costa Rica region ~92 (no army), Ireland ~88 (neutral), Portugal ~88, Austria ~88, Bhutan ~88. Mainstream democracies with active foreign roles fall in between: Japan ~80 (pacifist constitution + Ukraine aid only), Germany ~65 (Ukraine aid + smaller Israel exports), France ~65, UK ~50.

Subjectivity disclosure

Every rating is a judgment call. Different reviewers applying the same framework would land within ±10 in most cases. Treat the numbers as starting points, not verdicts. The Weights and Thresholds controls let you express your own framework over them.

Time window

State conduct covers approximately 2021–2026 (last 5 years). All other factors describe present-day conditions. A 25-year or 75-year window would surface different patterns (Iraq War, post-9/11 detention, Tiananmen legacy, colonial histories).

Civil liberties scope

Per the user's prior instruction, LGBTQ legal status, abortion access, recreational-drug policy, and euthanasia are described in the cells but do not affect the numeric civil liberties rating. They remain available for personal judgment in the detail view.

Personal context (class)

The "Personal context" sidebar lets you tell Atlas which economic class you'd live this life from. It applies multipliers on top of your weight sliders. Six profiles: Poverty, Working class, Middle class (the neutral baseline), Affluent, Wealthy (top 1%), and Ultra-wealthy. The same region rated 30/100 on Affordability is catastrophic for someone in poverty and irrelevant for an ultra-wealthy mover — the multipliers encode that. For Poverty, Affordability is 3× weighted, Healthcare and public Education matter more, Tax Efficiency barely registers (low income, low absolute tax). For Ultra-wealthy, Tax Efficiency is 3.5× weighted, Mobility is 2.5× (citizenship-by-investment territory), Affordability shrinks to 0.15×, and ethical factors (State Conduct, Civil Liberties, Rule of Law) rise. This is intentionally schematic — real lived experience at every income level is more varied than six labels can capture — but it surfaces a layer of analysis the raw weighted total can't.

Religious profiles

The five tradition profiles (Buddhist, Christian, Hindu, Islamic, Jewish) encode one reading of what someone holding that worldview might emphasize when choosing where to live — typically heavier Family Life, Community/Culture, Civil Liberties (especially religious freedom), and ethics-of-state-conduct, with tradition-specific accents (Buddhist Environment, Hindu and Jewish Education, Islamic Family + ethics, etc.). Adherents within any tradition vary enormously, and a serious mover should treat the preset as a starting point and adjust every slider. The presets do not reflect doctrine, jurisprudence, or specific religious-law requirements (kosher availability, halal slaughter, mosque/temple/synagogue access, recreational-drug rulings, etc.) — those are best evaluated through the Culture/Family/Civil-Liberties description text per region.

Known limitations

One cell per region collapses real within-region variation. Training data is denser for the US, Western Europe, India, and China than for Central Asia, the Pacific Islands, or smaller African states — ratings for less-covered regions are correspondingly more uncertain. Factors are correlated in reality (rich countries have low crime AND good healthcare AND strong rule of law), so the weighted total risks double-counting. Climate change is not its own factor and is not projected forward.
