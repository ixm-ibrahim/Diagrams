1. Our prayer for guidance

Every time we stand in prayer, we ask Allah:

اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ

"Guide us to the straight path."

Seventeen times across the five daily prayers, at least. We're taught to ask for guidance, over and over again. And the Quran itself opens with this surah, al-Fatiha, ending with that prayer for guidance.

Then, right at the beginning of the next surah, we read:

ذَٰلِكَ الْكِتَابُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ هُدًى لِلْمُتَّقِينَ

"That is the Book in which there is no doubt - a guidance for those mindful of Allah." (2:2)

Subhanallah: we ask Allah for guidance, and immediately we're introduced to the Book as guidance. So, let's look at how it answers that prayer.

Look at the structure of the next chapter. It starts off by describing different kinds of people: people who believe, people who reject, people who say they believe but don't. And then, there's a short account of the story of Prophet Adam (as), which then jumps to a series of stories about the Children of Israel. Later, we encounter snippets about Prophet Sulayman, and then Prophet Ibrahim - who lived long before him. Sometimes we're hearing about people who lived thousands of years ago, and then suddenly Allah is speaking directly to the people reading. And throughout all of these snippets are commands, warnings, prayers, and reminders.

It's interesting when you think about it: a story or history book would normally take us through events in chronological order, teaching us through visualizing the narrative. A science or mathematics textbook starts from the basics before naturally moving to the more advanced ideas - teaching us how reality works. But in the Quran, the subjects keep changing, bringing these different people, events, and teachings together - almost as if its guidance transcends time and space itself.

So why does the Quran move between all these different subjects? In what way does guidance manifest? How does this Book want to be read and understood?

pause

What's remarkable is that we can already follow what we're reading before we understand how all these subjects fit together. Its words move us, sometimes to tears, even when we've heard them many times before. A command teaches us how to live. A warning makes us reconsider what we're doing. A story helps us recognize something about ourselves. But when we read carefully, we find moments that make us... pause. And as we reflect more deeply, a passage we thought we understood opens up even further — exposing knowledge and wisdom on a whole nother level.

This khutbah is about one of those moments. Most of us know the story of Adam (as). He was put in the Garden, disobeyed God and ate from the tree, and that's why we're here on earth. But in the Quran, before we even hear about Adam or the Garden or the tree, the angels ask Allah a very interesting question: why create beings who will cause corruption and shed blood, when we already glorify and praise You?

pause

Wait a minute: is this... the Problem of Evil, in modern philosophy? Are they asking... what is the point of creating us, when He has creatures that already worship Him perfectly? Beings who can disobey Him, and can experience and cause so much suffering?

This is a question people have always asked: why are we here? What is the purpose of this life, and all the suffering we experience? Most of us remember this story as a lesson about disobeying Allah and how we ended up here on earth. But just by pausing over the angels' question, we begin to see how the Quran is making a far deeper point.

So how does Allah answer them? Let's follow what happens next, and see how the account of Adam develops that answer.

Speaker: Move directly from the prayer to the opening of al-Baqarah. Let the movement between subjects sound like something you're noticing together. The main pause comes after the questions about how to read the Book.

Positioning: the room connects its daily prayer with the Quran, becomes curious about how the Book guides, and enters Adam's account through a question that concerns its own existence.

2. The angels' question

Allah tells the angels:

إِنِّي جَاعِلٌ فِي الْأَرْضِ خَلِيفَةً

"I am placing a khalifah on earth." A human representative - a positive role, by the way - with the responsibility for taking care of the earth. And the angels ask:

أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَن يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ

"Will You place there those who cause corruption and shed blood, while we glorify You with praise and declare Your holiness?" (2:30)

Think about that question for a second. They already worship Allah. They already praise Him, perfectly, as He deserves. And now there will be creatures on earth who can do terrible things. Who hurt one another. Who kill one another. And certainly won't be worshipping God as He deserves. What is the wisdom in creating them?

This is such a powerful question. We look at what's happening in the world, at what people do to each other, and sometimes it's hard to see anything else. Why would Allah create beings capable of doing all this to each other?

So what does Allah say?

إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

"I know what you do not know."

At first, that might sound like the end of the discussion. Allah knows, and they don't. And sometimes that's where we stop too, and how we answer people who ask similar questions: “Allah knows best” — which is true, of course. But we can take that as a reason to stop reflecting, as if there's nothing more for us to understand. pause

And the Quran actually keeps going. Allah teaches Adam the names of all things, then presents them to the angels and asks them to do the same thing. They can't - something so simple, of identifying new things. They acknowledge that they only know what He has taught them. And then Allah tells Adam to inform them of the names - something so simple for us to do - and he can.

Subhanallah, this being who sins and will cause corruption, has some capacity for intelligence and knowledge that they themselves don't have. Already, there's more to him than what the angels revealed in their question - and Allah is using the act of teaching Adam the names of all things to demonstrate it.

But how does teaching Adam the names answer the angels' question? What does this tell us about why we're here?

Speaker: Let the angels' question carry its seriousness without sounding accusatory. Pause briefly before "Allah teaches him."

Positioning: the listener has a personal stake in the answer. The final line directs attention to Allah's gift before the familiar account of disobedience.

3. What we do with what we're taught

Allah then tells them something very subtle. He says:

أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ إِنِّي أَعْلَمُ غَيْبَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَأَعْلَمُ مَا تُبْدُونَ وَمَا كُنتُمْ تَكْتُمُونَ

“Did I not tell you that I know what is unseen in the heavens and the earth, and I know what you reveal and what you conceal?” (2:33)

What they revealed, we heard: human beings will cause corruption and shed blood. I mean, isn't that what history is - the study of human war and political dominance? They got that part right. But... what did they conceal? If you think about it, it's obvious: they left out our capacity to do tremendous good - the thing that isn't as obvious as the sins that we do. And what about our capacity to learn, to understand - what Allah previously demonstrated? They didn't mention that.

Look at what happens next. Allah commands the angels to prostrate before Adam — to honor this being He has created and taught and has this capacity that they don't have.

The angels obey. But Iblis refuses.

Iblis is the one we know as Shaytan. He was there among the angels, although the Quran tells us elsewhere that he was one of the jinn. (18:50) And when this command comes, the Quran says:

أَبَىٰ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ وَكَانَ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ

“He refused, and was arrogant, and was among the disbelievers.” (2:34)

Notice: he knows Allah exists — but willingly rejects His command. His disbelief here is refusing to act on what he knows is true, prioritizing his own pride instead. "I am better than him", he says elsewhere in the Quran. "You created me from fire, and You created him from clay." (7:12)

What kind of answer is that? Allah gives a command, and Iblis starts explaining why the person in front of him doesn't deserve it. He understands what he's been asked to do. But accepting it would mean honoring someone he thinks is beneath him.

So now we can see two very different responses. The angels acknowledge what they don't know, and obey. Iblis defends his own pride, and refuses. So there was actually more to the angels' question that was concealed, than just our capacity for good: it was why they were asking. And in this demonstration, Iblis' pride was also revealed.

This is a universal lesson for us: sometimes someone corrects us, and before we've even considered whether they're right, we're thinking: "Who are you to tell me?" Maybe they're younger than us, and we feel like they need to respect our status more. Maybe they're older than us, and we feel like they need to respect our intelligence more. Maybe they know less than us about something entirely different that we think is better. Maybe we just don't like them, or their tone was annoying. The correction might be completely right, but we're too busy with our own pride, deciding whether we deserve to be corrected or not.

This is actually a difficult thing to admit about ourselves. Because we all like to think that if we know what's right, we'll do it. But this part of the story reveals another obstacle: even though we can understand what is being asked of us, we can still refuse because of our pride.

Speaker: Keep the angels' humble response distinct from Iblis's arrogance. "Who are you to tell me?" should sound recognizable, not theatrical. All application stays in "we."

Positioning: the account moves from knowledge to the response it receives. Iblis's own refusal exposes his pride, preparing the listener for Adam's choice and his different response after disobedience.

4. The tree

pause Now, after all this, Adam and his spouse are finally placed in the Garden. Allah provides for them, and tells them to eat freely, wherever they want. But there is one condition:

وَلَا تَقْرَبَا هَٰذِهِ الشَّجَرَةَ

"Do not approach... this tree." (2:35)

And later on in the Quran, Allah also warns them about Iblis: "this is an enemy to you and your wife. Don't let him drive you out of the Garden." (20:117)

So they have provision. They have a command. And they have a warning about who their enemy is.

And as we know, Shaytan still tempts them. He makes disobeying Allah look like something that will help them, as with all temptation we experience. He tricks them, saying that "it is the tree of immortality and gives a kingdom that never fades." (20:120) And they both slip - a momentary lapse in their otherwise perfect obedience - and are brought out of the innocent state they were in. This part of the story we all know.

Now, if we stopped here, what would we have learned? Allah taught them. Allah warned them. They disobeyed anyway. We might think: well, isn't that exactly the concern the angels raised? Didn't Iblis prove this to be true? If given the chance, these creatures can go wrong.

But the story still doesn't end here. pause

فَتَلَقَّىٰ آدَمُ مِن رَّبِّهِ كَلِمَاتٍ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ إِنَّهُ هُوَ التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ

"Then Adam received words from his Lord, and He accepted his repentance. He is the One who accepts repentance, the Merciful." (2:37)

Look at that. After Adam disobeys, Allah teaches him how to return. In the same way Allah taught him all of his knowledge, He also taught him how to seek forgiveness. Later on in the Quran, we learn what those words are:

رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَا أَنفُسَنَا وَإِن لَّمْ تَغْفِرْ لَنَا وَتَرْحَمْنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

"Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. If You don't forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be among the losers." (7:23)

"We have wronged ourselves." No explanation of why they were too important to obey. They didn't defend themselves, or deflect blame to another, or try to blame God on why the tree was there in the first place. They acknowledge what they did, and ask for mercy.

Remember Iblis's answer: "I am better than him." Now listen to theirs: "Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves."

Both of them disobeyed God. But what matters is how they were different: one insisted on his superiority, on being right, while the other was humble and sought to be corrected and forgiven. And they are the ones who Allah accepts their return. And only after all this, they are finally placed on earth, with the same provision and guidance as before - but now, they know what to do to be successful.

Speaker: The real stop is before Adam receives the words. Recite the dua slowly; it will return at the close. Let the two responses sit next to each other without adding a slogan.

Positioning: the listener feels why the account must continue beyond the sin. Repentance appears as something Allah makes possible and teaches us to practice.

5. Preparing for life here

So that's the account — only ten verses. A lesson most of us already knew, but look at how much there is to consider when we pause over it - and this is just scratching the surface, by the way, there's so much that is left out. But let's bring what we've seen together by going back to where the story started. Remember: where did Allah say He was placing humanity at the beginning? pause

On earth. Before the Garden. Before the slip. Before any of this happened, earth was already announced as our destination - that's where we were meant to be.

So why was Adam first placed in the Garden? Before this, Adam hadn't experienced what it meant to disobey and then seek forgiveness. Now, he has learned what to do when he goes wrong. It's like a study guide for the test ahead, that is life on earth - and our life on earth is a test. And it's amazing: the same lesson Adam was taught then, is the same lesson we are grasping now, in pondering and reflecting on this story.

The main point seems to be exposing what our relationship with Allah looks like - a fitting lesson to put in the story of how mankind began. Just like in the Garden: Allah provides, He teaches, He sets up boundaries, and warns us about our enemy. And when we slip, we are taught on how to return, for He is the most forgiving, the ever-merciful.

So let's return to the angels' question. Why create beings who cause corruption and shed blood, when the angels already worship Allah perfectly?

It's about our free will - something the angels don't have - to choose to return to God, even when there are other tempting options in this life. There's more to the story than our ability to sin, as many people seem to be stuck on. We can be taught. We can recognize what is right, and choose to act on it. And even after we sin, we can acknowledge our wrongdoing and willingly seek our Lord's forgiveness - this matters more to our Creator than the sin itself, us returning to Him. Iblis didn't do that - he clung to his pride that he cares about so much. But Adam did - and this is our father, a Prophet of God, an example by which we follow. This is our purpose in life.

Speaker: Pause after asking where humanity was to be placed. Present the "study guide" as a reflection on what the experience teaches. Let the final sentence land before the closing formula and sitting.

Positioning: the first half brings the account together. The listener recognizes their own life in its gifts, boundaries, choices, and return. The promise of guidance will open the second half.

أَقُولُ قَوْلِي هَٰذَا وَأَسْتَغْفِرُ اللَّهَ لِي وَلَكُمْ وَلِسَائِرِ الْمُسْلِمِينَ، فَاسْتَغْفِرُوهُ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ.

6. The guidance continues

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ، وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ.

All praise belongs to Allah, and blessings and peace be upon His Messenger.

At the end of Adam’s account, Allah promises:

فَإِمَّا يَأْتِيَنَّكُم مِّنِّي هُدًى فَمَن تَبِعَ هُدَايَ فَلَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

“When guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will have no fear upon them, nor will they grieve.” (2:38)

And remember: the Book we’re reading has already introduced itself as that guidance.

So let’s return to our opening question. Why does the Quran move between all these different subjects? What happens when we keep reading the rest of the chapter with these lessons in mind?

For the sake of time, I’ll just summarize. The surah turns to the Children of Israel, where Allah reminds them of His favors, of their wrongdoing, and of the repentance He accepted - even when they worshipped a golden calf instead of God, and where arrogant with Moses and the prophet and caused corruption in the earth. Different people, hundreds, if not thousands of generations later — but now, we recognize the relationship. Allah provides and teaches, people sin, but because there is more to them than just their sin, He opens the way to return and accepts their repentance, should they act on it. (2:49–54)

Later, we are warned about people whose words captivate us and impress us, but they’re the kind of people who spread corruption in the earth. And when he’s told to be mindful of Allah, his pride carries him into sin. (2:204–206)

Hmm. Corruption on earth. Pride being revealed when called to obey… We’ve heard these things before, in the angels’ question and Iblis’ response. These are giving the same lesson, but from different angles, so we can better recognize them and be prepared.

But the surah also teaches us the good we are capable of choosing. Give from wealth we love. Care for those in need. Keep our promises. Be patient through hardship. (2:177) The good that wasn’t mentioned in the angels’ question is now being taught to us through everyday choices. Adam was given a boundary concerning the tree. These commands are the choices before us, in our own test of life.

Even the warning that Allah gave Adam shows up again: don’t follow Shaytan’s footsteps, and beware of slipping after guidance has come. (2:208–209) We already know this situation, through Adam, and we’ve learned how to return.

And the surah acknowledges the difficulties this life will involve: Allah tells us we will be tested with fear, hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and crops. Then He gives good news to those who are patient, and teaches us what to say when hardship reaches us: (2:155–156)

إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّا إِلَيْهِ رَاجِعُونَ

“We belong to Allah, and to Him we return.” (2:156)

Allah promises those who respond with patience His blessings and mercy, and calls them guided. Just as Adam received words from his Lord, we are being taught what to say, and how to understand our situation, when we face hardship.

Speaker: Keep the examples moving. Pause briefly at “We’ve heard these things before,” then slow down for the words about hardship.

Positioning: the listener experiences how the surah’s different subjects develop the relationships introduced in Adam’s account.

7. Reading and responding

So these subjects don’t seem so separate anymore. The stories, commands, and prayers help us understand one another — and help us understand ourselves.

We have only looked at a handful of connections in one surah. How much more might we discover if we kept reading?

Allah says about this Book:

كِتَابٌ أَنزَلْنَاهُ إِلَيْكَ مُبَارَكٌ لِّيَدَّبَّرُوا آيَاتِهِ وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

“A blessed Book We have sent down to you, so that they may reflect on its signs, and people of understanding may take heed.” (38:29)

Subhanallah, even as the Quran teaches us about the understanding Allah gave humanity, it calls us to use that understanding ourselves.

So let’s make a little time, each day or each week, to read with the meaning and reflect. What is Allah teaching us? Where do we recognize this in our own lives? And what should we do about it?

When we’re corrected, remember Iblis’s pride. When we’re tempted, remember Adam’s tree. And when we sin, remember the words Allah taught him. This is how what we read begins to change what we do.

We began today with:

اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ

“Guide us to the straight path.”

In the prayer we’re about to stand for, we’ll ask again. Let’s remember the guidance Allah has placed before us — and make time to understand and follow it.

Speaker: Let the three practical reminders stay brief. The final return to al-Fatiha closes the question raised at the beginning.

Positioning: the listener leaves with a concrete commitment to read, reflect, and respond.
