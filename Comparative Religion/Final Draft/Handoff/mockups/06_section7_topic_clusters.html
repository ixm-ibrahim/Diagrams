Topic 7.1

Christology

The nature, mission, and return of Jesus across the two traditions
↳ Ties back to Tier 5 · Coherence
1 · Ontological nature of Jesus
ChristianityDivine + human
Second Person of the Trinity, incarnated as a fully human being while remaining fully God.
IslamProphet
Messiah, born of a virgin, sent as a messenger of God — fully human, not divine; Q4:171, Q5:75.
Synthesis
Both affirm Jesus as a singular figure; they diverge on whether divine and human natures can coexist in one person.
↳ Ties back to Tier 4 · Integrity
2 · The crucifixion event
ChristianityCrucified, died, rose
The crucifixion is the central salvific event; resurrection on the third day vindicates the atonement.
IslamNot crucified
Raised to God before the crucifixion attempt; one was made to appear like him; Q4:157–158.
Synthesis
Christianity treats the crucifixion as the central salvific event; Islam treats it as a historical event divinely interrupted.
↳ Ties back to Tier 6 · Robustness
3 · Second coming / return
ChristianityReturns as judge
Second coming inaugurates the final judgment; living and dead are resurrected and judged.
IslamReturns, defeats dajjal
Returns near the end of time, defeats the antichrist (dajjal); final judgment is by God.
Synthesis
Both expect a return; Christianity assigns Jesus the judgment role, Islam reserves judgment for God alone.
Topic synthesis · Christology
Across all three christological tests, the two traditions diverge on Jesus's ontology, the necessity of his crucifixion, and his eschatological role. The split reduces to one metaphysical commitment: can a divine being become embodied? Christianity says yes and treats the incarnation, death, and resurrection as the central salvific arc; Islam says no and treats Jesus as the highest of the prophets within a strict monotheism. Every downstream christological disagreement traces back to this single yes/no.
