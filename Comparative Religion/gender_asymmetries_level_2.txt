================================================================================
OBSERVED ASYMMETRIES IN HOW MEN AND WOMEN ARE TREATED
LAYER 2 — inferences drawn from Layer 1


WRITING GOAL

Demystification. Use plain, common language and keep cognitive load low,
while staying precise enough that a specialist would agree it's
accurate. Be hard on the topic, soft on the reader: anticipate where
someone might feel defensive, but don't soften what's actually true.
Where something is uncertain, contested, or where an expert would
object, surface that directly in plain terms — don't hide it behind
jargon, hedging, emotional appeals, or common misreadings the reader
might bring in.

Drifts to avoid:
  (1) academic drift — reaching for Latinate words, nominalizations, or
      jargon when a common word works.
  (2) bland drift — hedging or both-sidesing until no real claim is
      made.
  (3) false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would
      object to.
  (4) emotional cushioning that buries the actual point.
If accessibility and precision seem to conflict in a sentence, flag
that sentence rather than silently picking one.

Self-check before releasing a version: a smart non-expert should
understand it on first read; a specialist should read it and not
wince; no sentence should hedge without saying anything. If any of
those fail, rewrite.


WHAT LAYER 2 IS

Layer 1 is observations — individual facts, stats, and patterns that
can each be checked against the world. Layer 2 is inferences drawn
across those observations: rules, regularities, or structural claims
that the observations together support. A Layer 2 entry is not itself
a direct observation; it's a claim about what the observations imply
when you stand back.

Each entry lists the Layer 1 observations it draws from. If the
support is removed or shown to be wrong, the inference falls with it.

Clusters of inferences — several Layer 2 entries that seem to share a
deeper pattern — are fine. That shared pattern is Layer 3 material,
not a reason to collapse the Layer 2 entries. Entries are only merged
when they are genuinely identical.


FOUR FILTER CRITERIA

Each inference must pass all four to stay in the main list:

  (1) Both-sidedness. The inference survives looking at both
      directions of the asymmetry. It doesn't only work when read as
      "women are favored" or only as "men are favored"; the structural
      claim holds when each side's cases are tested against it.

  (2) Smallest interpretive move. The inference is the smallest step
      the observations support. It doesn't reach past them to bigger
      causal, historical, or psychological claims that the
      observations alone don't establish.

  (3) Specialist-wince test. A specialist in the relevant domain
      (family law, criminology, sociology, etc.) might disagree with
      the framing but would not say the claim is factually wrong or
      technically sloppy.

  (4) Falsifiability. There is a concrete class of evidence that
      would force the inference to be revised or dropped. An
      inference that can't be broken by any finding is not admitted.

Entries that fail an audit are moved to the staging file with the
readmission condition that would let them back in.

LAYER 2: INFERENCES
================================================================================


I1. Arguments about gender are commonly judged by tone and by the
    speaker's supposed motives before they are judged by content.
    On loaded topics, a calm analytical register and a known-to-be
    contrarian position each function as independent reasons to
    dismiss a claim, separate from whether the claim is accurate.

    Draws from: DD1, DD2, DD4, DD5, DD8.

I2. Within a given discourse, the bar of evidence for being taken
    seriously is not the same across positions. Contrarian claims
    are held to a higher standard than mainstream ones: one factual
    error discredits the whole, motives are asked about first, and
    the burden of proof sits on the side departing from the dominant
    view.

    Draws from: DD4, DD5, DD6, DD9.

I3. Social enforcement has partially replaced argumentative
    engagement on gender topics. Positions are more often managed by
    reputational cost, labeling, and exclusion than by counter-
    argument, and speech itself is increasingly framed as a form of
    harm rather than as a claim open to contest.

    Draws from: DD6, DD7, DD8.

I4. Whether a group-level statement reads as analysis or as
    hostility depends on which group is the subject. "Men" and
    "white men" are routinely used as a group subject in public
    discourse in ways that other demographic groups are not; "men
    do X" reads as describing a pattern, while "women do X" with
    equivalent evidence reads as hostility toward women. The same
    sentence structure carries different social weight depending on
    which side it lands on.

    Draws from: LR1, LR2, LR3, LR6, LR8, DP4.

I5. Descriptive claims about female behavior, including well-
    documented ones, carry a social cost that matching claims about
    male behavior do not. The asymmetry shows up even where the
    underlying statistics are similar or stronger in the direction
    that is harder to say out loud.

    Draws from: BD1, BD2, BD3, BD4, LR1, LR4, LR5.

I6. Female responsibility and male responsibility are assessed on
    different scales. In the same interaction — a hookup gone bad,
    a fight between partners, a bad decision under influence — the
    man's choices are treated as his to own while the woman's are
    framed as caused by pressure, harm, or circumstance.

    Draws from: AC1, AC2, AC3, AC5, AC6, TA1, TA4, TA5, SR5.

I7. Claims of being hurt or wronged are weighted by who is making
    them. A woman's report against a man is treated as credible by
    default; a man's report against a woman, or a claim that a
    mainstream female-coded pattern is unfair to men, is treated as
    a move that needs explaining before it's weighed.

    Draws from: VO1, VO2, VO3, VO4, VO5, DD5.

I8. In public speech, the set of words available to describe male
    misbehavior is wider and more mainstream-usable than the set
    available for female misbehavior. Writers who reach for a harsh
    female-directed term pay a cost that writers using the male-
    directed equivalents don't.

    Draws from: LR1, LR2, LR3.

I9. Group-level accountability runs one way in current discourse.
    Men are asked to examine their behavior as a class; women being
    asked to do the same is itself treated as the problematic move.
    This is true even where the behavior at issue is well-documented
    on both sides.

    Draws from: LR5, TA6, PR4.

I10. Victim status functions as a social and political asset. Being
     positioned as the wronged party confers credibility, standing,
     and protection from criticism, which creates a real incentive
     to claim or hold the position. Any claim that a particular
     victimhood claim is strategic is itself costly to make.

     Draws from: VO1, VO2, VO3, VO4, VO10, PR5.

I11. Across domains where men and women show different outcomes,
     female-disadvantaging outcomes are named as problems and met
     with programs, funding, and policy attention, while male-
     disadvantaging outcomes with comparable or larger gaps are
     treated as unremarkable or as men's own doing. This holds
     across education, workplace deaths, homelessness, suicide,
     sentencing, combat exposure, custody, and post-divorce
     financial and asset outcomes.

     Draws from: VO3, ED1, ED2, ED3, ED4, ED5, WE1, WE2, VS1, VS2,
     HM1, HM2, HM3, HM4, HM5, FL1, FL2, FL6, FL7, FL11, FL12, FL13.

I12. Generic language gets flagged as exclusionary only when it's
     male-coded. Male-default terms (chairman, mankind, generic
     "he/him") are flagged and replaced in institutional style
     guides. Female-coded terms at comparable frequency are not
     flagged. In current writing, generic "she/her" is commonly
     treated as progressive while generic "he/him" is treated as
     sexist.

     Draws from: LR10.

I13. Individual counter-cases are accepted against a group-level
     claim when the claim runs against women, not when it runs
     against men. "Not all men" in response to a statistic about
     male behavior is treated as deflection. The structurally
     identical move in the other direction — "my wife out-earns me"
     in response to the pay-gap figure, "I've known women who abused
     their husbands" in response to DV-against-women statistics — is
     rarely produced in mainstream discourse and, when produced, is
     treated as deflection before the underlying claim is discussed.
     The same move lands differently by direction.

     Draws from: LR6, DP3, DP9.

I14. In interpersonal conflict, the same act is classified
     differently depending on the sex of the person doing it.
     Physical aggression, controlling behavior, and verbal abuse
     from a woman against a man are commonly downgraded or reframed
     as mutual, comedic, or provoked; the same behavior from a man
     against a woman is treated as its full category.

     Draws from: TA1, TA2, TA3, TA4, TA5, MR3, MR8.

I15. When institutional intake faces ambiguous or mutual facts, the
     default reading is that harm runs from man to woman. In
     domestic-violence calls with two-way aggression, the male
     partner is identified as primary aggressor at rates higher than
     incident-level facts predict. Shelters, safety campaigns, and
     crisis infrastructure are designed around female victimization
     even in domains where male victimization is numerically larger.
     This is a claim about institutional defaults, not about intent.

     Draws from: VO5, VO9, VS2, FL7, FL8.

I16. The concept of "toxic" behavior is tied to one sex as a
     category in common usage. "Toxic masculinity" is standard
     vocabulary in journalism, academia, and HR; "toxic femininity"
     is not, even as a descriptor for behaviors equally documented.

     Draws from: LR3, RT2.

I17. Humor about male inadequacy — incompetence, sexual inadequacy,
     being the butt of the joke in the family unit — is a default
     mainstream register. Equivalent humor about female inadequacy
     carries social cost and is largely absent from mainstream
     advertising, sitcoms, and greeting cards.

     Draws from: MR1, MR6, MR7, MR8.

I18. Sexual regret and sexual harm are kept in separate boxes for
     women and collapsed for men. When a woman later regrets a
     sexual encounter, there is institutional and discursive room
     to recategorize the encounter as harm; when a man later regrets
     a sexual encounter, that move is not available to him in the
     same way.

     Draws from: PF6, PF7.

I19. Same-sex conflict is read through different scripts depending
     on the sex involved. Female-on-female cruelty (exclusion,
     rumor, reputational destruction) is treated as interpersonal
     drama; male-on-male cruelty (physical, status-based) is treated
     as a social problem requiring intervention. Each is real; only
     one gets the public frame.

     Draws from: RS10.

I20. Boys' and men's educational, occupational, and health
     underperformance is treated as something they'd need to fix
     about themselves, while women's parallel underperformance in
     other domains is treated as structural and a target for
     intervention. The framing is not symmetric even when the size
     of the gap is.

     Draws from: ED1, ED2, ED3, ED4, ED5, ED6, ED7, ED8, WE6, WE7,
     HM6, HM7.

I21. Workplace death, serious injury, and deployment to dangerous
     jobs are overwhelmingly male. These outcomes are not discussed
     as a gender gap that requires correction; they are treated as
     side effects of the industries men "choose," while female
     underrepresentation in safer high-status industries is treated
     as a gap that requires policy.

     Draws from: WE1, WE2, WE3, WE4, WE5, FL5.

I22. Men's higher rates of suicide, loneliness in later life,
     incarceration, and untreated mental illness are treated as
     individual failures or inherent to being male, rather than as
     a population-level pattern that would be named as a crisis if
     the sexes were reversed.

     Draws from: HM1, HM2, HM3, HM4, HM5, HM6, HM7, HM8, WE5, RS3,
     RS4.

I23. Homelessness, addiction, and extreme poverty fall more heavily
     on men in aggregate, but public response (shelters, funding,
     campaigns) tracks female need more tightly than male need.

     Draws from: HM4, WE5, VS2, VS3, FL7, FL8.

I24. On bodily integrity of minors, non-therapeutic genital cutting
     is legally protected against in one sex and legally permitted
     in the other. Female genital cutting is prosecuted; male
     genital cutting (routine infant circumcision) is not. The
     asymmetry persists even as the broader framework claims
     children of either sex deserve equal protection.

     Draws from: FL10.

I25. For comparable offenses and comparable prior records, men
     receive longer prison sentences than women; pretrial detention
     rates run in the same direction; the size of the sex gap in
     sentencing is larger than the race gap. This is not treated as
     a civil-rights issue.

     Draws from: FL6, FL14.

I26. Military and draft exposure are formally and practically male.
     Selective Service registration applies only to men; combat
     deaths are overwhelmingly male. This asymmetry sits alongside
     a formal doctrine of gender equality and is not treated as an
     equity gap requiring correction.

     Draws from: FL5, VS6.

I27. The family-law system, in expected cases, transfers children,
     housing, and money from fathers to mothers. Custody defaults
     to the mother; child support flows from the non-custodial
     father to the custodial mother; the family home follows
     custody; alimony runs overwhelmingly from men to women. Each
     piece has its own legal justification; the combined pattern
     is that the expected outcome of a contested divorce for the
     father is less on every dimension at once.

     Draws from: FL1, FL2, FL3, FL4, FL11, FL12.

I28. Political terms for structural imbalance are available only
     when the imbalance runs one way. "Systemic," "structural," and
     "institutional" are standard for describing female-
     disadvantaging patterns; "patriarchy" stays in present tense
     even in domains where men now underperform. Comparable male-
     disadvantaging patterns (custody, sentencing, workplace death,
     suicide, homelessness, post-divorce outcomes) don't attract
     the same vocabulary; when they do, the label registers as
     partisan rather than descriptive.

     Draws from: LR7, FL13.

I29. Policies designed to correct past sex-based discrimination
     have not been paired with sunset criteria or symmetric review.
     Programs targeting female underrepresentation continue in
     domains where women now lead (college admission, graduate
     school, certain professions); no parallel programs address
     new male underrepresentation in those same domains.

     Draws from: FL9, ED1, ED2, ED3, ED4, ED7.

I30. The available shorthand for male-disadvantaging patterns is
     thinner than the patterns. Female-disadvantaging patterns
     have compact, widely-circulated terms (pay gap, mansplaining,
     glass ceiling, rape culture); comparable male-disadvantaging
     patterns (sentencing gap, custody gap, workplace-death gap,
     conscription gap, suicide gap, circumcision, divorce-outcome
     asymmetry) mostly require full clauses. The absence of compact
     terms raises the cost of referring to them.

     Draws from: LR3, FL13.

I31. Agency is named when it helps the expected conclusion and
     softened when it would hurt it. Where attributing agency helps
     a woman (she chose to leave, she chose this career), agency is
     named; where it hurts her (she chose to drink, she chose to
     stay), it is softened into circumstance. The mirror move
     applies to men: agency is named where it implicates him and
     softened where it would exonerate him.

     Draws from: AC1, AC2, AC3, AC5, AC6, SR5, TA3, TA4.

I32. The claim "women and men are equal" is used in two ways that
     contradict each other, and the switch is not flagged. It is
     used to argue that women should have equal representation in
     high-status domains, and it is used to argue that men and
     women are not similarly situated when the outcome in a given
     domain favors women. The discourse does not apply a single
     rule.

     Draws from: LR7, FL5, FL6, FL9, WE1, HM1, ED1.

I33. The direction of post-divorce financial transfer does not
     reduce to who earned more during the marriage. Women receive
     roughly 97% of alimony awards — a share larger than the share
     of marriages in which the wife was the lower earner. Cases
     where a higher-earning wife pays alimony to a lower-earning
     husband remain a small minority of the outcomes such a rule
     would produce if earning structure alone were doing the work.
     The transfer tracks sex on top of earning difference, not only
     earning difference.

     Draws from: FL11.

I34. Historical patterns are used as current-tense frames in one
     direction. Living men are routinely assigned partial
     responsibility in present-day discussions for actions of
     earlier members of their group; living women are not similarly
     assigned responsibility for actions of earlier members of
     theirs. "Patriarchy" stays in present tense even where men now
     underperform. When a current male-disadvantage statistic is
     raised, a common response invokes historical male advantage as
     if it settles the current claim; the mirror move is not
     standard and is flagged as deflection when attempted. The use
     of history is asymmetric in both directions of the move —
     what the past is responsible for, and what the past rebuts.

     Draws from: LR6, LR7, VO11, VO12, DD11.

I35. Across many domains, mainstream discourse places men in the
     position of the agent whose conduct is the target of correction
     and women in the position of the recipient of concern,
     protection, or services. The split shows up on the same topic
     and in the same genre, across public-health campaigns,
     workplace training, sex education, relationship advice, news
     framing of harms, children's emotional support, advice to
     partners in relational conflict, safety norms in public space,
     the "babysitting"-style address to fathers, the vocabulary of
     class-level self-correction, and media depictions of domestic
     competence. Each individual case has a local rationale; what is
     structural is that the local rationales point the same way
     across domains. This is not a claim about anyone's intent; it
     is a claim about the default subject position the discourse
     places each sex in.

     Draws from: AP1, AP2, AP3, AP4, AP5, EN5, EN6, LR5, MR1, MR7,
     PF5, RS8, TA1, TA2, VS4, VS5.

I36. Several large sex-asymmetric outcomes have not moved toward
     convergence over the past several decades. The workplace-
     fatality sex ratio, mothers' majority share of primary custody
     in contested cases, the sex gap in federal sentencing, the
     male-to-female suicide ratio, and the male-only draft
     registration have each held roughly fixed. Female
     representation in high-status domains has risen sharply over
     the same period. The non-convergence on the male-disadvantaging
     outcomes is consistent in direction across domains and is not a
     short-window artifact.

     Draws from: TS1, TS2, TS3, TS4, TS6, TS7.

I37. In several domains, male-disadvantaging outcomes have worsened
     in absolute terms over the same period in which female-
     disadvantaging outcomes have been targeted for correction. Men
     reporting "no close friends" rose from roughly 3% to roughly
     15% between 1990 and the early 2020s; the male suicide rate
     climbed; the share of male K-12 teachers fell; the share of
     male early childhood educators has stayed in low single digits
     with no upward trend. Over the same period, women's educational
     attainment climbed monotonically and female entry into high-
     status occupations has been actively supported by programs and
     funding. The two trajectories are running in opposite
     directions.

     Draws from: TS1, TS2, TS5, TS8.

I38. Formal doctrinal moves toward equal treatment have not produced
     parallel practical movement on the male-disadvantaging side of
     the domains those doctrines reach. Title IX has been in force
     since 1972 while the direction of educational attainment
     reversed past equal and kept going; combat roles were formally
     opened to women in 2013 while draft registration remains male-
     only; several US jurisdictions moved formal custody doctrine
     toward equal defaults while mothers' majority share of
     contested-case outcomes stayed roughly constant; anti-
     discrimination doctrine has been robust across the period in
     which the sentencing sex gap has not narrowed. Where doctrine
     has shifted toward equal treatment, practice in the male-
     disadvantaging direction has continued roughly as before.

     Draws from: TS1, TS4, TS6, TS7.

I39. Mainstream gender discourse splits its readership along
     ideological lines, and the split tracks the two evidentiary sets
     this framework catalogues. Left-coded general-audience
     publications, academic gender studies programs, and major
     philanthropic funders cover and fund work predominantly on the
     female-disadvantage set. Right-coded publications and men's-
     issues venues cover the male-disadvantage set. The two sides
     have unequal institutional weight: the left-coded side is
     reinforced by large, long-established institutions (universities,
     foundations, established advocacy organizations); the right-
     coded side has no comparable institutional reinforcement.
     Consequently, a typical reader who encounters gender-asymmetry
     material primarily through one of these reading ecosystems is
     reliably shown one subset of the evidence at a much higher
     frequency than the other.

     Draws from: RC1, RC2, RC3, RC4, RC5.
