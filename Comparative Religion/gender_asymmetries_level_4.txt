OBSERVED ASYMMETRIES IN HOW MEN AND WOMEN ARE TREATED
LAYER 4 — patterns across the Layer 3 structural claims


WRITING GOAL

Demystification. Use plain, common language and keep cognitive load low,
while staying precise enough that a specialist would agree it's
accurate. Be hard on the topic, soft on the reader: anticipate where
someone might feel defensive, but don't soften what's actually true.
Where something is uncertain, contested, or where an expert would
object, surface that directly in plain terms — don't hide it behind
jargon, hedging, emotional appeals, or common misreadings the reader
might bring in.

Drifts to avoid:
  (1) academic drift — reaching for Latinate words, nominalizations, or
      jargon when a common word works.
  (2) bland drift — hedging or both-sidesing until no real claim is
      made.
  (3) false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would
      object to.
  (4) emotional cushioning that buries the actual point.
If accessibility and precision seem to conflict in a sentence, flag
that sentence rather than silently picking one.

Self-check before releasing a version: a smart non-expert should
understand it on first read; a specialist should read it and not
wince; no sentence should hedge without saying anything. If any of
those fail, rewrite.


WHAT LAYER 4 IS

Layer 3 treats the Layer 2 inferences themselves as observations and
asks what larger structural claims they jointly support. Layer 4
takes the next step: it treats the Layer 3 structural claims as the
new observations and asks what patterns run across them.

A Layer 4 entry is a pattern across Layer 3 entries — a rule about
how the Layer 3 rules hang together. It is not a new observation,
not an inference over specific cases, and not a structural claim
about Layer 2. It sits one further step back and asks: given the
Layer 3 entries as a set, what holds across them?

Two shapes of L4 entry are admitted. The primary shape (H1–H6) names
a shared feature of the L3 set itself — direction, location,
casting, coupling, one-move switching, distribution. A secondary
shape (H7) names what happens when multiple L3 entries are applied
to a particular target and produce convergent predictions about it.
Both are legitimately higher-order than any single L3 entry; the
shapes differ, and that difference is flagged where it appears
rather than glossed.

Each Layer 4 entry lists the Layer 3 entries it draws from using
their M-numbers. If those structural claims fall or are revised, the
Layer 4 entry falls or is revised with them.

Layer 4 entries use the prefix H- (for higher-order pattern) to keep
them distinct from Layer 1 (two-letter domain codes), Layer 2 (I-),
and Layer 3 (M-).

Clusters of Layer 4 entries that appear to share a deeper rule are
Layer 5 material, not a reason to merge Layer 4 entries. Entries are
only merged when they are genuinely identical.

Throughout Layer 4: entries describe patterns visible across the
Layer 3 structural claims. They are claims about patterns, not about
anyone's intent, consciousness, or coordination.


FOUR FILTER CRITERIA

Each entry must pass all four to stay in the main list:

  (1) Both-sidedness. The pattern holds when tested against both
      directions of the asymmetry, not only one.

  (2) Smallest interpretive move. The smallest step the Layer 3
      entries together support, not a leap past them to bigger
      causal, historical, or psychological claims.

  (3) Specialist-wince test. A specialist in the relevant domain
      might disagree with the framing but would not say the claim is
      factually wrong or technically sloppy.

  (4) Falsifiability. A concrete class of evidence — including
      changes to the underlying Layer 3 entries — would force
      revision or retraction.

Entries that fail an audit move to the central staging file with the
readmission condition that would let them back in. A Layer 4
candidate may also fail because it is actually a Layer 5 claim (a
pattern across Layer 4 entries); such candidates are held for
Layer 5, also in the staging file.

LAYER 4: PATTERNS ACROSS STRUCTURAL CLAIMS
================================================================================


H1. Progress on gender equality runs in one direction.

    Look across the Layer 3 entries and a single direction emerges:
    the tools for seeing and closing gaps get used on the female-
    disadvantage side and not on the male-disadvantage side. How
    outcome gaps get read (M5), which way neutral-looking rules get
    applied (M6), which patterns have compact names (M7), who keeps
    victim or oppressor status across sub-domains (M8), which of two
    available rules gets picked in contested domains (M9), which
    claims enter discourse cheaply (M10), and which direction the
    practical trajectory has moved over several decades (M11) — all
    sort the same way.

    A gap closing in the female-disadvantage direction counts as
    progress, gets funded, and is sustained. A gap closing in the
    male-disadvantage direction does not get the same treatment — the
    reading mechanisms (M5), the rule-application direction (M6), the
    vocabulary (M7), the persistent victim/oppressor labels (M8), the
    rule selection (M9), and the speech cost (M10) all work against
    it — and over the same period several such gaps (male
    educational attainment, close-friendship counts, male suicide
    rate, share of male K–12 teachers) have held flat or widened.

    The narrow claim here is about the direction the equipment gets
    pointed in, not about anyone's intentions. The tools exist, they
    are applied, and they are applied on one side.

    Draws from: M5, M6, M7, M8, M9, M10, M11.


H2. The asymmetry lives mostly in the defaults, not in the rules on
    paper.

    Read the formal rules — antidiscrimination law, editorial style
    guides, equal-treatment doctrine, HR policy language — and most
    of them are symmetric. (A handful aren't: selective service
    registration is male-only; some programs are sex-targeted by
    design.) The larger share of the asymmetry runs not through the
    written rules but through the layer underneath them — and that
    layer has three registers that have to be kept distinct:

      - Interpretive defaults: which way institutions read ambiguous
        facts (M3), and which side of a neutral-looking rule gets
        picked up in application (M6).
      - Role-assignment defaults: how agent-vs-recipient positions
        get assigned in interactions and institutional intake (M4),
        and which of two available rules ("equal" vs. "not similarly
        situated") gets selected in a given domain (M9).
      - Trajectory defaults: where practice has actually gone while
        the rules were in force (M11).

    Each register is a different kind of "default," but they hang
    together in one direction.

    One consequence: arguments conducted at the level of formal rules
    miss the thing. A reader can agree with every line of the written
    rules and still see outcomes running consistently to one side.
    The rules mostly pass the sex-neutral test; the defaults
    underneath them don't.

    Draws from: M3, M4, M6, M9, M11.


H3. Men get cast as the one doing something; women as the one
    something is happening to.

    The same shape shows up in three distinct places. In ambiguous
    intake situations — a mutual-violence call, a distressed report —
    the default casts the man as agent of harm and the woman as the
    one harmed (M3). Across several distinct institutional behaviors
    — responsibility attribution, how the same act gets classified,
    primary-aggressor defaults, who the prevention message is
    addressed to — the same positioning holds (M4). And when outcome
    gaps run against men, they are read as things men are doing to
    themselves; when outcome gaps run against women, they are read as
    things being done to them (M5).

    This is not a claim about who actually did what in any particular
    case. It covers two registers at once — how ambiguous cases get
    read (a framing claim, via M3 and M4's discourse evidence) and
    how institutions actually act on them (a behavior claim, via M3's
    intake and primary-aggressor data). Both registers move the same
    direction.

    Draws from: M3, M4, M5.


H4. The missing names and the cost of saying something are two sides
    of one asymmetry, not two independent ones.

    M7 says compact, mainstream-usable names exist for one side's
    patterns and not the other's. M10 says a claim of male
    disadvantage costs more to raise than an equally well-evidenced
    claim of female disadvantage. M1 says the speech asymmetry runs
    harder and more one-directionally than the outcome asymmetry
    does.

    These three are tied together, not three independent facts. The
    vocabulary gap and the speech-cost gap are coupled: a pattern
    without a short name takes a clause to refer to, doesn't
    circulate casually, and forces the speaker to construct the
    category each time — which raises the cost of raising it.

    The vocabulary gap is part of what produces the cost gap, not a
    separate asymmetry alongside it. (The reverse direction — cost
    produces vocabulary — is also plausible: patterns that are
    expensive to name don't get short names coined for them. Either
    way, they are not two independent facts about speech.)

    Draws from: M1, M7, M10.


H5. Whether men and women get judged as a class or as individuals
    flips depending on which one the claim is about.

    Several distinct moves, each looking even-handed on its face,
    together produce a class/individual switch. When a claim runs
    against men, the class-level machinery engages: the pattern is
    named, individual counter-cases don't rebut it, past behavior is
    admissible, agency is attributed (M6). When a claim runs against
    women, the individual-level machinery engages on the same topic:
    the counter-case lands, the class-level claim becomes an
    illegitimate generalization, circumstance softens agency.

    This switch is narrower than H1 or H2. H1 covers the direction
    the whole toolkit gets pointed in; H2 covers where the asymmetry
    sits (defaults vs. rules). H5 is about one specific move inside
    that toolkit: the same topic can be framed at class level (a
    pattern about men / about women) or at individual level (this
    man / this woman), and the choice between the two framings
    tracks the direction of the claim. The rules are not worded
    differently by sex; which side of the rule — class or individual
    — gets picked up is.

    Draws from: M6.


H6. Both halves of the evidence are real, and each half is mostly
    read in a different kind of venue.

    M2 says the Layer 2 inferences cluster into two partially non-
    overlapping sets, each factually supported by its own Layer 1
    evidence. M12 says those same two sets map onto a readership
    split: the female-disadvantage set is covered overwhelmingly in
    left-coded mainstream publications and reinforced by the large
    institutions adjacent to them (academia, major foundations,
    established advocacy organizations); the male-disadvantage set
    is covered overwhelmingly in right-coded publications and
    adjacent men's-issues venues, which have no comparable large
    institutional reinforcement.

    What's new at L4: this explains why the asymmetry stays
    invisible to each side without needing to say either side is
    wrong about what it cites. A reader inside the left-coded
    ecosystem is reliably shown one half of the catalogue. A reader
    inside the right-coded ecosystem is reliably shown the other
    half. The disagreement between "gender asymmetries mostly run
    against women" and "gender asymmetries mostly run against men"
    is not two people looking at the same evidence and reaching
    different conclusions. It is two people looking at two different
    halves of the evidence, in venues sorted by ideology.

    Draws from: M2, M12.


H7. Multiple Layer 3 entries converge on predicting this
    framework's reception — the framework sits inside the pattern it
    describes.

    Four distinct L3 entries each predict something about how a
    document like this gets received. M10 predicts the cost
    differential overall: claims of male disadvantage enter
    mainstream discussion at higher cost than claims of female
    disadvantage, independent of evidence quality. M6 predicts that
    neutral-looking evaluation rules (engage the content before
    questioning motives; count counter-cases symmetrically) get
    applied by direction, which would show up here as motive-
    questioning being reached for faster than for an equivalent
    female-disadvantage analysis. M7 predicts extra cost from
    constructed vocabulary: short names don't exist in mainstream
    usage for several of the patterns this framework describes, so
    the framework has to build the category each time, and that
    construction itself raises the speaker's cost. M1 predicts that
    the speech-side asymmetry runs harder than the outcome-side
    asymmetry, so a document describing both should take more flak
    on the speech side than on the outcome side.

    Draws from: M1, M6, M7, M10.
