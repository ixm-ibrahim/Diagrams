================================================================================
OBSERVED ASYMMETRIES IN HOW MEN AND WOMEN ARE TREATED
LAYER 1 — raw observations


WRITING GOAL

Demystification. Use plain, common language and keep cognitive load low,
while staying precise enough that a specialist would agree it's
accurate. Be hard on the topic, soft on the reader: anticipate where
someone might feel defensive, but don't soften what's actually true.
Where something is uncertain, contested, or where an expert would
object, surface that directly in plain terms — don't hide it behind
jargon, hedging, emotional appeals, or common misreadings the reader
might bring in.

Drifts to avoid:
  (1) academic drift — reaching for Latinate words, nominalizations, or
      jargon when a common word works.
  (2) bland drift — hedging or both-sidesing until no real claim is
      made.
  (3) false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would
      object to.
  (4) emotional cushioning that buries the actual point.
If accessibility and precision seem to conflict in a sentence, flag
that sentence rather than silently picking one.

Self-check before releasing a version: a smart non-expert should
understand it on first read; a specialist should read it and not
wince; no sentence should hedge without saying anything. If any of
those fail, rewrite.


STRUCTURE

Sections are ordered from less gender-specific first (general discourse
patterns, meta-level observations about what can and can't be said)
toward more gender-specific later (concrete stats and policy in
specific domains of life).

IDs are preserved across versions as stable anchors for later layers.
New sections use new two-letter prefixes (DP, VO, EN). Existing
observations keep their prior IDs even where sections have been
reordered.

DISCOURSE DYNAMICS (DD)
How argument, evidence, and disagreement behave on loaded topics. Not
gender-specific on its face, but shapes what happens when gender
asymmetries are discussed.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DD1. When a topic is emotionally charged, speaking about it in a calm,
     analytical way is often read as coldness, dismissal, or hostility.
     The tone itself becomes the basis for the judgment — "you sound
     too detached about this" — separate from whether what the speaker
     said was accurate.

DD2. The intensity of a feeling is commonly treated as evidence that
     the underlying position is correct. "You can see how much this
     hurts them" functions as an argument, rather than as a reason to
     then ask whether the claim producing the hurt is in fact true.

DD3. Pointing out that an argument is badly structured (e.g., "this
     mixes two separate issues" or "this premise doesn't support that
     conclusion") is commonly read as disagreeing with the argument's
     conclusion — as if the only reason to critique an argument is to
     reject what it's defending.

DD4. A single factual error inside a contrarian argument is commonly
     used to discredit the entire position. An equivalent factual error
     made from the mainstream side is typically corrected and moved
     past without the speaker's overall position being treated as
     discredited.

DD5. When someone raises an uncomfortable observation, a common first
     response is to question the speaker's motives ("why are you
     bringing this up," "who's paying you to say this," "what are you
     really after") rather than to evaluate the observation itself.
     Someone raising an observation that fits the dominant view is not
     usually asked about their motives first.

DD6. Taboos in this area are mainly enforced through social punishment
     — mockery, being labeled, lost friends, professional cost,
     exclusion from social circles — rather than by someone producing
     a counter-argument against the claim.

DD7. Speech on these topics is increasingly described as itself a form
     of harm or violence. Saying the thing is treated as doing
     something to someone, rather than as a claim to be agreed with or
     contested.

DD8. If a listener feels uncomfortable hearing an idea, that discomfort
     is increasingly treated as evidence the idea itself is harmful,
     rather than as a signal that the listener is encountering
     something that challenges a belief or assumption they hold.

DD9. "Psychological safety" — originally meaning freedom from being
     personally attacked or humiliated — has come to also mean freedom
     from being exposed to views one disagrees with.

DD10. When women express distress in political debates or public
      forums (tears, visible upset, emotional appeals), it is typically
      received as meaningful testimony that adds weight to their
      position. When men express the same distress in the same
      settings, it is typically received as instability, overreaction,
      or evidence they shouldn't be in the conversation. The asymmetry
      is visible in contemporaneous media framing of equivalent
      displays across sex.

DD11. When a current claim of male disadvantage is raised, a common
      response is to introduce historical male advantage ("men had
      power for centuries") as if it rebuts the current statistic. The
      current claim and the historical fact concern different time
      periods and different measurements. The mirror move — responding
      to a current female-disadvantage claim by introducing historical
      female-advantage domains (exemption from conscription, maternal-
      preference custody, dower rights) — is not a standard rhetorical
      move and is typically flagged as deflection when attempted.

DEFENSIVE PATTERNS (DP)
Moves a person tends to make when an uncomfortable claim lands, instead
of engaging the claim on its merits. Not specific to gender, but very
active on gender topics. These moves are not always invalid — sometimes
the pushback is correct — but each one is also available as a way to
avoid engaging, so the move shows up whether or not the original claim
was right.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DP1. When someone offers an analogy ("A is like B in this one respect"),
     a common response is to list differences between A and B that are
     true but don't bear on the respect being claimed. Example: "routine
     infant circumcision is, in the relevant sense, like female genital
     cutting — both involve cutting a child's genitals without the
     child's consent." Common response: "no — FGM causes much more
     harm, has no medical rationale, and is done to suppress women's
     sexuality." All three differences are real; none of them address
     the specific point the analogy was making (non-consensual cutting
     of a child's genitals). The real differences are used to block
     engagement with the narrow point.

DP2. When a specific asymmetry is raised (e.g., "men are 92% of
     workplace fatalities"), a common move is to introduce a different
     asymmetry that favors the other side ("but women earn less"). The
     second may be true, but it doesn't address the first, and the
     effect of introducing it is to prevent the first from being
     examined at all. Commonly called "whataboutism" when done by the
     other side.

DP3. When a group-level statistic is raised, a common move is to
     produce an individual case that doesn't fit and treat that case
     as if it refutes the statistic. "Men are 70% of homicide victims"
     met with "my aunt was killed by her husband." The individual case
     is real; the statistic is still real; and the conversation moves
     on as if the statistic has been answered.

DP4. Tying a claim to a disliked group is treated as itself a reason
     to dismiss the claim. "That sounds like something a men's rights
     activist would say" or "that's an incel talking point" functions
     as a full refutation in many settings without the claim itself
     being engaged.

DP5. When a claim is made about a group someone identifies with, a
     common move is to reframe the claim as a personal attack. "You're
     describing a pattern in which some men behave badly" becomes "so
     you're saying I'm the bad guy?" The conversation shifts from the
     pattern to the hurt, and the pattern stops being discussed.

DP6. Attacking the source of evidence is routinely treated as if it
     rebuts the evidence. "That study was funded by X" or "that
     researcher has said problematic things elsewhere" is offered as a
     reason not to engage with what the study actually found.

DP7. A common response to an uncomfortable observation is to demand a
     ready policy solution before the observation can be acknowledged.
     "What do you propose we do about it?" shifts the question from
     "is this true?" to "is there a fix?" If the speaker doesn't have
     a fix at hand, the observation is often treated as thereby
     discredited.

DP8. In group conversation, the question of whether a claim is true is
     sometimes displaced by concern for the emotional impact of
     raising it. "This is upsetting people in the room" takes priority
     over "is what you said accurate?" — so the truth status of the
     claim is never reached.

DP9. Individual counter-cases to group-level statistics receive
     asymmetric treatment. "Not all men" offered in response to a
     statistic about male behavior is typically treated as deflection.
     The structurally identical move in the other direction — "my
     wife out-earns me" in response to the pay-gap figure, "I've known
     women who abused their husbands" in response to domestic-
     violence-against-women statistics — is rarely produced in
     mainstream discourse, and when produced is typically treated as
     deflection before the statistic is discussed. The same rhetorical
     move lands differently by direction.

WHAT CAN BE SAID ABOUT BIOLOGICAL DIFFERENCE (BD)
Less about the biology itself than about what speech is permitted
around it.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BD1. Research consistently finds differences, on average, between men
     and women in things like occupational interests, risk-taking
     behavior, and some cognitive patterns. ("On average" means
     on-the-whole across a population, not for every individual.)

BD2. People who publicly discuss biological differences as a possible
     contributor to outcomes (e.g., why more men work in engineering
     and more women work in nursing) have repeatedly lost jobs,
     speaking invitations, or professional standing. People who
     publicly discuss discrimination as the explanation for the same
     outcomes do not face equivalent professional risk.

BD3. When men and women end up doing different things on average
     (fields of study, occupations, life choices), the default
     institutional explanation is discrimination. Explaining any part
     of the pattern through different average preferences is treated
     as politically suspect and requires heavier justification.

BD4. In countries with more formal gender equality (e.g., the Nordic
     countries), men and women actually choose more different
     occupations and show larger differences in stated preferences,
     not smaller. This finding — called the "gender equality paradox"
     — receives limited mainstream coverage.

BD5. Medical research and clinical practice continue to rely on
     biological sex as a category for estimating disease risk,
     calculating medication dosages, and setting reference ranges for
     physiological measurements (e.g., hematocrit, hormone levels).

BD6. Sports governing bodies (the IOC, World Athletics, World Rugby,
     and others) have issued and revised policies on trans-athlete
     eligibility that weigh physiological effects of biological sex
     against inclusion principles. Different bodies have reached
     different conclusions, and the policies continue to change.

BD7. Public policy disputes about sex-segregated spaces (prisons,
     domestic violence shelters, sports categories, hospital wards)
     involve explicit disagreement about whether the operative
     category is biological sex or self-declared gender identity.

BD8. The same institutions sometimes use biological sex as the
     operative medical category — e.g., calling screening programs
     "prostate cancer screening" or "cervical cancer screening" — while
     in adjacent reproductive-health communications replacing the
     sex-based term with "people who menstruate" or "people who can
     become pregnant." The operativeness of sex is affirmed and denied
     in overlapping communications from the same source.

BD9. Multiple independent research programs (Schmitt and colleagues on
     personality, Lippa on interests, Falk and Hermle on preferences,
     Stoet and Geary on STEM choices) have found that sex differences
     in personality traits, stated partner preferences, and
     occupational preferences measure as larger — not smaller — in
     more egalitarian societies.

RECEPTION OF OBSERVATIONS LIKE THESE (PR)
How young men raising the points in this framework — or adjacent points
— tend to be received, and what happens next.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PR1. Young men who voice observations like the ones in this document
     in mainstream settings (classroom, workplace, online among mixed
     audiences) commonly receive responses that pathologize them —
     "that's incel talk," "you sound radicalized," "you have issues
     with women." Young women voicing grievances framed as female
     grievances in the same settings typically receive support and
     engagement with the grievance itself.

PR2. Public figures who bring up observations like these — even
     alongside content that is low-quality, conspiratorial, or
     harmful — have built very large male audiences. The co-presence
     of bad content does not prevent the audience from forming around
     the naming itself.

PR3. When a figure draws a large male audience, the mainstream
     response tends to focus on condemning the figure rather than on
     asking why the audience formed. Parallel phenomena drawing large
     female audiences are more often engaged on the level of what
     drew the audience (what unmet need the content is speaking to).

PR4. When male-specific struggles are raised in progressive spaces
     (e.g., boys falling behind in school, the male suicide rate,
     male homelessness), the conversation is frequently redirected to
     female-specific struggles ("but women still face…"). The reverse
     redirection — someone raising a female-specific struggle and
     having the conversation redirected to a male-specific one — is
     rare.

PR5. Young men commonly report that when they openly express
     difficulty (struggling at school, feeling isolated, failing to
     find a partner), the response they receive is suspicion or
     lecturing rather than support. Young women report that
     equivalent expressions of difficulty are met with support and
     concern.

READERSHIP AND COVERAGE (RC)
Where gender-asymmetry topics get covered, by whom, and for which
readers. How the two evidentiary sets this framework catalogues are
distributed across reading locations.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RC1. Mainstream left-coded general-audience publications (New York
     Times, Washington Post, The Atlantic, Guardian, NPR) mostly
     cover gender-asymmetry topics from the set that documents
     female disadvantage and male-misbehavior patterns — pay gap,
     MeToo, reproductive rights, leadership representation,
     harassment. Coverage of the other set — sentencing gap, custody
     outcomes, male-only draft registration, workplace-death sex
     ratio, male suicide rate, male educational underperformance —
     is rare in the same venues. When it does appear, it is more
     often framed as a problem because of its effect on women
     ("men falling behind" treated as a problem for women looking
     for marriageable partners) than as a problem about men
     standing on its own.

RC2. Right-coded publications (Wall Street Journal op-ed, New York
     Post, The Free Press, Quillette, National Review) and
     explicitly men's-issues venues (fathers-rights organizations,
     large parts of online men's discourse, books like Warren
     Farrell's "The Boy Crisis" or Richard Reeves's "Of Boys and
     Men") mostly cover gender-asymmetry topics from the set that
     documents male disadvantage. Coverage of the female-
     disadvantage set is thin in these venues and is more often
     contested than reported.

RC3. Academic gender studies programs focus their curricula on the
     female-disadvantage set. Courses engaging the male-disadvantage
     set are rare inside gender studies departments, though related
     material does appear elsewhere in the university — criminology
     (sentencing, incarceration), family law (custody, alimony),
     labor economics (workplace deaths, occupational segregation),
     and education research (boys falling behind in school). The
     topics exist in academia; they just tend not to be housed in
     the department with "gender" in its title.

RC4. Philanthropic and institutional funding tagged "gender" in the
     US — major private foundations (Ford, Gates, MacArthur,
     Rockefeller), federal programs (EEOC, Title IX enforcement,
     Women's Bureau, the White House Gender Policy Council), and
     corporate DEI spending — flows overwhelmingly to programs
     addressing the female-disadvantage set. Funding tagged for the
     male-disadvantage set (men's mental health, boys' school
     underperformance, custody equality, fatherless-household
     interventions) is a small fraction — orders of magnitude
     smaller at the foundation level.

RC5. Institutionally embedded, well-funded feminist advocacy
     organizations (NOW, Planned Parenthood, UN Women, Time's Up,
     the Women's Law Project) address the female-disadvantage set.
     Men's-issues advocacy (National Coalition For Men, men's-health
     nonprofits, fathers-rights coalitions) is smaller, less well
     funded, and carries lower cultural standing — even within its
     own subject-matter expertise.

VICTIM AND OPPRESSOR LABELS (VO)
How the words "victim" and "oppressor" (and their softer stand-ins) get
attached to people, and how the attachment is used socially and
politically.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

VO1. "Victim" is applied asymmetrically for comparable harms. A man
     hit by a female partner is less likely to be described as "a
     victim of domestic abuse" in casual conversation or mainstream
     media than a woman hit by a male partner in equivalent
     circumstances. Same physical event, different likelihood of the
     victim label.

VO2. "Oppressor" and its softer forms ("privileged," "part of the
     problem," "the dominant class") are commonly assigned by group
     membership, not by individual conduct. A man who has personally
     harmed no one is still describable as part of the oppressor
     class on the basis of being a man. A woman who has personally
     behaved cruelly to multiple people is not routinely described as
     an oppressor on the basis of her conduct — the oppressor label
     is almost never applied to her as an individual.

VO3. Standing to speak on a topic is often indexed to victim status
     on that topic. A woman's view on sexism is treated as carrying
     greater evidential weight than a man's — she is seen as the
     person with direct experience. The same logic is not applied in
     the domains where men do worse (custody outcomes, conscription,
     workplace deaths, suicide rate): a man's view on those domains
     is not treated as carrying extra evidential weight, because men
     are not widely seen as a victim class there.

VO4. Claiming victim status publicly is a socially and politically
     valuable position. It attracts sympathy, grants evidential weight
     in disputes, unlocks institutional resources and protections,
     and gives the speaker rhetorical high ground. Having oppressor
     status applied to you is the inverse: loss of standing to speak,
     reversed burden of proof, pressure to apologize. The incentive
     gradient pushes speakers to frame situations in victim terms when
     they plausibly can, and to deflect oppressor framing when it's
     applied to them.

VO5. Claims of harm made by members of a designated victim group are
     received with a default of credibility ("believe women"). Claims
     of harm made by members of a designated oppressor group are
     received with a default of skepticism or reframing ("why is he
     bringing this up," "what's he really after," "this is a cover
     for resenting women").

VO6. Once a group holds the "victim" designation on a given axis, the
     designation tends to persist in sub-domains where the relevant
     harm has reversed or resolved. Women retain the victim
     designation in discussions of higher education in countries
     where women now earn a clear majority of degrees; the
     designation does not update to track the current outcome.

VO7. When a member of a designated oppressor group is harmed by a
     member of a designated victim group, the harm is more often
     described in individual or situational terms (a one-off, a
     dispute, a bad actor). When the direction reverses — a member of
     the victim group harmed by a member of the oppressor group — the
     harm is more often described in class terms (a pattern, a hate
     crime, evidence of structural violence). The same physical event
     yields different framings depending on who did it to whom.

VO8. "Victim-blaming" — asking what the person on the receiving end
     did to contribute to their own harm — is a named rhetorical
     offense when applied to members of designated victim groups. The
     same move applied to a male victim (suggesting a man hit by his
     female partner "must have provoked her") is not commonly called
     victim-blaming, and the term is rarely used for it.

VO9. In US jurisdictions with "primary aggressor" policies for
     domestic-violence calls — police training that instructs officers
     to identify the main aggressor in a mutual-violence incident
     instead of arresting both parties — male partners are identified
     as primary aggressor at substantially higher rates than
     incident-level facts (who hit first, who was injured more) would
     predict by themselves. This is contested in the research
     literature: some argue the disparity reflects real differences
     in severity and intent, others argue it reflects policies being
     applied asymmetrically by sex. Both readings are defended by
     domestic-violence researchers.

VO10. Political movements routinely claim victim status for their
      constituency as a rhetorical move — not a description of a
      specific harm done to specific people by specific others, but a
      class-level positioning that places the movement on the morally
      unassailable side of a dispute and, by implication, places its
      opposition in the oppressor role. The class-level frame
      substitutes for identifying specific conduct. When done by the
      other side, the same move is commonly flagged as exaggeration
      or grievance politics; when done by one's own side, it is
      commonly treated as simply describing reality.

VO11. "Oppressor" is applied to living people on the basis of actions
      taken by earlier members of their group, without requiring any
      specific conduct from the living person being designated. The
      mirror-image move — designating a living person "a victim" on
      the basis of harms done to earlier members of their group —
      also happens, but the inherited oppressor status is harder to
      escape: disclaiming, demonstrated conduct, and individual life
      history typically do not lift the label.

VO12. Inherited collective responsibility is applied asymmetrically
      by sex. Living men are routinely assigned partial responsibility
      in current-day discussions for actions of earlier members of
      their group (historical disenfranchisement of women, historical
      patriarchal law, wars started by male rulers). Living women are
      not similarly assigned responsibility for actions of earlier
      members of their group (historical support for restrictive
      gender roles, participation in witch-hunt accusations, lobbying
      for maternal-preference custody doctrines). The inherited-
      responsibility move runs in one direction.

LANGUAGE AND RHETORIC (LR)
Which phrases about men and women are mainstream-acceptable, and which
parallel phrases are not.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LR1. Expressing frustration with men as a group is socially acceptable
     in mainstream settings: "ugh, men," "men are exhausting," "men
     are the worst" pass without sanction. The equivalent statements
     about women — "ugh, women," "women are the worst" — are not
     acceptable and are typically flagged as misogyny.

LR2. "Men are trash" and "kill all men" circulate as acceptable
     humor in mainstream online and cultural spaces. Structurally
     identical phrases — "women are trash," "kill all women" — do not
     circulate as humor and are treated as threats or hate speech.

LR3. "Mansplaining" (a man condescendingly explaining something to a
     woman), "manspreading" (sitting with legs open), "male
     fragility," and "toxic masculinity" are mainstream words with
     wide circulation. No parallel shorthand naming a typical female
     behavior (e.g., for a woman interrupting a man she assumes knows
     less, or for a common pattern of female social aggression) has
     comparable currency in everyday speech.

LR4. "Believe women" is offered as a principle applied to
     accusations made by women. There is no parallel principle
     "believe men" applied to accusations made by men.

LR5. "Men need to do better" works as mainstream collective framing.
     "Women need to do better," used the same way about women as a
     group, does not.

LR6. When an individual man describes suffering, a common response
     is to point out that men as a group still benefit structurally
     ("but you have privilege"). When an individual woman describes
     suffering, she is not typically answered by pointing out domains
     where women as a group benefit (e.g., longer life expectancy,
     lower homelessness rates, preferential custody outcomes).

LR7. "Patriarchy" — meaning a system in which men hold dominant power
     — is used as a current-tense description of society even in
     specific domains where men are now underrepresented or
     underperforming (higher education, early-career earnings in
     certain cities, school achievement). There is no parallel
     structural term ("matriarchy" or similar) applied to those
     domains.

LR8. Calling a claim "what women think" is commonly contested as an
     illegitimate generalization about women. Calling a claim "what
     men think" is rarely contested on the same grounds.

LR9. Women writing, speaking, or making content about the inner lives
     of men — what men want, what men fear, why men are the way they
     are — is a mainstream, uncontested genre. Men writing, speaking,
     or making content about the inner lives of women is commonly
     contested as presumptuous, as "mansplaining women to women," or
     as an illegitimate claim to speak for a group he isn't in. The
     same cross-sex authorial move is licensed in one direction and
     challenged in the other.

LR10. Male-default generic terms (chairman, mankind, generic "he/him")
      have been flagged as exclusionary in institutional style guides
      and widely replaced (chairperson, humanity, they/them, singular
      they). Female-coded generic terms at comparable frequency have
      not been flagged or replaced. In current writing, generic
      "she/her" is commonly treated as progressive; generic "he/him"
      in the same contexts is treated as sexist.

ATTRIBUTION AND CHARACTER FRAMING (AC)
How the same behavior is described differently depending on who's doing
it — which determines the moral frame before any analysis begins.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AC1. When a woman behaves difficultly — snapping, being controlling,
     losing her temper, being cruel — it's commonly explained by what's
     happening to her: stress, hormones, being overwhelmed, past
     trauma, being provoked. When a man behaves the same way, it's
     commonly explained by who he is: a controlling personality,
     toxic traits, a character defect.

AC2. In therapy talk and self-help writing, female anger tends to be
     presented as a signal of unmet needs that deserves exploration
     ("what is your anger trying to tell you"). Male anger tends to be
     presented as a pathology requiring management ("anger issues,"
     "anger management").

AC3. When a woman visibly breaks down — cries, shouts, lashes out —
     the surrounding discourse commonly describes it as "having a
     bad moment," "a hard day," "being at the end of her rope." When
     a man visibly breaks down in the same way, it is commonly
     described as "showing who he really is" or "a red flag."

AC4. The everyday vocabulary for women's ongoing difficulty is wide
     and sympathetic: burnout, overwhelm, mental load, depletion,
     postpartum, perimenopause, hormonal cycle. The everyday
     vocabulary for men's ongoing difficulty is narrower and more
     often carries a negative tone: midlife crisis, anger issues,
     emotional unavailability, avoidance, toxic.

AC5. Women behaving difficultly are commonly described as "going
     through something." Men behaving difficultly are less often
     granted that framing and are more often described as "being
     difficult" — the "something happening to her" vs. "something
     true about him" distinction.

AC6. Female hostility toward an intimate partner is commonly framed
     in advice columns and media as something the other partner
     should try to understand, accommodate, or work through. Male
     hostility toward an intimate partner is commonly framed as
     something the other partner should exit or report.

EMOTIONAL NEEDS (EN)
How each sex's emotional needs are named, treated, and used — both in
private relationships and in public claims.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EN1. "Unmet emotional needs" in relationship discourse is almost
     exclusively applied to women. When a woman reports her emotional
     needs aren't being met, the framing treats the unmet need as a
     real condition that should be addressed. When a man reports the
     same — his emotional needs aren't being met by his partner —
     the framing more often treats him as needy, entitled, or as
     someone who should work on himself.

EN2. The assumption that men have simpler or fewer emotional needs
     ("men just want sex and food") circulates widely in pop
     psychology and mainstream advice. It both underestimates what
     men actually report wanting — connection, being seen, being
     respected, physical affection — and makes the vocabulary of
     "unmet needs" less available to them when those needs go unmet.

EN3. The vocabulary for naming emotional needs is not equally usable
     across sex. A woman saying "I need more emotional intimacy" or
     "I need to feel seen" is typically taken at face value. A man
     using the same phrasing is often read as weak, needy, or
     effeminate. A man naming the same underlying need in a more
     traditionally-masculine register ("I need to feel respected,"
     "I need physical affection from my partner") is often read as
     making demands. The register he can use to be heard without
     penalty is narrower.

EN4. Need-claims function politically as well as personally. "I need
     to feel safe" — said by a woman, or by a member of a designated
     victim group — has become a load-bearing basis for policy
     changes, speaker cancellations, and institutional concessions;
     the speaker's felt-safety is treated as sufficient justification
     for action. The same need-claim from men (or from designated
     non-victim groups) does not carry the same load-bearing weight
     and is more often treated as unreasonable or as a demand for
     special treatment.

EN5. Men's emotional struggles are commonly narrated about them in
     the third person by outside observers (media, therapy discourse,
     relationship columns) before men themselves speak to the
     struggles, and the third-person narration often carries a
     critical tone (emotionally stunted, avoidant, fragile, checked
     out, toxic). Women's emotional struggles are more often narrated
     in the first person by women in mainstream media using
     vocabulary women supply themselves, and that first-person
     narration is more often received sympathetically.

EN6. In children, girls' emotional needs are more likely to be
     treated as requiring adult attention and response (naming the
     feeling, talking it through, validating it). Boys' emotional
     needs are more likely to be treated as requiring self-regulation,
     toughening up, or a redirect to activity. The pattern is
     observable in parenting advice, teacher response patterns, and
     popular depictions of child-rearing.

EN7. "Emotional unavailability" — the charge that a partner is closed
     off, not offering emotional presence — is a mainstream complaint
     leveled at men. The equivalent complaint about women — that a
     woman is unavailable for the emotional content her male partner
     wants (presence, attention, sexual desire, affirmation) — has no
     widely-used name, and is more often framed as him having an
     unreasonable demand than as her being emotionally absent.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AGENT/PATIENT POSITIONING (AP)
Who mainstream discourse addresses as the agent whose behavior is the
target of the message, and who it addresses as the recipient of
concern, protection, or services. Observable at the level of campaign
materials, training scripts, curricula, news framing, and everyday
advice genres.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AP1. Public-health campaigns on sexual violence and intimate-partner
     harm address men and women in different subject positions on the
     same topic. Men are addressed as the agent whose behavior the
     campaign targets ("It's On Us," "HeForShe," "ask for consent,"
     "call in your bros"). Women are addressed as the recipient of
     protection and services ("know your rights," "call this hotline,"
     "here's how to report"). The topic is shared; the subject
     position differs by sex.

AP2. Workplace sexual-harassment training, despite policy language
     written to be sex-neutral, typically frames scenarios and
     behavioral asks with men as the default potential harasser (how
     to examine your conduct, what not to do) and women as the
     default potential victim (how to report, what your rights are).
     The training's subject position tracks sex.

AP3. Sex education addresses boys about their conduct (consent,
     boundaries, respect, managing urges) and girls about their
     safety and self-advocacy (recognizing coercion, saying no,
     reporting). Where the formal curriculum is the same across
     sexes, the emphasis, examples, and role-play casting differ.

AP4. Mainstream advice content addressed to women about relationships
     and inner life speaks to them about what is happening to them —
     what you're feeling, what you need, what your partner is doing,
     what to expect. The same genre addressed to men speaks to them
     about what is wrong with them — what to change, what to work on,
     what to stop doing, what you're getting wrong. Genre is the
     same; the addressed position — someone to whom something is
     happening versus someone doing something wrong — splits by sex.

AP5. In media coverage of a class of harm (assault, street
     harassment, intimate-partner violence), the prevention frame
     defaults to addressing men (what men should stop) and the
     response frame defaults to addressing women (how to handle it
     when it happens). Prevention-addresses-men and response-
     addresses-women is observable across major campaigns, news
     features, and advisory content.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TOLERANCE AND ACCOMMODATION (TA)
What behaviors each partner is expected to put up with, de-escalate
around, or leave over.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

TA1. In relationships, men are routinely expected to tolerate a
     female partner's emotional ups and downs — stay calm, de-escalate,
     work around them. When a woman is expected to do the same for a
     male partner's emotional ups and downs, it is commonly framed as
     her enabling him, staying too long, or failing to leave.

TA2. Advice aimed at men tells them to be patient, regulate their
     emotions, and do emotional labor on behalf of their partner.
     Advice aimed at women tells them to set boundaries, advocate for
     themselves, and leave relationships that don't meet their needs.

TA3. "Walking on eggshells" is applied asymmetrically. A woman
     walking on eggshells around a man is framed as a crisis
     requiring her to get out. A man walking on eggshells around a
     woman is framed as normal relational maintenance — what a good
     partner does.

TA4. A woman raising her voice at a male partner is commonly coded
     as acceptable venting or justified anger. A man raising his
     voice at a female partner is commonly coded as verbal abuse,
     even at comparable volume and content.

TA5. When a woman gives the silent treatment, stonewalls, or
     withdraws in a relationship, it's commonly framed as
     self-protection. When a man does the same thing, it's commonly
     framed as "weaponized silence" or emotional withholding.

TA6. Women complaining about their male partners to friends — or
     consuming media content that does this on their behalf — is a
     normal, mainstream form of sociality. Men doing the same thing
     about their female partners, in the same tone, is commonly read
     as misogynistic, bitter, or a warning sign about the man.

TA7. When a woman is described as "difficult," the implicit
     expectation is that the people around her should work to
     understand and support her. When a man is described as
     "difficult," the implicit expectation is that the people around
     him should distance themselves.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
MEDIA AND REPRESENTATION (MR)
How men and women are depicted on screen and in commercial imagery.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MR1. Fathers in commercials and sitcoms are disproportionately shown
     as incompetent at domestic tasks (cooking, laundry, childcare)
     and needing a competent mother to step in and fix things.
     Mothers in the same media are not routinely shown as incompetent
     at those tasks.

MR2. Villains in children's media (Disney films, animated series,
     major children's franchises) are disproportionately male. Female
     villains occur but are notably rarer.

MR3. In modern action films, a small woman physically beating up
     larger men is shown as normal and requires no in-story
     explanation for how she can do it. A man physically overpowering
     a woman of similar or smaller size, when it appears on screen,
     nearly always marks that man as the villain.

MR4. "Girl power" is a mainstream slogan directly marketed to
     children on clothing, toys, and school supplies. "Boy power" is
     absent from mainstream child-directed branding.

MR5. In mixed male-female ensemble casts, the female character is
     frequently written as the smartest, most competent, or most
     morally correct member of the group. A mixed cast in which a
     man holds that role is now rare, and when it occurs, the story
     often sets him up to be humbled or exposed.

MR6. Male nudity in mainstream film is typically used for comedy
     (the embarrassed naked guy running through a scene). Female
     nudity is typically used for seduction or vulnerability.

MR7. Commercials showing men doing childcare or housework often
     show them fumbling it — wrong ingredient, diaper on wrong,
     child running riot. Commercials showing women doing the same
     tasks do not routinely show them fumbling.

MR8. A woman hitting or slapping a man in film and television is
     routinely played for laughs or framed as her righteous
     response. A man hitting or slapping a woman is consistently
     framed as assault and presented with serious weight.

MR9. Female characters who behave badly on screen — cruel,
     manipulative, explosive — are usually given surrounding context
     that explains the behavior: a trauma, a provocation, a
     situation that produced it. Male characters who behave the same
     way are usually presented as simply being that kind of person,
     with no comparable contextualizing.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RELIGION, TRADITION, AND MEANING (RT)
What scripts each sex is offered for what it means to live a good life
as a man or a woman.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RT1. Traditional religious frameworks that gave men defined roles
     (provider, protector, spiritual head of the household) have been
     broadly critiqued and largely dismantled in mainstream secular
     culture.

RT2. "Positive femininity" has many mainstream articulations (the
     empowered woman, the nurturing woman, the boss, the wise elder,
     the girl's-girl). "Positive masculinity," when it appears in
     mainstream discourse, is most often defined negatively — as the
     absence of toxic traits ("not toxic," "not fragile") rather than
     as a positive picture of what a good man is.

RT3. Rites of passage that marked the transition from boy to man
     (bar mitzvah, confirmation, tribal initiation, formal
     apprenticeship) have largely disappeared from mainstream secular
     Western culture. There is no broadly agreed-on secular
     replacement.

RT4. Male participation in organized religion has declined faster
     than female participation across most Christian denominations,
     producing congregations that are now majority-female by wide
     margins.

RT5. Secular institutions have produced multiple mainstream
     affirmative visions of what a female life can be organized
     around: career-plus-motherhood, "having it all," the empowered
     professional, the autonomous chooser. They have not produced a
     matching affirmative vision of what a male life can be
     organized around to replace the traditional scripts that were
     dismantled.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE PREDATOR FRAMING (PF)
The default suspicion attached to men in contexts involving children,
women, or vulnerability.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PF1. Men alone with children in public (a father at a playground
     with his daughter, a man walking a niece home) are routinely
     treated with suspicion regardless of the actual relationship.
     Women alone with children in the same settings are not treated
     with equivalent suspicion.

PF2. Men working in teaching, childcare, pediatric medicine, and
     similar roles face more background scrutiny and more policies
     designed around the possibility they might harm a child than
     women in the same roles face.

PF3. Some airlines have had explicit policies (or informal practices)
     reassigning unaccompanied minors away from seats next to adult
     men. No equivalent policy applies to adult women.

PF4. "All men are potential rapists" circulates as an acceptable
     statement framed as caution. The same kind of statement about
     women — e.g., "all women are potential child abusers" — is
     rejected as bigotry.

PF5. Men crossing the street at night to avoid alarming a solo
     woman is promoted as considerate behavior a decent man should
     do. There is no reciprocal expectation on women to change their
     path to avoid alarming solo men.

PF6. When a man reports being sexually coerced or assaulted by a
     woman, he is frequently disbelieved, mocked, or told he should
     have enjoyed it. When a woman reports being sexually coerced or
     assaulted by a man, she is increasingly supported by default
     and not subjected to that treatment.

PF7. Cases in which an adult woman has sexual contact with an
     underage boy (e.g., a female teacher with a male student) are
     frequently described in media as "affairs" or "relationships."
     Mirror cases — adult man with underage girl — are consistently
     described as abuse, assault, or statutory rape.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SEXUAL AND ROMANTIC DYNAMICS (SR)
Concrete patterns in dating, partner choice, and how sexual behavior
is judged.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SR1. Dating app data consistently shows that female attention
     concentrates on a minority of men (roughly the top 10–20% of
     men receive the majority of right-swipes from women), while
     male attention distributes much more evenly across women.

SR2. In younger cohorts, women now out-credential men in most
     Western countries (more bachelor's and graduate degrees) and
     out-earn men in some urban educated subsets. At the same time,
     women's stated and revealed partner preferences continue to
     favor higher-status men — men who earn more or are more
     educated than they are.

SR3. A man being involuntarily celibate past a certain age is
     socially stigmatized and treated as a mark against him. A
     woman in the same position is not stigmatized in the same way.

SR4. "Incel" — a slur for an involuntarily celibate man — is a
     mainstream pejorative with wide circulation. No equivalent
     widely-used pejorative exists for an involuntarily single
     woman.

SR5. A woman having had many sexual partners is commonly framed as
     her prerogative, her self-actualization, or her sexual freedom.
     A man having had many sexual partners is commonly framed as
     predatory, exploitative, or a warning sign about his character.
     The same behavior (extensive sexual experience) carries
     different moral weight depending on the sex of the person doing
     it.

SR6. In mainstream media and sex education, male sexual desire is
     presented as suspect by default — something to be managed,
     contained, or apologized for. Female sexual desire is presented
     as healthy by default — something to be explored, honored, or
     celebrated.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RELATIONAL AND PRIVATE SPHERE (RS)
Specific roles, expectations, and invisible contributions inside
relationships and families.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RS1. Women initiate roughly 70% of US divorces.

RS2. After divorce, self-reported life satisfaction trajectories
     diverge by sex: men show worse average outcomes (lower
     satisfaction, higher rates of depression and suicide) than
     women in the same demographic.

RS3. Men have fewer close friendships on average than women, and
     the gap has widened over the past three decades.

RS4. Men's friendships decline sharply after marriage (fewer
     close friends, less frequent contact). Women's friendships are
     more stable across the same transition.

RS5. "Emotional labor" — the invisible work of managing feelings
     and relationships, typically framed as done more by women — is
     a mainstream concept. There is no equivalent mainstream term
     for the invisible contributions more often made by men: taking
     on physical risk for the household, shouldering financial risk,
     absorbing blame when things go wrong.

RS6. "Weaponized incompetence" — doing a task badly on purpose so
     someone else takes it over, typically framed as a male pattern
     — is mainstream shorthand. There is no equivalent mainstream
     shorthand for the inverse pattern: insisting a task be done a
     particular way and treating a partner's attempt as inadequate
     ("gatekeeping" is sometimes used but with far less reach).

RS7. Men are still culturally expected to propose marriage, pay for
     dates, handle outdoor and dangerous household tasks (climbing
     ladders, dealing with intruders, lifting heavy objects), and
     confront threats. These are not usually framed as "labor."
     Female-coded domestic contributions — meal planning,
     scheduling, maintaining family relationships, "mental load" —
     are framed as labor.

RS8. A man alone with his own children in public is often asked
     whether he is "babysitting." A woman alone with her own
     children is not asked the same question.

RS9. An absent father is commonly labeled "deadbeat" or irresponsible
     — the absence is read as a choice he made badly. An absent
     mother is more commonly framed as in crisis, troubled, or
     unwell — the absence is read as a situation that happened to
     her rather than a choice she made. "Deadbeat mom" has very
     little mainstream usage compared to "deadbeat dad."

RS10. Same-sex social cruelty is framed differently by sex. Female-
      on-female relational aggression — rumor, exclusion, reputational
      sabotage — is framed in mainstream media and advice discourse
      as interpersonal drama. Male-on-male aggression — physical
      confrontation, status violence — is framed as a social problem
      calling for institutional intervention (anti-bullying programs,
      school-violence prevention). Both are real; the framings differ.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EDUCATION (ED)
Concrete statistical and institutional asymmetries in schooling.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ED1. Women now earn roughly 60% of US bachelor's degrees. The gap
     favoring women is larger than the gap favoring men was when
     Title IX was passed in 1972.

ED2. Boys trail girls in reading and writing achievement at every
     grade level in most Western countries.

ED3. Boys are diagnosed with ADHD and behavioral disorders at
     several times the rate of girls.

ED4. Boys are suspended and disciplined at higher rates than girls
     for comparable infractions. For example: in a classroom
     incident where two students talk back to the teacher at
     similar volume and level, the boy is statistically more likely
     to receive a suspension while the girl is more likely to
     receive a verbal warning or lower-level consequence.

ED5. Men make up roughly a quarter of US K–12 teachers and a small
     single-digit percentage of early childhood educators. Women
     hold the corresponding majorities in both.

ED6. Scholarships, mentorship programs, and recruitment initiatives
     for women in STEM fields are widespread and institutionally
     supported. Equivalent programs for men entering female-dominated
     fields (nursing, elementary education, social work) are rare.

ED7. Campus women's resource centers are standard at US
     universities. Attempts to establish men's resource centers
     have, on several campuses, been met with organized protest
     that prevented or reversed the establishment.

ED8. Title IX's enforcement mechanisms have been used to address
     female underrepresentation in specific fields and activities
     (e.g., athletics). They have not been used in parallel fashion
     to address male underrepresentation in domains where men are
     now the minority (e.g., undergraduate enrollment overall).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WORKPLACE AND ECONOMICS (WE)
Concrete statistical and policy asymmetries in work and pay.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WE1. Men make up roughly 92% of US workplace fatalities.

WE2. Men dominate the occupations that are most physically
     dangerous (logging, fishing, roofing), dirtiest (sanitation,
     mining, oil work), and most socially isolating (long-haul
     trucking, remote operations).

WE3. Men work longer average hours than women, take fewer career
     breaks, and commute further on average.

WE4. The most commonly reported "gender pay gap" figure is the raw
     difference between the average pay of all working men and all
     working women. It is typically not adjusted for differences in
     hours worked, occupation, industry, seniority, or continuous
     years of experience — factors which, when adjusted for,
     substantially reduce the measured gap.

WE5. Men are overrepresented at both the top and the bottom of the
     income distribution — more billionaires and CEOs, and also
     more of the unemployed, incarcerated, and homeless.

WE6. Initiatives that direct public and institutional funding to
     women-owned businesses exist widely. Equivalent initiatives
     for men-owned businesses do not exist.

WE7. Corporate diversity-and-inclusion programs address bias
     against women. They do not typically address bias against men,
     even in functions where men are the minority (e.g., HR,
     marketing, early education).

WE8. Workplace diversity metrics track female representation in
     male-dominated fields (percentage of women in engineering,
     leadership, STEM) but rarely track male underrepresentation in
     female-dominated fields.

WE9. In the current configuration of workplace discourse, a woman
     displaying strong directive behavior (interrupting, pushing
     back hard, setting a firm line) tends to be described as
     "assertive leadership." A man displaying the identical
     behavior in the same role tends to be described as
     "aggressive" or "arrogant." (Note: this observation reverses
     the older, more familiar claim — that women were penalized for
     assertiveness while men were rewarded — and asserts that in
     current professional settings the coding now runs the other
     way. This reversal is contested; some researchers argue the
     older asymmetry still holds in many settings.)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
HEALTH AND MORTALITY (HM)
Concrete statistical asymmetries in how long each sex lives and what
each sex dies from.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HM1. Men die by suicide at three to four times the rate of women in
     most Western countries.

HM2. Men have shorter life expectancy than women in virtually every
     country in the world. In the US the gap is roughly five to six
     years.

HM3. Men are roughly 70% of homicide victims and roughly 80% of
     homicide perpetrators.

HM4. Men make up the substantial majority of homeless populations
     and of overdose deaths.

HM5. Men are less likely than women to seek preventive medical
     care (routine check-ups, early screening) and more likely to
     die of preventable conditions.

HM6. Prostate cancer research receives substantially less public
     and charitable funding than breast cancer research despite
     comparable incidence rates in the population.

HM7. Women's health awareness campaigns are culturally prominent
     (pink ribbons, breast cancer awareness month, heart disease
     in women campaigns). Men's health awareness campaigns (e.g.,
     Movember) exist but are far smaller in cultural footprint and
     institutional backing.

HM8. Mental health services and outreach are mostly designed
     around patterns more common in women: talking about feelings,
     seeking help, describing internal states. Patterns more common
     in men — irritability, physical symptoms, action-oriented
     avoidance, externalizing — are less well served by the design
     of mainstream mental-health services.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VIOLENCE AND SAFETY (VS)
Who gets hurt, who gets protected, and who is expected to do the
protecting.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

VS1. Men are the majority of victims of violent crime overall, and
     the overwhelming majority of victims of stranger violence
     (violence committed by someone the victim doesn't know).

VS2. Public safety campaigns and urban safety design (lighting,
     patrol patterns, hotline infrastructure) are built around
     reducing harm to women. Male victimization, which is numerically
     larger in absolute terms, receives little equivalent attention.

VS3. "Women and children first" persists as a norm in evacuation
     framing and informal culture (who gets onto the lifeboat, who
     gets shielded in a crisis). No parallel norm prioritizes men's
     survival in emergencies.

VS4. Men are culturally expected to intervene in violent situations
     at risk to themselves — confront the intruder, break up the
     fight, stand between the threat and bystanders. Women are not
     held to the same expectation.

VS5. "Protect women and children" is a common political and
     rhetorical phrase. No parallel phrase — positioning men as a
     category requiring protection — has comparable mainstream use.

VS6. US combat deaths across post-WWII conflicts are overwhelmingly
     male (single-digit percentages female or lower). This reflects
     both historically restricted female combat roles and continued
     male overrepresentation in front-line positions after those
     restrictions were formally removed.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FAMILY LAW AND STATE POLICY (FL)
Concrete legal and policy asymmetries.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FL1. Mothers receive primary physical custody in the majority of
     contested US custody cases. Fathers receive primary physical
     custody in a minority of cases, including in cases where the
     two parents are otherwise similarly situated.

FL2. Because custody falls disproportionately to mothers (FL1),
     child support obligations — owed by the non-custodial parent
     to the custodial parent — flow disproportionately from fathers
     to mothers. A father who sought primary custody and was denied
     remains legally obligated to pay the mother for raising the
     children.

FL3. A man can be legally required to continue paying child support
     for a child later proven — through DNA testing — not to be
     biologically his. Women face no equivalent category of being
     legally required to financially support a child they did not
     produce or agree to raise.

FL4. Paternity fraud — a woman knowingly naming a man as the
     biological father when he is not — is not criminalized in most
     jurisdictions.

FL5. US Selective Service registration (the draft-eligibility
     registry) applies only to men. Women are not required to
     register, despite the formal removal of gender restrictions on
     combat roles in the US military.

FL6. Sentencing research consistently shows men receive longer
     prison sentences than women for comparable offenses with
     comparable prior records.

FL7. Domestic violence shelters for men are rare to nonexistent in
     most jurisdictions. Shelters for women are institutionally
     established, publicly funded, and geographically widespread.

FL8. Violence against women is a discrete federal funding and
     policy category (e.g., the Violence Against Women Act in the
     US). Violence against men is not a parallel funding or policy
     category.

FL9. Affirmative action and diversity programs target female
     underrepresentation (in STEM, in leadership, in certain
     trades). No parallel programs target male underrepresentation
     in teaching, nursing, veterinary medicine, psychology, or
     social work.

FL10. Non-therapeutic genital cutting of female minors is
      criminalized in most Western countries and prosecuted as
      child abuse (even in its mildest forms). Non-therapeutic
      genital cutting of male minors — routine infant circumcision —
      remains legal and common, with no equivalent criminal
      framework and no comparable mainstream policy debate about
      the bodily integrity of male children.

FL11. In US divorces, court-ordered spousal support (alimony) flows
      overwhelmingly from men to women. Historical US Census data
      finds roughly 97% of alimony recipients are women. Surveys of
      matrimonial attorneys report that cases in which the wife pays
      alimony to the husband — including cases where she was the
      higher earner during the marriage — remain a small minority.

FL12. In contested US divorces with children, the family home is
      commonly either awarded to the custodial parent for continued
      use or ordered sold with proceeds divided. Because mothers
      receive primary physical custody in the majority of contested
      cases (FL1), the practical effect is that the family home is
      more often retained by or awarded to the mother than to the
      father.

FL13. No discrete federal funding category, policy initiative, or
      major advocacy infrastructure currently addresses divorce-
      outcome asymmetries (custody, support, asset distribution,
      post-divorce life satisfaction) as a sex-equity concern in
      the way VAWA, Title IX enforcement, and women-in-STEM
      programs address asymmetries elsewhere. Divorce outcomes that
      favor women on average are not treated in policy as an
      imbalance requiring correction.

FL14. Pretrial detention research finds men are detained pretrial at
      higher rates than women for comparable charges and risk scores,
      and the gap persists after controlling for flight risk and
      criminal history. Starr (2012, 2015) finds the magnitude of the
      sex gap in federal sentencing is larger than the magnitude of
      the race gap. The sex gap is not treated as a civil-rights
      concern in the way the race gap is.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TRAJECTORIES OVER TIME (TS)
Concrete time-series observations: how specific sex-asymmetric
outcomes have moved over the past several decades, in which
direction, at what rate. Each entry is individually checkable
against the cited data source or an equivalent one.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

TS1. Women's share of US bachelor's degrees rose from roughly 42% in
     1970 to roughly 60% by 2020, a monotonic climb across the
     entire period. The climb continued through the 1972 passage of
     Title IX and across every subsequent decade; it has not
     reversed or plateaued.

TS2. In US data, the male suicide rate rose over the past two
     decades. The female rate also rose, but by a smaller amount.
     The 3-to-4x male-to-female suicide ratio has not narrowed; in
     several recent years it has widened.

TS3. The share of US workplace fatalities that are male has
     remained roughly 92% across the past several decades. Female
     representation in safer high-status occupations has risen
     sharply over the same period; female representation in the
     most dangerous occupations (logging, fishing, roofing, mining)
     has not.

TS4. In US data, mothers' majority share of primary physical
     custody in contested cases has remained roughly stable over
     the past several decades. Several US jurisdictions have moved
     formal doctrine toward equal-custody defaults across that
     period; the actual distribution of outcomes has not converged
     to the formal doctrine.

TS5. In polling of US adults, the share of men reporting "no close
     friends" rose from roughly 3% in 1990 to roughly 15% in surveys
     conducted around 2021 (Survey Center on American Life). Women's
     reported friendship counts over the same period have been more
     stable.

TS6. US Selective Service (draft registration) has been male-only
     since its reinstatement in 1980 and remains so. Combat roles
     in the US military were formally opened to women in 2013.
     Formal sex-equality doctrine has advanced substantially over
     the same period; the male-only registration requirement has
     not changed.

TS7. The sex gap in US federal criminal sentencing has not narrowed
     meaningfully over the past several decades. The sex gap is
     larger than the black-white sentencing gap and has been
     roughly stable over the same period in which the race gap has
     been the subject of sustained reform effort.

TS8. In US Bureau of Labor Statistics data, the share of K-12
     teachers who are male declined from roughly 33% in 1980 to
     roughly 24% by 2020. The share of early childhood educators
     who are male remains in low single digits with no meaningful
     upward trend. Programs to expand female representation in
     male-dominated fields have been widespread over the same
     period; no equivalent program to expand male representation
     in teaching has been launched at comparable scale.
