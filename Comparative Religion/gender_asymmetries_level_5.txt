OBSERVED ASYMMETRIES IN HOW MEN AND WOMEN ARE TREATED
LAYER 5 — patterns across the Layer 4 higher-order patterns


WRITING GOAL

Demystification. Use plain, common language and keep cognitive load low,
while staying precise enough that a specialist would agree it's
accurate. Be hard on the topic, soft on the reader: anticipate where
someone might feel defensive, but don't soften what's actually true.
Where something is uncertain, contested, or where an expert would
object, surface that directly in plain terms — don't hide it behind
jargon, hedging, emotional appeals, or common misreadings the reader
might bring in.

Drifts to avoid:
  (1) academic drift — reaching for Latinate words, nominalizations, or
      jargon when a common word works.
  (2) bland drift — hedging or both-sidesing until no real claim is
      made.
  (3) false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would
      object to.
  (4) emotional cushioning that buries the actual point.
If accessibility and precision seem to conflict in a sentence, flag
that sentence rather than silently picking one.

Self-check before releasing a version: a smart non-expert should
understand it on first read; a specialist should read it and not
wince; no sentence should hedge without saying anything. If any of
those fail, rewrite.


WHAT LAYER 5 IS

Layer 4 treats the Layer 3 structural claims themselves as observations
and asks what patterns run across them. Layer 5 takes the next step:
it treats the Layer 4 higher-order patterns as the new observations
and asks what holds across them.

A Layer 5 entry is a pattern across Layer 4 entries — a rule about how
the Layer 4 rules hang together. It is not an inference over Layer 3,
not a structural claim about Layer 2, and not a causal or historical
claim that reaches past what the Layer 4 entries together support. It
sits one step further back than Layer 4 and asks: given H1–H7 as a
set, what holds across them?

Layer 5 is thinner than Layer 4. Each step back compresses. Where
Layer 3 has twelve entries and Layer 4 has seven, Layer 5 settles
into two. If a candidate reads as genuinely L5-shaped, it earns its
place on the basis of what the H-entries jointly support, not on a
desire to fill the layer out.

Each Layer 5 entry lists the Layer 4 entries it draws from using their
H-numbers. If those higher-order patterns fall or are revised, the
Layer 5 entry falls or is revised with them.

Layer 5 entries use the prefix G- (for governing pattern) to keep them
distinct from Layer 1 (two-letter domain codes), Layer 2 (I-), Layer 3
(M-), and Layer 4 (H-).

Clusters of Layer 5 entries that appear to share a deeper rule are
Layer 6 material, not a reason to merge Layer 5 entries. Entries are
only merged when they are genuinely identical.

Throughout Layer 5: entries describe patterns visible across the
Layer 4 higher-order patterns. They are claims about patterns, not
about anyone's intent, consciousness, or coordination.


FOUR FILTER CRITERIA

Each entry must pass all four to stay in the main list:

  (1) Both-sidedness. The pattern holds when tested against both
      directions of the asymmetry, not only one.

  (2) Smallest interpretive move. The smallest step the Layer 4
      entries together support, not a leap past them to bigger
      causal, historical, or psychological claims.

  (3) Specialist-wince test. A specialist in the relevant domain
      might disagree with the framing but would not say the claim is
      factually wrong or technically sloppy.

  (4) Falsifiability. A concrete class of evidence — including
      changes to the underlying Layer 4 entries — would force
      revision or retraction.

Entries that fail an audit move to the central staging file with the
readmission condition that would let them back in. A Layer 5
candidate may also fail because it is actually a Layer 6 claim — a
pattern across Layer 5 entries, or a causal/dynamics claim L4 does
not support. Such candidates are held for Layer 6, also in the
staging file.

LAYER 5: PATTERNS ACROSS HIGHER-ORDER PATTERNS
================================================================================


G1. The asymmetry doesn't run through one channel; it runs through
    every channel the discourse uses at once.

    Look at the H-entries as a set. The asymmetry shows up in what
    counts as progress and which direction the equipment is pointed
    in (H1); in where the asymmetry lives — underneath the rules
    rather than in them (H2); in who gets cast as the agent and who
    as the one something is happening to (H3); in what short words
    exist for which side's patterns and what it costs to say them
    out loud (H4); in whether a claim gets judged at the class level
    or the individual level (H5); in which publications and
    institutions cover which half of the evidence (H6); and in how
    the framework itself gets received when it's raised (H7).

    Each of these is a different register. They run to the same
    direction at the same time. The claim is not "the asymmetry
    exists in one place and there are side effects elsewhere"; it is
    "whatever part of the discourse you look at, the asymmetry is
    the same shape there too."

    Shape note. H1–H6 describe the asymmetry as it shows up in the
    world — in rules, tools, casting, vocabulary, framing, and
    reading venues. H7 describes how claims of the kind H1–H6 make
    get received when someone raises them. In H7 the framework is
    pointing at itself. Both kinds fit G1 as registers where the
    asymmetry runs, but the two kinds are different shapes, and that
    difference is flagged here rather than hidden. If H7 were
    removed, G1 would still hold as a claim about H1–H6; adding H7
    strengthens the pattern rather than being its sole support.

    Narrow claim. G1 says the direction is shared across registers.
    It does not say the registers cause each other, that removing
    one would collapse the rest, or that the shared direction
    implies intent or coordination. Those would be bigger claims.
    G1 is the smallest step the H-entries together support.

    Draws from: H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6, H7.


G2. Each register is sex-neutral in its rules and sex-asymmetric in
    its operation.

    H2 says it directly for the first register: the formal rules are
    mostly symmetric; the defaults underneath them aren't. The same
    shape runs through the other H-entries. H1's toolkit — how
    outcome gaps get read, how neutral-looking rules get applied,
    which patterns get short names — is sex-neutral in its
    definition; the direction it points in is the asymmetry. H3's
    agent and recipient roles are not assigned by a rule that names
    sex; the default assignment does. H5's class-or-individual
    framing sits on a rule that isn't worded by sex; which side of
    the rule gets picked up tracks the direction of the claim.

    This is not an incidental pattern. Each H-entry describes a
    mechanism with a local, sex-neutral justification that runs in
    one direction when you stack the cases. A reader can agree with
    every rule line by line and still see the outcomes sort the
    same way.

    The fit varies. H1, H2, H3, and H5 fit this shape directly —
    there is a clean split between the rule and the operation, and
    the asymmetry lives in the split. H4 (vocabulary supply), H6
    (readership coverage), and H7 (reception) fit more loosely: no
    rule codes for sex in any of them, but the mechanism producing
    the asymmetry isn't itself a single rule that gets applied one
    way or the other. The shape is clearest in H1, H2, H3, H5 and
    operates as a looser fit in H4, H6, H7. Flagged rather than
    overclaimed.

    One consequence. Arguments conducted at the level of formal
    neutrality — "but the rule treats everyone the same" — can be
    factually right and still miss the claim. The rule is one thing;
    how the rule is picked up, applied, and aggregated is another.

    Draws from: H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6, H7.
