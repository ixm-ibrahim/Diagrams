================================================================================
OBSERVED ASYMMETRIES IN HOW MEN AND WOMEN ARE TREATED
LAYER 3 — structural claims drawn across Layer 2 inferences


WRITING GOAL

Demystification. Use plain, common language and keep cognitive load low,
while staying precise enough that a specialist would agree it's
accurate. Be hard on the topic, soft on the reader: anticipate where
someone might feel defensive, but don't soften what's actually true.
Where something is uncertain, contested, or where an expert would
object, surface that directly in plain terms — don't hide it behind
jargon, hedging, emotional appeals, or common misreadings the reader
might bring in.

Drifts to avoid:
  (1) academic drift — reaching for Latinate words, nominalizations, or
      jargon when a common word works.
  (2) bland drift — hedging or both-sidesing until no real claim is
      made.
  (3) false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would
      object to.
  (4) emotional cushioning that buries the actual point.
If accessibility and precision seem to conflict in a sentence, flag
that sentence rather than silently picking one.

Self-check before releasing a version: a smart non-expert should
understand it on first read; a specialist should read it and not
wince; no sentence should hedge without saying anything. If any of
those fail, rewrite.


WHAT LAYER 3 IS

Layer 1 is observations — individual facts that can each be checked
against the world. Layer 2 is inferences drawn across those
observations: rules or patterns the observations together support.

Layer 3 treats the Layer 2 inferences themselves as the new
"observations" and asks what claims they jointly support when you
stand back one more level. A Layer 3 entry is a structural claim
about how the Layer 2 inferences hang together. It is not a direct
observation and not an inference over specific cases; it is a rule
about the rules.

Each Layer 3 entry lists the Layer 2 inferences it draws from using
their I-numbers. If those inferences fall or are revised, the Layer 3
entry falls or is revised with them.

Layer 3 entries use the prefix M- (for meta-inference) to keep them
distinct from Layer 1 (two-letter domain codes) and Layer 2 (I-).

Clusters of Layer 3 entries that appear to share a deeper rule are
Layer 4 material, not a reason to merge Layer 3 entries. Entries are
only merged when they are genuinely identical.

Throughout Layer 3: entries describe defaults in mainstream discourse
and institutional behavior. They are claims about patterns and
defaults, not about anyone's intent, consciousness, or coordination.


FOUR FILTER CRITERIA

Each entry must pass all four to stay in the main list:

  (1) Both-sidedness. The structural claim holds when tested against
      both directions of the asymmetry, not only one.

  (2) Smallest interpretive move. The smallest step the Layer 2
      inferences together support, not a leap past them to bigger
      causal, historical, or psychological claims.

  (3) Specialist-wince test. A specialist in the relevant domain
      might disagree with the framing but would not say the claim is
      factually wrong or technically sloppy.

  (4) Falsifiability. A concrete class of evidence — including
      changes to the underlying Layer 2 inferences — would force
      revision or retraction.

Entries that fail an audit move to the central staging file with the
readmission condition that would let them back in. A Layer 3
candidate may also fail because it is actually a Layer 4 claim (a
pattern across Layer 3 entries); such candidates are held for
Layer 4, also in the staging file.

LAYER 3: STRUCTURAL CLAIMS
================================================================================


M1. Speech asymmetry runs harder and more one-directionally than
    outcome asymmetry does.

    The Layer 2 inferences sort into two kinds. One kind is about
    speech — what can be said about whom, which terms are available,
    which phrases cost the speaker something. The other kind is about
    outcomes — who dies at work, who keeps the children, who goes to
    prison longer, who drops out of school, who kills themselves. On
    the speech side, the asymmetry runs strongly in one direction: it
    is cheaper and more socially acceptable to speak critically of
    men as a class, and mainstream language supplies more usable
    words for doing so. On the outcome side, the asymmetries are
    mixed: several large domains run against men, others run against
    women (the female-disadvantaging side is referenced in I11's
    internal scope rather than given its own dedicated L2 entries
    here), and the direction depends on the domain.

    Draws from: I4, I5, I7, I8, I11, I12, I13, I16, I17, I21, I22,
    I23, I25, I27, I28, I30.


M2. The Layer 2 inferences cluster into two partially non-overlapping
    sets of patterns, and which set a reader encounters depends on
    which discourse they're reading in.

    One set describes speech conditions, representation at the top of
    workplaces, sexual-harm asymmetries, and credibility defaults
    favoring women's reports. The other set describes outcomes at the
    bottom (mortality, homelessness, incarceration), family-law
    outcomes, educational underperformance, and speech conditions
    constraining what can be said about men or by men. Each set is
    factually supported by the L1 observations its L2 entries cite;
    neither set is the whole picture. Discourse that treats one set
    as the whole picture is working from half the evidence.

    Draws from: I5, I9, I11, I13, I16, I17, I20, I21, I22, I23, I25,
    I27, I28, I30, I34.


M3. When the facts are unclear, institutions default to reading harm
    as running from man to woman.

    In settings where an institution has to decide without the facts
    being clear — a mutual-violence call, a distressed accusation, an
    act that could be read as aggression or as venting — the default
    reading places the man as the person responsible and the woman as
    the person harmed.

    Draws from: I6, I7, I14, I15, I31.


M4. The agent-vs-recipient positioning of men and women is not
    confined to discourse-addressing; the same positioning runs
    across several distinct institutional behaviors.

    I35 establishes that mainstream discourse addresses men as agents
    of conduct to be corrected and women as recipients of concern or
    services. Four other L2 inferences, each drawn from its own L1
    evidence, show the same positioning operating in other registers:

      - Responsibility attribution (I6, I31): in the same interaction,
        male choices are treated as his to own; female choices are
        softened into pressure, harm, or circumstance.
      - Act classification (I14): the same physical or verbal act is
        downgraded when done by a woman against a man and upgraded
        when done by a man against a woman.
      - Institutional intake under ambiguity (I15): crisis
        infrastructure and primary-aggressor defaults treat the male
        partner as default agent of harm.

    Each of those L2s arrived at its own aggregation from its own L1
    cases. What is new at L3 is that the same positioning — man as
    agent, woman as recipient — runs across all five. It is not only
    a feature of who the messaging is addressed to; it is a feature
    of how acts are scored, how responsibility is assigned, and how
    institutions act when facts are ambiguous.

    Draws from: I6, I14, I15, I31, I35.


M5. Outcome gaps where men do worse are read as men's own doing;
    outcome gaps where women do worse are read as the system's doing.

    Across suicide, homelessness, early death, incarceration, school
    underperformance, workplace fatalities, combat exposure, and
    contested custody, the default reading is that the gap reflects
    choices men make, industries men select, or the kind of man he
    is. Across matching or smaller gaps where women do worse, the
    default reading is that the gap reflects a system — bias,
    pattern, structure. The same category of evidence gets a
    different default reading depending on which direction the gap
    runs.

    Draws from: I11, I20, I21, I22, I23, I25, I26, I27.


M6. Rules that look neutral are applied by direction.

    Several interpretive moves look even-handed on their face: judge
    the content not the speaker; individual counter-cases don't rebut
    group-level claims; past behavior bears on present judgment;
    attribute conduct to agency rather than circumstance where agency
    is visible. Each move has a local justification wherever it is
    used. What is visible at L3 is that the direction in which each
    move is applied correlates with the direction of the claim it
    operates on.

    When a claim runs against men, the class-level side of the rules
    applies: the pattern counts, individual exceptions don't rebut
    it, past behavior is admissible, agency is named. When a claim
    runs against women, the individual side of the same rules
    applies: the counter-case admits, the class-level claim becomes
    an illegitimate generalization, past behavior doesn't carry,
    circumstance takes over from agency. The rules are not worded
    differently by sex; the direction of application is.

    Draws from: I1, I4, I9, I13, I31, I32, I34.


M7. Short words exist for one side's patterns and not the other's,
    and the missing words raise the cost of naming the missing
    patterns.

    Female-disadvantaging patterns and male-misbehavior patterns have
    compact, mainstream-usable names: pay gap, glass ceiling,
    mansplaining, toxic masculinity, rape culture, male fragility,
    weaponized incompetence. Male-disadvantaging patterns and
    female-misbehavior patterns mostly don't, or have names with a
    fraction of the reach: sentencing gap, custody gap, workplace-
    death gap, conscription gap, toxic femininity, female relational
    aggression. A pattern without a compact name takes a clause to
    refer to and does not circulate in casual conversation at the
    same frequency as one with a compact name.

    Draws from: I8, I12, I16, I17, I28, I30.


M8. Victim and oppressor designations hold even in sub-domains where
    the running facts don't support them.

    Once a group is designated the victim class on a given axis, the
    designation persists in sub-domains where the current harm has
    reversed. Women retain the victim designation in higher education
    in countries where women now take the clear majority of degrees;
    in sentencing, where men serve longer time for comparable
    offenses; in combat deaths, which are overwhelmingly male. Once a
    group is designated oppressor, current disadvantages in specific
    domains don't lift the label. The designation is held
    independently of the running outcome data in those sub-domains.

    Draws from: I10, I11, I26, I28, I29, I34.


M9. The switch between "men and women are equal" and "men and women
    are not similarly situated" tracks which rule preserves the
    current female-favorable outcome in a given domain.

    I32 establishes that both claims are in active use and the switch
    between them is not flagged. The three other L2s cited here show
    the switch operating as a pattern rather than as occasional slip:

      - Custody (I27): contested-case outcomes favoring mothers are
        preserved under a case-level standard that treats mothers and
        fathers as not similarly situated by default.
      - Alimony direction (I33): transfer runs from men to women at a
        rate larger than earning structure alone would produce;
        preserved by a case-level framing that doesn't apply the
        "equal" rule symmetrically.
      - Draft registration (I26): registration remains male-only,
        preserved under "not similarly situated" reasoning, alongside
        formal equality doctrine.

    What is new at L3 is that the choice between the two rules is not
    random or case-specific; in the domains cited, the rule reached
    for is the one that preserves the female-favorable outcome in
    that domain.

    Draws from: I26, I27, I32, I33.


M10. Whether a gender claim can be raised without cost depends on its
     direction, not on how well-evidenced it is.

     A well-documented claim of female disadvantage enters mainstream
     discussion at lower cost than a well-documented claim of male
     disadvantage. The first is less often met with motive-
     questioning, tone policing, or a demand for a ready policy fix
     before the observation itself is engaged. The variable doing the
     sorting is the direction of the claim, not the strength of the
     evidence behind it.

     Draws from: I1, I2, I3, I5, I7.


M11. The move toward sex equality has been active in one direction
     and absent in the other.

     I36 establishes that several large sex-asymmetric outcomes have
     not moved toward convergence over the past several decades. I37
     establishes that several male-disadvantaging outcomes have
     worsened in absolute terms over the same period in which female-
     disadvantaging outcomes were targeted for correction. I38
     establishes that formal doctrinal moves toward equal treatment
     have not produced parallel practical movement on the male-
     disadvantaging side of the domains those doctrines reach.

     What is new at L3 is that these three hold together as one
     trajectory, not three coincident ones: where a doctrinal shift
     would reduce a female-disadvantaging gap, the practical
     trajectory has been sustained and substantial; where the same
     shift would reduce a male-disadvantaging gap, the practical
     trajectory is flat or running in the male-disadvantaging
     direction. Over the observation window the cited L2s cover,
     there is no evidence of the male-disadvantaging side moving
     toward the equal-treatment endpoint.

     Draws from: I36, I37, I38.


M12. The evidentiary split between the two subsets of Layer 2
     inferences is also a readership split. Where each subset gets
     read tracks the venue's ideology, not a random or mixed
     sampling across readers.

     M2 established that the Layer 2 inferences cluster into two
     partially non-overlapping sets and that each set is factually
     supported by its own Layer 1 evidence. I39 establishes,
     independently, that those same two sets map onto the
     ideological split in mainstream media and its adjacent
     institutions: the female-disadvantage set is where left-coded
     publications, academia, and major funders put their attention;
     the male-disadvantage set is where right-coded publications and
     men's-issues venues put theirs, without comparable institutional
     reinforcement.

     What's new at L3 is the join: the evidentiary split is the same
     as the readership split. A typical reader inside one ecosystem
     is not missing the other set by chance; the venues sort the
     evidence, and each venue reliably delivers roughly one side. It
     follows that "half the evidence" is not a metaphor — two actual
     halves of the catalogue get distributed to two different
     populations of readers.

     Draws from: I39.
