## The chain

> `claim (inferential proof) <- cycle of (rule of inference) <- cycle of (rule of abduction) <- phenomenological primitives`

`A <- B` reads "A is grounded in B." A cycle is a chain with a clear start that has been wound into loops — never a closed circle.

## What the pieces are

**An inference** is getting a new statement out of statements you already have. "Pans on lit stoves get hot; this stove is lit; so this pan is hot."

**Deduction and induction are two kinds of inference — tried in the same court, on different charges.** Every rule earns its place by beating its rivals, and the rivals are always convicted of one of the same two crimes. Deduction's rival convicts itself out of its own mouth: "ice is always cold; this is ice; but not cold" — the denial *is* an exception to a premise that said "always"; the evidence arrives inside the rival's own statement. Induction's rival is convicted by the record: "the stones will start floating tomorrow" contradicts nothing — it fails because nothing that ever occurred points at it. Deduction feels more certain only because its trials end faster; neither kind stands anywhere except on the same chain below.

**Abduction is choosing between candidates already on the table, and it has two moments.** Comparing — which needs no preferences: check which candidate covers what actually occurred, and which carries made-up parts (the one settled comparison rule: never hold a candidate that is strictly worse on both counts). And settling — actually holding the winner, which no comparison ever forces: what moves anyone off the fence is that they wanted an answer. Wanting the truth is a working part of the machine, wherever reasoning closes.

**The bottom.** The floor (below) is not a rule but a claim, used as a premise inside these trials. Below the floor sit two things that are not claims at all: **the want** — everything here binds you because you asked a question; it has nothing to say to someone not seeking — and **occurrences** — the snap you feel when you try to deny an obvious conclusion happens to you; things that happen need no license, so the chain of "why?" ends without a vicious loop.

**Why trust the whole practice.** The machine's parts do lean on each other in a loop when running — but each pass is fed by fresh occurrences, so it never closes into a circle. And what separates this practice from a fortune-teller's (whose convictions also just arrive, freshly, gaze after gaze) is not the loop's shape: it is that when reasoning is caught wrong it changes the rule *everywhere* — refinement — while the fortune-teller excuses one case at a time — the double standard. Reasoning's credential is that it keeps passing the floor's own test.

## The floor of the abduction cycle — settled

Plain form: **"methods that justify falsehood can't distinguish it from truth."**

Grounded form, two steps (one rule at two depths — tree rows F1, F2):

> **A method of reasoning that justifies falsehood is independent from truth.**
> **Truth and falsehood are not distinct in a method of reasoning that is independent from truth.**

In one picture: a compass with a painted needle points the same way regardless of where north is. Tested: challenged against every rule of reasoning we know, plus open hunts across formal logic, statistics, and law/medicine/history — every defense of every rule bottoms out here; no counterexample found.

The floor judges methods against what is currently known. Learning something new does not change the rules of preference — the same rules get applied to the new knowledge as it is integrated — so honest reasoning from incomplete knowledge is never disqualified; its conclusions correct as knowledge grows.

## The floor's children — settled, doubly related

A justified falsehood can enter reasoning in exactly two ways, and **the absence is the crime; the establishable collision is the evidence.**

**Child 1 — grounding.** Plain form: **"Ungrounded reasoning cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** The reasoning claims what is absent.

**Child 2 — consistency.** Plain form: **"Inconsistent reasoning cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** What the reasoning endorses collides — and since holding claims together in one act of reasoning claims that they fit together, a deniable collision is itself a claimed-but-absent fitting-together. So consistency is both a child of the floor and a child of grounding (the tree is many-to-many); grounding comes first on every path. Each child still does work the other cannot: collision convicts where the absence cannot be located (a conclusion known false whose failed step nobody can find); absence convicts where nothing collides (the perfectly cohesive circle). A conflict among *true* claims convicts no one — that is a paradox, a question answered by refinement.

## The children's children — settled

**Under grounding** — a process only has starts and steps:

- **The start.** Plain: **"Reasoning that starts from speculation cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** A start is any step depending on nothing before it, wherever it sits; speculation is an ungrounded claim from ignorance. A start is grounded when the claim *as made* occurred — the gauge reading happened — while endings are judged as said. (Ex.: "maybe you were dreaming" entered against "I got wet in the rain.")
- **The steps.** Plain: **"Reasoning whose steps do not result from the steps before them cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** A step leans on a grounded correlation — backed by one, it has its resulting even when this instance misses; the crime is a click claimed where nothing grounded connects. (Ex.: "house prices doubled, so the crash is coming." Lies queued here: claiming a resulting or a given one *knows* to be absent.)

**Under consistency** — the three things there are to collide with:

- **External.** Plain: **"Reasoning whose conclusions conflict with what is known to be true cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** (Ex.: "no swan is black," after black swans are known.)
- **Internal.** Plain: **"Reasoning whose own claims deny each other cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** One act of reasoning is a chain of claims; this fires when a claim in the chain denies another in it. (Ex.: the feather; "no one can know anything," where the stated claim denies the claim made by concluding it.)
- **Inferential.** Plain: **"Reasoning with double standards cannot distinguish truth from falsehood."** A double standard is a process's ungrounded exception to its own method's pattern; its opposite is refinement. Hangs from both sides of the tree. (Ex.: three anecdotes accepted for the socks, a controlled study demanded from a matching case.)

## Vocabulary settled along the way

Match (a phenomenon matches a pattern when the pattern's quality occurs in it — what "like" means); exception (a match that does not follow the pattern), splitting into grounded (the counterexample — it occurs, so the pattern updates for every match: refinement) and ungrounded (claimed while absent — held only where wanted: the double standard's fuel); question, answer, knowledge (when a question's answer occurs), ignorance (when it is absent); speculation (an ungrounded claim from ignorance); contradiction (a conflict in which a claim is false — a verdict, its answer in knowledge) and paradox (a conflict in which the claims are true — a question, its answer in ignorance, answered by refinement); and one node awaiting the author's name: imagination that is independent from knowledge, where the flying pig lives.
