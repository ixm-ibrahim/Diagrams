# The Structure of a Claim — A Working Framework

**Author:** Ibrahim Mahmoud. **Status:** living document, draft 3 (2026-07-07). **Purpose:** an operating manual for analyzing claims — decomposing them, defending them, verifying them, and communicating them faithfully. This document is written to be handed to any reader, human or AI, with no other context. It is the latest statement of the author's framework and supersedes the author's earlier artifacts wherever they differ (§10).

---

## 0. To the reader — how to engage

Your job is to **apply this framework using its own definitions** — to decompose, defend, attack, and verify claims through its structures. It is not your job to first adjudicate whether the framework is true by standards imported from elsewhere. If you want to challenge it, rule 3 tells you how.

**Rules of engagement:**

1. **Definitions are binding.** Where this document defines a term, that sense overrides the textbook or everyday sense for the entire conversation. Contrast warnings (⚠) mark the terms where silently substituting the standard sense will corrupt your understanding. When in doubt, re-read the definition here.
2. **Quote, don't relabel.** When discussing the framework, use its words. If you must introduce a term of your own, define it on the spot in plain language — never assume the author shares your label. (This rule exists because an AI once abstracted the author's own notation into a description the author couldn't recognize.)
3. **Objections must name the step.** To object, identify the specific definition, inference, or chain-link you reject, state what you would replace it with, and name the standard you are applying — so the disagreement can be located precisely. External standards are admissible *when named*: naming them is exactly what makes the standard itself examinable alongside the step it attacks. What violates this rule is the unnamed standard: "that's unfalsifiable," "that's a faith claim," or "that's not how philosophers use the word" are genre-dismissals, not objections.
4. **Speculation is not proof or refutation — and this binds you too.** A merely possible alternative ("maybe you're hallucinating," "maybe the laws differ elsewhere") does not refute a grounded claim, and a merely possible support does not establish one. See §7 for the derivation. Hedging a verdict into oblivion because an alternative is *conceivable* is a violation.
5. **No false balance, no flattery.** The framework's own compass rule (§7) cuts both ways: you must flag any claim — including the author's — whose support would equally confirm a falsehood. Agreement that is not derived through the framework is worthless to the author. Do not give equal weight to all claims out of "neutrality"; the goal is truth, not comfort.
6. **Scope discipline.** Every verdict is indexed to a scope and to the verdict-giver's current knowledge (§6, pipeline 6). Always state the scope your verdict lives in.
7. **The honest-inquirer assumption.** This framework describes and serves a person putting in sincere effort to be aware of what is true — not the intentionally heedless, and not one whose pride prevents them from seeing truth. Failures of motivated reasoning are a real topic, but they are outside this document's scope.
8. **Rules of reasoning** (imported from the author's comparative religion project): inductive inferences hold until clear *counterexamples* — not mere speculation — invalidate them; favor best-fit explanations (most data, fewest assumptions), naming the metric of preference; reject arguments from ignorance and appeals to authority or mainstream popularity without evidence; verify seemingly mainstream views against primary evidence; refine iteratively when challenged; apply every principle uniformly and flag double standards wherever they appear.

## 1. Reading the notation

- **`A <- B`** reads: *A is grounded in B* / *A is produced from B*. Structures are written claim-first, but you can run them in either direction: trace a claim down to its ground, or build from the ground up to the claim.
- **"chain of (X <- X)"** is a finite repeated linkage of X-to-X steps that terminates at an anchor. The author's original word was "cycle" — picture a chain with a clear start that has been *wound into loops*, not a closed circle. There are no closed circles anywhere in this framework; the Law of Non-Circularity (§2) forbids them.

## 2. The core move: grounding and the Law of Non-Circularity

Everything in this framework is driven by one repeatedly observed law:

> **The Law of Non-Circularity** (plain statement: *every kind of thing comes from what it is not*): every kind of thing is observed to originate from something that is not that kind of thing. Chickens from a non-chicken, animals from a non-animal, life from non-life, planets from non-planets, spacetime from non-spacetime. And in reasoning itself: deductions from inductions, inductions from observations, observations from phenomena.

⚠ Do not confuse this with the familiar fallacy rule against "circular arguments" — the classroom rule that an argument may not use its own conclusion as one of its supports ("this book is true because the book says it is"). That rule is a norm about how a single argument may be built; you could follow it perfectly and never notice the Law. The Law of Non-Circularity is an observed inductive law about *origins*, discovered from observation like any other pattern, and it does heavy lifting: it is what licenses every terminus in this framework — the ground under every chain of definitions, the uncaused start of every chain of causes.

Two words in the Law carry weight worth fixing here. A **kind** is individuated by the essential quality of the observed cycle itself: in the chicken-and-egg cycle that quality is reproduction, and the start of that cycle is neither a chicken nor a chicken egg. Re-carving both as "animal matter" does not invalidate the observed cycle — it just names a different pattern. And **observed** means grounded the way anything in this framework is grounded — through signs and chains anyone can run for themselves (§5, *sign*) — not "directly experienced as the ontological occurrence"; demanding the latter is the hyperskeptic move this framework rejects at its root (§8). In the grounded sense, termini are observed constantly: people are born (though people look the same from one day to the next), light has sources (though lighting is ambient), the planet came from dust (though it changes consistently day to day), the first egg came from a non-chicken (despite the chicken-and-egg cycle) — and phenomenological grounding itself is observed directly, despite claims coming from other claims.

Applied to claims: justification cannot be claims all the way down. Every chain terminates in **phenomenological primitives** — things that *just appear or occur* at the most fundamental level: red, loud, hungry, happy, pain, want, 2. Primitives are the ultimate givens. They are not claims — they simply occur — so they cannot be circularly justified and need no justification. They can only be recognized or not. You know a chain has hit bottom when the only thing left to say is "this occurs."

**Why not an infinite regress instead?** A chain where each kind comes from yet another kind, forever, is never what we observe — so an infinite regress lives within the context of speculation: it has no phenomenological grounding. (One could say it is "grounded in imagination" — but note the layer: not imagination as the medium where all thought occurs, but the category of imagination whose correspondence with reality does not matter. At that layer, imagination is independent of what actually exists outside it: that is the scope of fiction.) Several independent observations corroborate the rejection:

- It contradicts the Law itself, which is grounded; the regress is not — so it cannot even be abductively chosen (§7: a groundless rival has no metric by which to prefer it).
- A further discovered law — *all information requires a source* — leaves an infinite regress with no ultimate source for any of its information.
- An infinite regress has the structure of a contradiction: to justify any step, you must appeal to something not itself yet true, and to justify that, the same again, forever. Justification is perpetually deferred and never arrives — the same structure as a method that cannot distinguish truth from falsehood (§7). This covers every target of regress equally — origination or justification — because the structure is the same; in fact, justifying a contradiction is the only observable occurrence of an infinite regress we know of.

And the follow-up "then what caused the uncaused start?" exports temporal causality beyond its scope: at the cosmological terminus our ordinary concept of causality stops projecting, because causality as we know it is fundamentally temporal (§6.3). The question is not deep — it is out of scope, the Newtonian mistake of §6.6.

The author has earlier formal constructions of this grounding — a directed acyclic graph where every concept is defined strictly from previously established concepts with no forward references, and a broader node-tree built for an earlier website draft. Both are prior attempts, now outdated where they differ from this document (§10); this document is the current research layer.

## 3. The grounding chain: from phenomena to claim

Each step below either names something directly observed or narrows an earlier term to a context ("X = Y, within the context of Z"). Nothing is stipulated from nowhere.

1. **Phenomena occur.**
2. **Distinct phenomena occur** — differences. (Distinction is the deepest layer after occurrence itself: qualities reduce to *kinds of distinction*.)
3. **Qualities occur**; associations (felt connections — "red is like blue") are a type of quality. A named category can be both its own node and a type of a prior one — "mental phenomena" is its own node *and* a kind of quality (the "mental" quality); an association is its own node *and* a type of quality (a distinction made *between* qualities). There is no conflict in that.
4. **Will occurs** (a type of quality). **Temporal qualia occur** — "before," "after," "now." **Scope** is a pattern that a set of observations have — a pattern that joins or categorizes observations.
5. **Correlation** is when a phenomenon often follows another. ⚠ Asymmetric and temporal — "A often follows B" — not statistics' symmetric co-variation.
6. **Independence** is when phenomena do not correlate. **Necessity** is when phenomena correlate *without exception*. ⚠ This is exceptionless-so-far observed regularity — not modal "true in all possible worlds" necessity. Reading modal necessity into this word makes the framework claim far more than it does.
7. **Ontological** = whatever is independent of will — *including will itself*, and including whatever is not directly observed. Will here means the wanting itself; what actually *occurs* depends on whatever facilitates the will into reality. A stutterer wills speech and no words come; an amputee wills the arm to move and nothing moves. Thoughts are no different: you can neither decide what your next thought is nor decide whether something is a contradiction. And you cannot will your own willing — you neither willed your will into existence nor can you will it out of existence — so will itself is ontological.
   - *"But then what is NOT ontological?"* The demand assumes something must be excluded, and that framing is wrong: we start by labeling everything we experience as "reality," and that is neither invalid nor inaccurate (§5, *real*). Two different statements hide nearby, and reduction separates them. "The occurrence of will is independent of will" is a direct observation — you can try to will your will's occurrence in and out of existence, and it doesn't move. But the question "is will *dependent* on will?" is different: "dependent" is a layer over "necessary" — roughly, "if you take it away, the effect necessarily changes." Reduce the question and it becomes "if you take will away, does will change?" — a nonsensical question. That is the category mistake, like asking *"who baked the baker?"*
   - What the "ontological events" restriction in step 8 actually narrows is not a willed/unwilled divide among occurrences (nearly everything that occurs is will-independent) but a domain divide the chain itself draws next: step 8's causality ranges over all such events, and step 9 narrows to the mental subdomain. Separately, will does have one genuine dependent: its own aim — the *direction* of the want — which is the will itself, not a second phenomenon that depends on it.
   - *Why will is the bridge:* mind-independence is not inferred from sensory clues — it is directly encountered in every act of willing. Both the wall you cannot walk through and the feather you successfully lift reveal patterns that hold regardless of what you want: you must reach to where the feather *is*, grasp the shape it *has*, lift the weight it *actually* weighs. The author calls these encountered patterns counter-will; mapping them is how the properties of reality are discovered. There is no inferential gap to bridge — the encounter itself is the discovery.
8. **Causality** = necessity, within the context of ontological events. By the Law of Non-Circularity, causal chains start with an uncaused event. ⚠ Causality here *is* exceptionless succession among non-willed events — it is deliberately constructed from correlation. The objection "correlation isn't causation" is a category error against this definition; the difference between them here is precisely the "without exception" and the restriction to the ontological.
9. **Intellectual causality** = causality, within the context of imagination/thoughts. One thought following another without exception — experienced as discovery, not decision. Conclusions *happen to you*: "something clicks inside that I have no control over."
   - *Worked example (the click, and why valid ≠ correct):* Suppose your entire knowledge is "patterns expose structure" and "chickens come from eggs and eggs come from chickens." You can validly infer "time is infinite" — and be wrong, because you haven't yet learned the Law of Non-Circularity, so you haven't realized the first egg came from an animal you wouldn't classify as a chicken, and so on down to spacetime and its origin. The moment someone teaches you that law and your observations hold without counterexample, something clicks that you have no control over — that click is intellectual causality. If you then attempt to deny it and say "no, time must be infinite," the denial contradicts what you now know. Inference from incomplete knowledge can be valid and still incorrect; refinement (§7) is how verdicts track growing knowledge.
   - *Reading that example without drifting scopes:* "was 'time is infinite' true for the earlier person?" mixes two vantage points — the seeker inside the earlier knowledge state, and someone with fuller knowledge observing the seeker. Verdicts belong to a knower (§6.6); the observer's verdict is not floating "objective truth" against the seeker's "subjective truth" — it is another knower's verdict, issued from more knowledge. The two converge as the seeker's knowledge grows, because the Law is an observation anyone can make if they truly seek, and future discoveries corroborate it.
10. **Truth** = intellectual causality, within the context of undeniability. **Falsehood** = intellectual causality, within the context of deniability. (See §5 for deniability, and §4 for how correspondence relates.)
11. **Claim** = a statement with a truth-value.

## 4. The reduction method

A **phenomenological reduction** is an operation, not a separate definition: take a definition and replace each term in it with *that term's* phenomenological definition, and repeat as far as needed. Any term can be reduced. Reductions expose what a term actually commits you to.

*Worked example (author's):* truth = "intellectual causality, within the context of undeniability." Replace "intellectual" with its ground — mental phenomena (a primitive category: phenomena with the "mental" quality) — and "causality" with its definition — "necessity, within the context of ontological events" — giving the intermediate: **"necessity, within the context of undeniable mental events."** One can go further still; each substitution exposes another layer of what "truth" is made of.

The compressed reductions the author has landed on:

- **truth** = a necessary ontological (non-willed) event that is an undeniable imagination.
- **claim** = a set of definitions and their associations carrying a deniability status (undeniable = true claim, deniable = false claim), referring to an ontological event, where the ontological event corresponds with the intellectual event.

**How correspondence relates to truth:** undeniability is truth's defining *context* — it is something you observe, not something you will from your own mind. **Correspondence is a property of truth**: where there is truth, the inferential structure of that truth corresponds with the ontic structure it refers to. Concretely:

- A **structure** is a set of associated ontological events.
- An **inferential structure** is a set of associated inferences — *a logical chain*.
- An **ontic structure** is a set of associated ontological events — *a causal chain*.
- *Example:* the logical chain "I see rain and feel wet, therefore the rain is making me wet" corresponds with the causal chain "rain makes skin wet." The causal chain can be held at whatever level you currently have knowledge of — skin-level, tissue, molecular, quantum — correspondence is not hostage to the deepest level (§6.3).
- Note that *both* chains live internally: saying "rain makes skin wet" does not cause rain anywhere — the ontic structure, as held and tested by a knower, is that knower's reconstruction of the causal chain, built from and answerable to observation.

So the framework does not have three competing definitions of truth — it has one definition (via undeniability), one property (correspondence), and reductions of both.

## 5. Definitions register

⚠-marked entries collide with a standard sense; the document's sense governs.

- **Statement** — a set of definitions + their associations.
- **Definition** — anything that references something specific: a concept, a term, a label. ⚠ Not a dictionary entry or sentence-level explanatory act. The meaning pipeline's "chain of definitions" is a chain of referring units, not a regress of dictionary entries.
- **Meaning** — information with context. Information without context is meaningless: "apple," by itself, means only *imagine an apple*; "that's an apple" carries context — identifying the what at that location. Meaning also fails when a claim joins **incompatible scopes** — applying a term whose use requires knowledge from one scope to a referent living in a scope where that knowledge does not exist (worked example in §6.2). (Earlier formulations: meaningful = information constrained within a known context; = placement within a hierarchy.)
- **Event** — a transition from not-occurring to occurring. An **ontological event** is such a transition independent of will.
- **Deniability** — whether or not denying something results in an inconsistency with what you currently know — the internal occurrence of "knowing you're wrong." The test: *based on what you know now, does denying this contradict anything?* "Deniability" names the category; **deniable** and **undeniable** are its two statuses. ⚠ NOT the social ability to openly deny something, and NOT "undeniable = obvious to everyone."
  - **What "inconsistency" is, reduced:** "contradiction" is a kind of "conflict" (conflict within the scope of logic); both reduce to "inconsistency" within their own contexts; and inconsistency reduces to **correlation and pattern-recognition** — which are more phenomenologically primitive. So an inconsistency is not restricted to formal contradiction: recognizing that something breaks a grounded, established pattern is the same occurrence at a different layer.
  - **Validly deniable**: a claim is *validly* deniable when the denial is itself produced by intellectual causality from grounded knowledge — a counterexample, an observed pattern-break, an inconsistency — as opposed to a merely speculative denial ("maybe not"), which rule 4 excludes. Speculative denial does not make a claim deniable.
- **Truth-value** — the deniability status of a claim, equivalently whether the claim's inferential structure corresponds with the ontic structure it refers to (corresponds = truth, does not = falsehood).
- **Proof / justification** — a sequence of claims in an inferential structure. ⚠ Not a conclusive derivation: a proof can be ungrounded, invalid, and unsound and still be *a proof* — grounded/valid/sound are separate gradings, below.
- **Conclusion** — the final step of a proof.
- **Grounded** — when a proof's first step is phenomenological. The first step of a grounded proof is an observation-claim whose content is a primitive occurrence — e.g. "I see red," "I feel wet" — which is undeniable while the phenomenon occurs, since denying it contradicts the occurrence itself. (This is how a sequence of claims bottoms out in non-claims: the primitive is not a step in the proof; the report of it is.)
- **Valid** — when the link between a proof's conclusion and its previous steps occurs by intellectual causality. ⚠ Shifted from the textbook sense (whole-argument form-validity).
- **Sound** — when every link in a proof occurs by intellectual causality. ⚠ The textbook "sound" bundles in premise-truth; here, premise quality is handled separately by *grounded*. Grounded + sound together do the work the textbook pair does. (⚠ Note: the author's earlier website tree uses "sound" in the near-textbook sense — "a deduction is sound when its premises are true." This document's sense governs here; see §10, divergences.)
- **Faithfulness** — whether a communicative structure corresponds with the inferential/ontic structures it refers to. The communication-layer analogue of truth-value; its two statuses are **faithful** and **unfaithful**.
- **Comprehensibility** — whether a statement is imaginable at all, regardless of observed context: "pink elephant" is imaginable even though elephants aren't pink. Positive form **coherence**, negative **incoherence**. Bounded by the limits of imagination, not by observed reality.
- **Rationality** — whether a statement's definitions are comprehensible *within their contexts*, taken from the precedent of reality: "pink elephant" is irrational because elephants aren't pink. ⚠ Not an accusation of bad reasoning by an agent — it is an empirical-fit property of statement content, and it is revisable: refinement updates rational/irrational verdicts on new evidence.
- **Real / reality** — all that resists your will or is independent of it (including will itself, as you can't will not to will). We first observe basic things — red, anger, 2 — and "reality" names all of that. Discovering dreams and hallucinations later does not reveal "non-reality"; those are categorizations *within* reality. ⚠ So "illusions are real" is a theorem here, not a confusion — the apparent paradox comes from semantically drifting "real" between its universal sense and its narrower "not-a-dream / what everyone corroborates" sense. If you catch yourself objecting "but illusions aren't real," you have switched senses mid-argument.
- **Sign / support** — something that points to a claim, available for anyone to observe *for themselves*. ⚠ All phenomenological primitives — the basis of all knowledge — are first-person; there is no "public proof" over and above each person's own access. When we see someone else in pain we do not feel their pain: we *reconstruct their experience* internally, as direct knowledge, from what they show and communicate. "Anyone can observe it" therefore means: anyone can run the same chain for themselves, including through experience-reconstruction from another's communication — not that the sign exists in some third-person space no one actually occupies. The pointing is the proof pipeline's business; the *licensing* of the pointing is the inferential pipeline's business (§6.4–6.5).
- **Reason** — a sequence of intellectual causality: one does not decide whether something results in truth or falsehood, one discovers it.
- **Scientific method** (as this framework reads it): Observation → Definition → Induction → Deduction → Refinement. That is: grounding in reality/established knowledge → something identifiable with clear scope → identifying the pattern behind the defined scopes → applying observational precedent to the patterns → refining scopes from new information.

## 6. The seven pipelines

A claim's full structure decomposes into seven analyses. Each answers one question about the claim and produces one output. The pipelines supply *structure*; the analyst fills them with their actual empirical knowledge of the subject matter — the framework does not contain the world's content. The listed order is a dependency order — ontology's causes operate within scope's output, truth consumes all prior outputs — so run them in order when doing a full analysis.

### 6.1 Meaning — *what does the claim say?*

> `claim (the 'what') <- chain of (definition <- definition) <- phenomenological primitive`

Unpack each referring unit in the claim into the definitions it depends on, down to primitives. **Output:** what the claim is talking about — what to imagine.

*Example:* "I see rain" unpacks toward "I see shapes, going down rapidly, that are a kind of color and texture, which I label 'rain'" — and shapes, motion, color, texture bottom out as primitives.

### 6.2 Scope / reference — *what part of reality is the claim about?*

> `claim (its scope) <- chain of (definition scope <- pattern identification <- ...) <- phenomenological primitives`

A scope is a pattern joining observations, and it fixes what is admissible downstream: which causes count (6.3) and which deniability tests apply (6.6). **Output:** the part of reality under discussion — the pattern/domain the claim lives in.

*Example 1 (author's — basis fixes the verdict's form):* "There is microbial life somewhere outside Earth." Everything hangs on the basis you choose. If your knowledge of other planets comes from statistics — atmospheric analysis, gravitational effects, all secondhand — then the claim's scope is already one of *probability*, not certainty, and the analysis proceeds statistically at every layer. If instead you insist on the basis "I want to observe directly," that is currently impossible — and on that basis the claim is *meaningless* (information without an available context). The scope is not decoration; it determines what kind of verdict can exist at all.

*Example 2 (author's — incompatible scopes dissolve fake "undetermined" claims):* "The number of stars is even." Establish meaning and scope first: "the number of stars" refers either to *the number we know now* or to *the actual amount that exists*. Knowing "odd" or "even" requires the full count of a thing (or some proof otherwise). If the meaning is "the number we know now," the scopes are compatible and the claim analyzes normally. If the meaning is "the actual amount," that count is not currently known — so applying "odd/even" to it joins incompatible scopes, and the result is a *meaningless meaning*. The claim never reaches the truth pipeline; no third truth status ("undetermined") is needed, because the failure happened upstream.

*Basis selection:* a basis must be *available* (a currently-impossible basis yields meaninglessness, as above) and *compatible* with the claim's meaning. Among available compatible bases, the claim's own generality selects — a general claim ("regular exercise improves mood") lives primarily in the statistical scope; a first-person claim in the direct scope. Two analysts on different bases do not contradict each other: their verdicts are indexed to different scopes (§0 rule 6). What §8 warns against is not choosing the direct-observation basis — it is *demanding it as the only admissible basis* and then declaring claims on every other basis illegitimate.

*Ambiguity is not dissolution:* if a claim's meaning resolves into two or more clear scopes (as with "time is absolute"), split it and run the remaining pipelines per scope, delivering a verdict for each. Dissolution is reserved for meaninglessness — an unavailable basis or genuinely incompatible scopes — not for ambiguity the analyst has already resolved. Declaring an upstream stop and then issuing downstream verdicts anyway is a procedure error: either the claim stops, or it runs.

### 6.3 Ontology — *what has to happen in reality for this claim to occur?*

> `claim (its known ontological conditions) <- ontological event (that it references) <- chain of (cause <- cause) <- intrinsic cause`

"Cause" operates within the scope fixed in 6.2, and the chain is held at *whatever level you currently have knowledge of* — it does not owe anyone the deepest level. **Output:** a fit (or misfit) between the claim's referenced event and the known ontological conditions for such events.

**The terminus — intrinsic cause — comes in two kinds, and both bottom out the same way:**

- *The discrete kind:* the actual "how" between any two ontological occurrences. Water causes plants to grow — how? Water molecules are absorbed by root molecules. How? The way atoms of one molecule interact with another. How? The way sub-atomic particles interact. How? The way quantum behaviors interact. How? — and here every chain of "how" bottoms out at ignorance. (Same with two magnets pulling toward each other: field, spin alignment, a quantum property, and then — nobody knows.) No level ever identifies the intrinsic "how" of how anything happens; each level only re-describes effects at finer grain. Whatever the actual how is, it cannot itself be the kind of thing that is caused.
- *The cosmological kind:* the origin of the entire chain — chickens from non-chickens, life from non-life, spacetime from non-spacetime — where our ordinary concept of causality stops projecting, because causality as we know it is fundamentally temporal.

Both kinds answer "how does anything happen?" the same way, by the Law of Non-Circularity: **something that is not itself caused.**

### 6.4 Proof — *what points to the claim?*

> `claim (proof) <- chain of (sign/support <- sign/support) <- phenomenological primitives`

Signs are available for anyone to observe for themselves (in the first-person sense fixed in §5). Each sign has its own grounding chain down to primitives. **Output:** the claim's external justification, available for anyone to check.

*Example (author's):* Claim: "I am wet." A sign: "it is raining right now." That sign has its own chain: "I see rain" ← "I see clouds" ← "I see rain coming from clouds" — each bottoming out phenomenologically (shapes, falling motion, color, texture).

### 6.5 Inferential — *what makes those signs actually lead to the claim?*

> `claim <- inferences <- chain of (rule of inference <- rule of inference) <- logical/intellectual causality`

**This is a different chain from the proof chain** — proof is the *what points*, inferential is the *how it validly points*. The inference from "it is raining" to "the rain is why I feel wet" is licensed by rules of inference — e.g., "correlation without exception is causation," applied to the observed 100% correlation between falling rain and the feeling of wetness. The rules themselves have reasons, which bottom out in a specific kind of phenomenological observation (naming that kind precisely is an open area, §9). Both chains contribute to one higher structure of the claim — how exactly the pipelines organize into that higher structure is an open area (§9). **Output:** the claim's internal/logical justification.

Abductive steps belong here too: an abductive preference is itself an instance of intellectual causality (the preference *comes to you*; you don't own your thoughts), and the honest inquirer eventually discovers the rules of abduction the way rules of inference are discovered — grounded in a special kind of observation. (Those rules are not yet fully worked out — §9.)

### 6.6 Truth — *what is the claim's truth-value?*

> `claim (its truth-value) <- deniability (known ontological conditions) & (deniability [inferential proof] <- deniability [proof]) <- deniability (scope) <- deniability (meaning)`

(The formula is preserved from the author's original notes; the plain reading below governs.) Plain reading: **run the deniability test on every prior pipeline's output.** Is the meaning deniable? The scope? The proof? The inference from the proof? The fit with known ontological conditions? Every step of this pipeline is a deniability context. If all come out undeniable — given current knowledge, within the fixed scope — the claim is true *there*. If any step is validly deniable (§5), the claim is not established as truth — and presenting it as truth anyway is a compass violation (§7). **Output:** the claim's truth-value, always indexed to scope and current knowledge.

**Whose knowledge?** The verdict-giver's. Two honest inquirers with different knowledge can reach different verdicts on the same scoped claim — *because they have different knowledge*, and neither is the all-knowing, all-just knower who alone could issue the final verdict. That is not relativism; it is the reason for the whole endeavor: in a world where you can seek knowledge and reconstruct others' experiences through communication, honest inquirers **converge** — the same clicks (intellectual causality) occur in anyone who reaches the same knowledge, so after enough seeking and communicating, we should get the same answers. A verdict is a waypoint of that convergence, not a private possession.

**For probabilistic scopes** the verdict lives at the statistical level — undeniability applies layer by layer (to the statistics, to the physics, to each scope layer, down to the claim), exactly as in the microbial-life example. The claim is held *at* its scope — "for most people, on average" — and receives a binary verdict there; the verdict's form is dictated by the basis chosen in 6.2, and exporting the in-scope verdict to a stronger scope (e.g. exceptionless-universal) is the violation below, not a refinement of it.

*The Newtonian rule:* the existence of relativity does not make "time is absolute within Newtonian physics" false — it is true within that scope, and only "wrong" if you claim the scope is the intrinsic nature of the world. Exporting a claim beyond its scope produces wrong answers, not refutations.

### 6.7 Communication — *how to communicate the claim faithfully?*

> `claim (communication) <- demystification (word choice, selection, etc.) <- audience knowledge & audience disposition & audience language <- (human) psychological nature`

**Output:** a proper, faithful understanding of the claim in the audience — the received picture corresponds with the inferential/ontic structures the claim actually carries, no more and no less. Faithfulness has two sides: the communicating side (does the message correspond to the structures? — §5) and the receiving side (does the listener's reconstruction correspond?). A faithful message poorly reconstructed is not a contradiction — the two sides are graded separately.

**Demystification, operationalized** (from the author's writing-goal work): use plain, common language and keep cognitive load low, while staying precise enough that a specialist would agree it's accurate. Be hard on the topic, soft on the reader: anticipate where someone might feel defensive, but don't soften what's actually true. Where something is uncertain or contested, surface that directly in plain terms. Five drifts to avoid:

1. *Academic drift* — Latinate words, nominalizations, or jargon where a common word works.
2. *Bland drift* — hedging or both-sidesing until no real claim is made. (The communication-layer twin of §0 rule 5.)
3. *False accessibility* — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would object to.
4. *Emotional cushioning* that buries the actual point.
5. *Weight drift* — a phrase that does more work than the point requires, so the reader spends effort deciding which reading was meant.

Between joined clauses, make the relationship explicit ("but," "instead," "so") rather than making the reader infer it. Self-check: a smart non-expert should understand it on first read; a specialist should not wince; no sentence should hedge without saying anything. If accessibility and precision conflict in a sentence, flag the sentence rather than silently picking one.

When communicating this framework's own content, the specialist judges by whichever senses are better grounded. If their objection rests on framing the agnostic/hyperskeptic default as neutral, the grounding problem is theirs to fix first (§8); otherwise, they can work from this document's definitions as the starting point.

*Live example of the failure mode:* an AI once discussed this framework using "rung" (its own invented metaphor) and "genus" (its own jargon) as if the author shared those labels, and paraphrased the author's own notation into a description the author couldn't recognize. Faithful communication uses the audience's/author's vocabulary or explicitly defines new terms at first use — rule 2 of §0.

## 7. Conditions on truth — the compass rules

- **Deniability at every step.** Every node of the truth pipeline is a deniability context. If something is validly deniable, presenting it as established truth is the compass failure below.
- **Discriminative power.** A justification that would equally confirm a falsehood confirms nothing — like a clock whose hands point to all times (it doesn't tell the right time; it points at true and false times alike), a smoke detector that beeps with no smoke, or a compass with a painted needle. The threat is not only falsehood but *truth mixed with falsehood*: a method that certifies both is worthless even when its output happens to be true. (This does not deny that false outputs can come from false inputs — it is about the method's ability to distinguish.)
- **Speculation is not proof or refutation — derivation:** suppose the opposite, that speculating X counts as proof or refutation. Then speculating the opposite of X is an equally valid inference, undermining X — and by the same token undermining anything true. A standard that confirms and refutes everything equally is the painted-needle compass: it cannot distinguish truth from falsehood, so it fails the discriminative-power condition. Therefore speculation is not proof or refutation.
- **Ignorance is never the output of abduction.** Where abduction applies, the exercise is to identify the metric by which one stance among rivals is preferred and apply it. Returning the prior state of ignorance as the "conclusion" — "we just can't know" — treats the starting point as the result and abandons the exercise while appearing to complete it. An honest interim report — "the named metrics currently tie; no stance is yet preferred" — is not a violation: it is a current state of an ongoing search, not a terminus dressed as a conclusion. The violation is stopping there and calling it the answer. A rival with no grounding at all is not yet a candidate for this exercise — there is nothing for a metric to weigh (this is why the infinite regress in §2 cannot be abductively chosen).
- **Hard problems are limits of knowledge, not refutations.** Every framework has them — including physicalism (the intrinsic nature of causality, energy, of reality itself). Since knowledge is scope-indexed, an unanswered "what is it intrinsically?" marks a boundary, not a defeat — and it cannot be selectively fatal to rivals while being tolerated at home.
- **On "how many observations is enough":** the framework sets no numeric threshold for when correlation has been observed "often" enough, or exceptionlessly enough, to ground necessity. This is not a claim that every specific correlation out there is automatically well-observed — a thin correlation is handled by the ordinary machinery: keep observing, seek counterexamples, refine. The point is narrower: the load-bearing primitive observations behind this framework's own groundings are met many times over, because a human life contains an inordinate number of observations per day, plus the experience-reconstructions gained from others' communication. The hypothetical person with no senses at all, who could never accumulate those observations, is outside this endeavor's scope — their inability to experience even one successive chain does not invalidate the chains we have observed. Demanding that the framework first solve that case is a deflection.
- **Refinement.** Verdicts are indexed to current knowledge and update on new evidence or newly considered evidence. Nothing above implies that a present verdict is immune to revision — see the chicken/egg/time example in §3.9.

## 8. On the "neutral default"

The common default stance — agnostic, physicalist, hyperskeptical — is a *rival position*, not a neutral baseline, and treating it as neutral is itself the distortion this section names. (The three labels travel together as one practical posture — suspend judgment by default, presuppose some model of the physical as the entirety of reality, demand direct observation — and each bullet below targets that posture where it applies. Note the ambiguity in "physical" between *tangible* and *real*: treating tangible things as real is not the problem — everything experienced is real, §5 — the problem is presupposing that a model of the physical exhausts reality.)

- It has rivals; in the absence of actual reasoning, no one of them is "the" default.
- Its hard problems are real too (intrinsic nature of reality, causality, energy). Pointing at a rival's hard problem while ignoring one's own is a double standard — rule 8 of §0 requires flagging it.
- "Methodological naturalism" sounds neutral — "when doing science, look for natural mechanisms" — but ask what "natural" means *intrinsically* and it usually unpacks to "what happens passively," which is metaphysical physicalism restated. A stance is not neutral because it is unstated.
- The illusion-vs-reality puzzle ("how do we know anything is real?") starts from a model instead of observation, then discovers the model can't be checked. Start from observation instead (§5, *real*) and the puzzle dissolves: illusions are categorizations within reality, and the word "real" was drifting between senses.
- None of this says physicalism is false, or that bias makes a view false. It says: make the stance explicit and apply every standard uniformly. An AI reading this document should notice when its default framing quietly assumes the "neutral" stance — e.g., demanding direct observation as the only admissible basis (that is one basis among several, per §6.2), or treating conceivable alternatives as refutations (§7).
