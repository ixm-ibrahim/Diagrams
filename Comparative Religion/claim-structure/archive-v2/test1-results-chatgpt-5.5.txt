"I am wet, because it is raining."

I’ll treat the sentence as one claim with an embedded cause: “I am wet” plus “the rain is why.” I’ll stop if an upstream pipeline breaks instead of pushing it into truth.

Thought for 27s

Claim: “I am wet, because it is raining.”

6.1 Meaning — what does the claim say?

Output: The claim says: the speaker is in a wet state, and the cause of that wet state is current rain.

Reduced plainly:

I am wet = the speaker’s body/clothes/skin has the wetness quality.
because it is raining = falling rain is being offered as the cause of that wetness.

This is meaningful. The definitions are imaginable and do not join incompatible scopes.

6.2 Scope / reference — what part of reality is the claim about?

Output: The claim lives in a first-person, present-tense, local weather/body-condition scope.

It is not about rain in general. It is about this speaker, now, being wet because of this rain. Since the framework says scope fixes what counts downstream, the needed scope is not merely “rain exists somewhere,” but “rain is contacting the speaker or their clothes.”

6.3 Ontology — what has to happen in reality for this claim to occur?

Output: Ontological fit is possible, but only with a missing condition.

For the claim to occur as stated:

speaker becomes wet <- rainwater contacts speaker/clothes <- rain falls in the speaker’s location <- weather conditions produce rain

Rain can cause wetness. But rain only explains the speaker’s wetness if rainwater reaches the speaker. If the speaker is inside, under a roof, in a car, or using an umbrella, “it is raining” can occur while “I am wet because of rain” does not occur.

So ontology gives a conditional fit: the claim fits known conditions only if rain-contact is included.

6.4 Proof — what points to the claim?

Output: The stated proof is: “it is raining.”

That is a sign for possible wetness, but by itself it is not enough. The stronger proof would be something like:

I feel wet + I see/feel rain hitting me + my wetness began with that contact

The document itself separates proof as “what points” from inference as “how it validly points,” and gives rain as a possible sign for “I am wet.” But here the sign is incomplete because the contact step is not stated.

6.5 Inferential — what makes those signs actually lead to the claim?

Output: The inference fails as written.

The needed inference is:

It is raining → rain is contacting me → rain is making me wet

But the sentence only gives the first step. “It is raining” does not, by itself, validly lead to “I am wet,” because the same support could also fit a falsehood:

It is raining, and I am not wet because I am indoors.

That is a rule 5 / compass problem: the stated support would equally confirm cases where the claimed cause is absent. A painted-needle support confirms too much, so it confirms nothing at the needed strength.

Stop point

The claim does not reach the truth pipeline as stated, because it fails in the inferential pipeline. I do not assign true, false, or “undetermined.”

Verdict

Verdict: Within the present, first-person rain/wetness scope, indexed to current knowledge, the claim is not established through the framework as stated, because “it is raining” does not validly lead to “I am wet because of the rain” unless the missing rain-contact condition is added.

the spacetime/cosmological chain began from something uncaused

It is not about ordinary causes inside time, like fire causing smoke. It is about the origin of the whole causal chain where ordinary temporal causality stops projecting. The document says the cosmological terminus is where chains like “spacetime from non-spacetime” bottom out, and where asking “what caused the uncaused start?” exports causality beyond its scope.

spacetime / cosmological chain <- non-spacetime origin <- uncaused start

every traced kind originates from what it is not → spacetime is a kind → spacetime originates from non-spacetime → the causal chain cannot be circular or infinite → the origin bottoms out in something uncaused

Thought for 10s
Claim: “Miracles are impossible — they’d violate the laws of nature.”
6.1 Meaning — what does the claim say?

Output: The claim says that miracles cannot occur because they would break the regular patterns we call “laws of nature.”

Since the document does not define “miracle,” I have to define it plainly for this analysis:

Miracle = an unusual ontological event that does not fit the ordinary causal pattern we expect from nature, often because a non-ordinary cause is being claimed.

“Laws of nature” must be read through the document’s terms as observed necessities: exceptionless-so-far patterns among ontological events, not modal rules that are true in every possible world. The document defines necessity as exceptionless observed regularity, and causality as necessity within ontological events.

So the claim is meaningful if it means:

miracles cannot occur <- miracles would break observed ontological regularities

6.2 Scope / reference — what part of reality is the claim about?

Output: The claim has a scope problem.

“Laws of nature” live inside the scope of observed natural patterns. But “miracle” refers to a claimed event where the ordinary pattern may not be the whole causal story.

So the claim tries to use this scope:

ordinary observed natural regularities

to rule out this stronger scope:

all possible ontological events, including events with non-ordinary causes

That is a scope jump. The document warns against this kind of move through the Newtonian rule: a claim can be true inside its model-scope, but exporting it beyond that scope produces wrong answers.

6.3 Ontology — what has to happen in reality for this claim to occur?

Output: Ontology does not support the claim as stated.

For the claim to work, the ontological chain would need to be:

miracles impossible <- all ontological events are exhausted by ordinary natural regularities

But the claim does not establish that. It only says miracles would violate the laws of nature.

That is not enough inside this framework, because a broken expected pattern can mean at least two different things:

the event is impossible

or

the scope/model of the law was incomplete

The second possibility matters because the document treats laws and verdicts as scope-indexed and revisable by refinement. A regularity holding in one scope does not automatically prove nothing outside that scope can occur.

6.4 Proof — what points to the claim?

Output: The stated proof is:

miracles would violate the laws of nature

But this proof assumes the thing it needs to prove: that the laws of nature, as currently known, exhaust what can occur.

It gives no independent sign that miracles are impossible. It only restates that miracles do not fit ordinary patterns.

6.5 Inferential — what makes those signs actually lead to the claim?

Output: The inference fails.

The attempted inference is:

miracles do not fit ordinary natural regularities
→ therefore miracles are impossible

That does not validly follow.

The same support would also fit a different claim:

miracles do not fit ordinary natural regularities
→ therefore, if a miracle occurred, our scope of natural regularity was not the whole causal picture

So the support does not discriminate. It would equally confirm a falsehood if the falsehood is: “anything outside our current model is impossible.” That is a compass failure under the document’s rule that a justification confirming falsehood confirms nothing.

Stop point

The claim fails at 6.5 Inferential, with a scope problem already visible in 6.2.

It does not reach the truth pipeline. I do not call it true, false, or “undetermined.”

Verdict

Verdict: Within the scope of ordinary observed natural regularities, indexed to current knowledge, the claim is not established through the framework. “This would violate the laws of nature” does not validly lead to “this is impossible,” because that exports ordinary natural laws beyond their established scope.

"The Quran has been preserved unchanged since its revelation."

I’ll separate the claim’s possible scopes first, because “Quran,” “preserved,” and “unchanged” can point to different things. Then I’ll use current historical/manuscript evidence only inside the scope it actually supports.

Thought for 50s
Claim: “The Quran has been preserved unchanged since its revelation.”
6.1 Meaning — what does the claim say?

Output: The claim says that the Quran today is the same as it was when revealed.

But the claim hides two meanings that must be fixed:

“The Quran” could mean the Uthmanic consonantal skeleton, one modern printed reading like Ḥafṣ, the full set of canonical qirāʾāt, or the exact original revealed recitation in every letter/vowel/reading.

“Unchanged” could mean no change in core message, no change in surah/ayah content, no change in consonantal text, or no change at all down to every reading and vocalization.

So the sentence is imaginable, but its meaning is not yet stable.

6.2 Scope / reference — what part of reality is the claim about?

Output: The claim fails here as written.

The document says scope fixes what can be tested downstream, and a claim cannot move forward if it joins or shifts scopes without naming them. Here, different scopes produce different possible verdicts:

Scope A: Uthmanic consonantal text from the mid-7th century onward.
This has strong support. The Islamic tradition places standardization under ʿUthmān, and academic discussion treats the Uthmanic rasm as an early standard text; Sinai describes the tradition as crediting ʿUthmān with promulgating a uniform consonantal skeleton, while his article argues against dating major closure as late as around 700 CE. The Birmingham fragments are also very early, with parchment dated to 568–645 CE at 95.4% probability, close to the Prophet Muhammad’s lifetime and the first caliphs.

Scope B: exact unchanged wording across every early textual tradition and every recited form.
This scope is not supported as stated. The Ṣanʿāʾ palimpsest lower text is described by Sadeghi and Goudarzi as a non-Uthmanic textual tradition beside the standard Uthmanic one. They also say the lower tradition and the Uthmanic tradition must have diverged before the spread of the Uthmanic tradition in the mid-7th century. The qirāʾāt also include differences in pronunciation, vowelization, letters, and sometimes meaning, depending on the reading.

So the claim’s support changes depending on what “preserved unchanged” means. Evidence for early preservation of the Uthmanic text does not equally prove exact unchangedness across every textual/recitational layer. Treating it that way would confirm too much, so it triggers the document’s compass rule: support that would equally confirm a falsehood confirms nothing at that stronger scope.

Stop point

The claim stops at 6.2 Scope / reference.

It does not reach ontology, proof, inference, or truth as one stable claim. I do not force it to true, false, or “undetermined.”

Verdict

Verdict: As written, the claim is under-scoped. Within the narrower scope of the Uthmanic consonantal text from the mid-7th century onward, current evidence strongly supports preservation. Within the broader scope of exact unchangedness across every letter, vowel, reading, and early textual tradition since revelation, the claim is not licensed by that evidence and is validly deniable. This verdict is indexed to current knowledge.
