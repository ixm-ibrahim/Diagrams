## The worked example — thread 1's sealed result (2026-07-31), verbatim

THE RULE (final wording): "A method of reasoning that justifies falsehood can't distinguish what is true from what is not." (The author's original sentence with one repair: the pointing-word "it" removed — the sole defect that allowed an empty one-claim reading.)

COMPANION SENTENCE: "Even when such a method's declarations happen to be true, they were not produced by what makes them true — so it never knows."

TRANSLATION LINES (travel with the rule; translation, not rule):
- "Justifies falsehood" = declares true a claim that what reached the reasoner does not force — judged by what had reached him AT THE TIME.
- A declaration counts only after the method's own checks have finished.
- What no observation could ever bear on is not a claim at all; the rule is silent about it — turned away at the door (the category mistake).
- "Can't distinguish what is true from what is not" = the incapacity is general: none of the method's declarations, right or wrong, were produced by checking what the evidence forces — none carry knowing.

STATUS: the system's first theorem. Not an ordinary testable claim — given the definitions it cannot be false. Its payload is the license it grants: one clean bad declaration convicts every declaration produced the same way, including the correct ones, demoting them from knowing to merely matching.

HIS TEN RATIFIED RULINGS (2026-07-30/31):
1. Method = the repeated way declarations are actually produced — not the person, not one day. One run still counts; a bad move can't hide among unlike good ones.
2. Damage spreads exactly as far as sameness-of-way: when a declaration is caught bad, every declaration produced the same way loses standing — no more, no less. The astrologer's star-leaps all burn; his careful gardening advice doesn't.
3. Justifies = concludes it and holds it as true. Caught by your own checks before it stood = never justified. Still holding after new evidence arrives = justifying it anew, today.
4. The trigger: declaring true what what-reached-you doesn't force — leaps onto lucky guesses included. Person and method split: the method is broken regardless; the person is at fault only if the insufficiency was within his reach.
5. What reached you: shrugging at evidence you were shown doesn't remove it; deliberately avoiding it doesn't either; honest ignorance and unwilled non-arrival excuse.
6. Forced = by the observations, again and again without exception — never by how automatic a habit feels.
7. No honest error: whoever was wrong either leapt or ignored something. Old declarations stay judged by their own time's evidence forever; the offense is re-declaring after the evidence has widened.
8. Hedged declarations are real claims about rates ("probably works" = "works more often than chance") and can be genuinely established.
9. Meaningful = concept + context. Concept = a definition (a bundle of qualities and associations). Context = the observations the statement is about. No-context statements are never claims.
10. Reform replaces, never acquits: adding a real check creates a new method with a fresh record; the old declarations stay worthless.

Note especially ruling 9 — it is a meaning ruling, and your question is the meaning question asked of "claim" itself.

## The author's seven-part breakdown (his words, verbatim, 2026-07-07; enters as his earlier attempt, not as the answer key)

"It basically broke it down into 7 pipelines: meaning (what does the claim say? output: what the claim is talking about, like what to imagine), scope/reference (what is the claim about, like what scope does the claim live under? output: the part of reality we're talking about), ontology (what has to happen in reality to make this claim occur? output: a fit with the known ontological conditions for the claim), proof (what justifies/points to the claim? output: the claim's external justification/proof, for anyone to observe for themselves), inferential (what allows the proof to lead to the claim? output: the claim's internal/logical justification/proof), truth (what makes the claim true? output: the claim's truth-value), and communication (how to faithfully communicate the claim? output: a proper, faithful understanding of the claim)."

What each structure is made of (his words):

1. "Meaning: 'claim (the "what") <- cycle of (definition <- definition) <- phenomenological primitive' (where 'definition' encompasses concepts, terms, etc. anything that references something specific)"
2. "Scope: 'claim (its scope) <- cycle of (definition scope <- pattern identification <- ...) <- phenomenological primitives' (as a 'scope', phenomenologically, is a pattern that joins/categorizes observations)"
3. "Ontological: 'claim (its known ontological conditions) <- ontological event (that it references) <- cycle of (cause <- cause) <- intrinsic cause' (note that 'cause' occurs within the scope defined earlier)"
4. "Proof: 'claim (proof) <- cycle of (sign/support <- sign/support) <- phenomenological primitives' (note that I use 'sign' here because signs 'point to' a claim, and the actual logic is the rules of inference between them - ex. I feel wet, I ask 'what points to that claim?' or 'why should I believe that?', then I see that rain is occurring, and the droplets are corresponding to the feeling I get - and so some rule of inference allows me to go from seeing the rain to realizing that it's the reason for why I'm wet)"
5. "Inferential: 'claim <- inferences <- cycle of (rule of inference <- rule of inference) <- logical/intellectual causality'"
6. "Truth: 'claim (its truth-value) <- deniability (known ontological conditions) & (deniability [inferential proof] <- deniability [proof]) <- deniability (scope) <- deniability (meaning)' (basically applying deniability with all the previous outputs)"
7. "Communication: 'claim (communication) <- demystification (word choice, selection, etc.) <- audience knowledge & audience disposition & audience language <- (human) psychological nature'?"
