**What a claim is made of — thread 2's record, with his correction applied:**

A claim's parts: **the concept** (singular — all the claim's definitions and their associations together; his correction: "the 'ties' is already a part of the 'concepts', like the claim has a singular 'concept' that encompasses all its definitions and associations"), **its context** (his definition: "context = the scope of observations the statement is about" — "it will rain tomorrow" is a claim today, its context being the observed weather pattern whose scope reaches tomorrow), and **a truth-value it sides with** (the statement says "this is so" — what separates "the kettle is hot" from "is the kettle hot?"). Making a claim is not an assessment step; the claim arrives whole, and all assessment runs on a made claim.

Assessment runs in three sets, eight pipelines (3 + 4 + 1): the meaningfulness set (concept, context; result: meaningfulness), the truth set gated on meaningfulness passing (ontology, proof/signs/evidence, inferential; result: truth via deniability), the faithfulness set gated on nothing (communication). Three ways assessment fails, one per set: meaningless (no concept — "blork is hungry"; no context; contexts that don't associate — "seven is hungry"), false (pointed and missed; a falsehood always has an "instead"), unfaithful (the claim is fine; the listener received a different one). His ruling: meaningless is a SIBLING of falsehood, never its child — folding it into falsehood either mints truths from nonsense ("seven is not hungry" would come out true) or makes a statement and its denial both false. His grounding rule: the meaningfulness verdicts are themselves claims — "hungry's context and seven's context don't associate" is a true, deniable claim, and its truth is what grounds the meaninglessness.
