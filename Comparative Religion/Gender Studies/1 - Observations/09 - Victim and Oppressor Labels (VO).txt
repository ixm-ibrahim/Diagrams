--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VICTIM AND OPPRESSOR LABELS (VO)
How the words "victim" and "oppressor" (and their softer stand-ins) get
attached to people, and how the attachment is used socially and
politically.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

VO1. "Victim" is applied asymmetrically for comparable harms. A man
     hit by a female partner is less likely to be described as "a
     victim of domestic abuse" in casual conversation or mainstream
     media than a woman hit by a male partner in equivalent
     circumstances. Same physical event, different likelihood of the
     victim label.

VO2. "Oppressor" and its softer forms ("privileged," "part of the
     problem," "the dominant class") are commonly assigned by group
     membership, not by individual conduct. A man who has personally
     harmed no one is still describable as part of the oppressor
     class on the basis of being a man. A woman who has personally
     behaved cruelly to multiple people is not routinely described as
     an oppressor on the basis of her conduct — the oppressor label
     is almost never applied to her as an individual.

VO3. Standing to speak on a topic is often indexed to victim status
     on that topic. A woman's view on sexism is treated as carrying
     greater evidential weight than a man's — she is seen as the
     person with direct experience. The same logic is not applied in
     the domains where men do worse (custody outcomes, conscription,
     workplace deaths, suicide rate): a man's view on those domains
     is not treated as carrying extra evidential weight, because men
     are not widely seen as a victim class there.

VO4. Claiming victim status publicly is a socially and politically
     valuable position. It attracts sympathy, grants evidential weight
     in disputes, unlocks institutional resources and protections,
     and gives the speaker rhetorical high ground. Having oppressor
     status applied to you is the inverse: loss of standing to speak,
     reversed burden of proof, pressure to apologize. The incentive
     gradient pushes speakers to frame situations in victim terms when
     they plausibly can, and to deflect oppressor framing when it's
     applied to them.

VO5. Claims of harm made by members of a designated victim group are
     received with a default of credibility ("believe women"). Claims
     of harm made by members of a designated oppressor group are
     received with a default of skepticism or reframing ("why is he
     bringing this up," "what's he really after," "this is a cover
     for resenting women").

VO6. Once a group holds the "victim" designation on a given axis, the
     designation tends to persist in sub-domains where the relevant
     harm has reversed or resolved. Women retain the victim
     designation in discussions of higher education in countries
     where women now earn a clear majority of degrees; the
     designation does not update to track the current outcome.

VO7. When a member of a designated oppressor group is harmed by a
     member of a designated victim group, the harm is more often
     described in individual or situational terms (a one-off, a
     dispute, a bad actor). When the direction reverses — a member of
     the victim group harmed by a member of the oppressor group — the
     harm is more often described in class terms (a pattern, a hate
     crime, evidence of structural violence). The same physical event
     yields different framings depending on who did it to whom.

VO8. "Victim-blaming" — asking what the person on the receiving end
     did to contribute to their own harm — is a named rhetorical
     offense when applied to members of designated victim groups. The
     same move applied to a male victim (suggesting a man hit by his
     female partner "must have provoked her") is not commonly called
     victim-blaming, and the term is rarely used for it.

VO9. In US jurisdictions with "primary aggressor" policies for
     domestic-violence calls — police training that instructs officers
     to identify the main aggressor in a mutual-violence incident
     instead of arresting both parties — male partners are identified
     as primary aggressor at substantially higher rates than
     incident-level facts (who hit first, who was injured more) would
     predict by themselves. This is contested in the research
     literature: some argue the disparity reflects real differences
     in severity and intent, others argue it reflects policies being
     applied asymmetrically by sex. Both readings are defended by
     domestic-violence researchers.

VO10. Political movements routinely claim victim status for their
      constituency as a rhetorical move — not a description of a
      specific harm done to specific people by specific others, but a
      class-level positioning that places the movement on the morally
      unassailable side of a dispute and, by implication, places its
      opposition in the oppressor role. The class-level frame
      substitutes for identifying specific conduct. When done by the
      other side, the same move is commonly flagged as exaggeration
      or grievance politics; when done by one's own side, it is
      commonly treated as simply describing reality.

VO11. "Oppressor" is applied to living people on the basis of actions
      taken by earlier members of their group, without requiring any
      specific conduct from the living person being designated. The
      mirror-image move — designating a living person "a victim" on
      the basis of harms done to earlier members of their group —
      also happens, but the inherited oppressor status is harder to
      escape: disclaiming, demonstrated conduct, and individual life
      history typically do not lift the label.

VO12. Inherited collective responsibility is applied asymmetrically
      by sex. Living men are routinely assigned partial responsibility
      in current-day discussions for actions of earlier members of
      their group (historical disenfranchisement of women, historical
      patriarchal law, wars started by male rulers). Living women are
      not similarly assigned responsibility for actions of earlier
      members of their group (historical support for restrictive
      gender roles, participation in witch-hunt accusations, lobbying
      for maternal-preference custody doctrines). The inherited-
      responsibility move runs in one direction.

VO13. "I am a victim of X" combines two distinct claims: (a) I have
      had a particular felt experience, and (b) the events that
      produced it occurred the way I'm describing them. The two are
      not the same. The felt experience can be entirely real while
      the description of who did what to whom is mistaken. In current
      mainstream discourse the two are commonly merged — the felt
      experience is treated as establishing the factual claim — and
      the merger skips the step that would normally be required to
      move from a subjective report to a public conclusion about what
      happened.

VO14. "Believe women," "believe survivors," and adjacent principles
      answer the question "should the listener take the claim
      seriously?" — a sound answer is yes. In practice they are
      increasingly applied to answer "is the claim correct?" — a
      different question, where seriousness alone does not establish
      accuracy. The slide between the two questions is rarely flagged
      and is socially costly to flag.

VO15. There is no widely-used, non-pejorative vocabulary for "felt
      but inaccurate" victim status. The available framings reduce to
      "real victim" and "lying." Because "lying" carries heavy moral
      weight and is socially ruinous to apply, the practical default
      becomes "real victim," even where the underlying account is
      sincerely mistaken — confused about the actor, the intent, the
      sequence of events, or the harm itself. Memory research is
      clear that sincere accounts can be substantially inaccurate;
      the discourse vocabulary does not have a non-accusatory way to
      say so about a specific case.

VO16. Conversational norms for engaging someone in a victim posture —
      listen without challenging, do not introduce contradicting
      evidence, do not ask questions that could be construed as doubt
      — are stronger when the listener belongs to a group designated
      as the source of harm. The result is a process that does not
      produce the cross-examination normally required to distinguish
      accurate from inaccurate accounts. Compassion and
      accuracy-checking are different functions; the norms make it
      hard to do both at once. A separate process for each — care
      about the felt experience over here, examine the factual claim
      over there — would in principle resolve the conflict. In
      practice the two are commonly conducted in the same conversation
      with the accuracy step suppressed.

VO17. Compassion for someone's felt experience and credit for their
      factual claim are distinct stances. Conflating them — treating
      sympathy as equivalent to belief — produces a position that is
      hard to revise even when later evidence contradicts the
      original account. Once compassion has been issued at the level
      of "I believe you," walking it back reads as withdrawing the
      compassion, not as revising the factual judgment. The
      conflation makes correction socially expensive.

VO18. The asymmetric reputational cost of error in the two
      directions — being judged a false accuser (rare, very costly)
      versus being judged a true accuser whose claim was ignored
      (more common, also very costly) — is not the same as the
      asymmetric cost of error for the listener: wrongly believing a
      false claim (cost paid mostly by the accused) versus wrongly
      disbelieving a true claim (cost paid mostly by the accuser).
      Discussions of "which error is worse" commonly mix these two
      framings, and the answer depends on which one is being asked.

VO19. The "victim" frame, once applied, tends to displace other
      descriptions of the same person — agent, peer, party to a
      dispute, ordinary participant — that would carry obligations,
      accountability, or symmetric scrutiny. Designating someone a
      victim is therefore not only a description of harm; it is also
      an adjustment to the role they play in the rest of the
      interaction. This is sometimes appropriate (the harm is
      severe and the role-shift is warranted) and sometimes not (the
      harm is contested or modest and the role-shift becomes a way
      to avoid ordinary scrutiny). The frame does not contain an
      internal procedure for deciding which case is which.

VO20. "Lived experience" is treated in mainstream gender discourse
      as a high-weight form of evidence on questions of sexism,
      harassment, and discrimination. When two lived experiences
      contradict each other — one woman reports that a particular
      room or institution silences women, another woman reports the
      same room engages women's contributions — the contradicting
      experience is typically reframed as that woman's internalized
      misogyny, false consciousness, distance from the experience
      the speaker considers authoritative, or proximity to male-
      coded power structures. The reframing allows the original
      claim to retain its evidentiary weight without the
      contradicting claim being credited as comparable lived
      experience.

VO21. Women who publicly contest mainstream feminist framings —
      Christina Hoff Sommers, Camille Paglia, Bari Weiss, Caitlin
      Flanagan, Helen Joyce — are commonly described in mainstream
      coverage as not speaking from authentic female experience,
      as "handmaidens of the patriarchy," as having internalized
      misogyny, or as serving male audiences. The "lived experience"
      criterion that grants women evidentiary standing on gender
      questions (VO3) is in practice withdrawn from women whose
      lived experience produces non-mainstream conclusions. The
      withdrawal is not flagged as withdrawal; it is presented as
      these women not really being the women whose voices are being
      centered.
