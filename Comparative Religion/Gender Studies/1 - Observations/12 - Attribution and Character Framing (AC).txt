--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTRIBUTION AND CHARACTER FRAMING (AC)
How the same behavior is described differently depending on who's doing
it — which determines the moral frame before any analysis begins.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AC1. When a woman behaves difficultly — snapping, being controlling,
     losing her temper, being cruel — it's commonly explained by what's
     happening to her: stress, hormones, being overwhelmed, past
     trauma, being provoked. When a man behaves the same way, it's
     commonly explained by who he is: a controlling personality,
     toxic traits, a character defect.

AC2. In therapy talk and self-help writing, female anger tends to be
     presented as a signal of unmet needs that deserves exploration
     ("what is your anger trying to tell you"). Male anger tends to be
     presented as a pathology requiring management ("anger issues,"
     "anger management").

AC3. When a woman visibly breaks down — cries, shouts, lashes out —
     the surrounding discourse commonly describes it as "having a
     bad moment," "a hard day," "being at the end of her rope." When
     a man visibly breaks down in the same way, it is commonly
     described as "showing who he really is" or "a red flag."

AC4. The everyday vocabulary for women's ongoing difficulty is wide
     and sympathetic: burnout, overwhelm, mental load, depletion,
     postpartum, perimenopause, hormonal cycle. The everyday
     vocabulary for men's ongoing difficulty is narrower and more
     often carries a negative tone: midlife crisis, anger issues,
     emotional unavailability, avoidance, toxic.

AC5. Women behaving difficultly are commonly described as "going
     through something." Men behaving difficultly are less often
     granted that framing and are more often described as "being
     difficult" — the "something happening to her" vs. "something
     true about him" distinction.

AC6. Female hostility toward an intimate partner is commonly framed
     in advice columns and media as something the other partner
     should try to understand, accommodate, or work through. Male
     hostility toward an intimate partner is commonly framed as
     something the other partner should exit or report.
