--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT CAN BE SAID ABOUT BIOLOGICAL DIFFERENCE (BD)
Less about the biology itself than about what speech is permitted
around it.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BD2. People who publicly discuss biological differences as a possible
     contributor to outcomes (e.g., why more men work in engineering
     and more women work in nursing) have repeatedly lost jobs,
     speaking invitations, or professional standing. People who
     publicly discuss discrimination as the explanation for the same
     outcomes do not face equivalent professional risk.

BD1. Research consistently finds differences, on average, between men
     and women in things like occupational interests, risk-taking
     behavior, and some cognitive patterns. ("On average" means
     on-the-whole across a population, not for every individual.)
     Whether these differences are produced by biology, socialization,
     or some combination is contested; that they exist as measured
     averages is not. The empirical finding is the substance BD2
     describes as restricted in mainstream professional speech.

BD3. When men and women end up doing different things on average
     (fields of study, occupations, life choices), the default
     institutional explanation is discrimination. Explaining any part
     of the pattern through different average preferences is treated
     as politically suspect and requires heavier justification.

BD4. In countries with more formal gender equality (e.g., the Nordic
     countries), men and women actually choose more different
     occupations and show larger differences in stated preferences,
     not smaller. This finding — called the "gender equality paradox"
     — receives limited mainstream coverage.

BD5. Medical research and clinical practice continue to rely on
     biological sex as a category for estimating disease risk,
     calculating medication dosages, and setting reference ranges for
     physiological measurements (e.g., hematocrit, hormone levels).

BD6. Sports governing bodies (the IOC, World Athletics, World Rugby,
     and others) have issued and revised policies on trans-athlete
     eligibility that weigh physiological effects of biological sex
     against inclusion principles. Different bodies have reached
     different conclusions, and the policies continue to change.

BD7. Public policy disputes about sex-segregated spaces (prisons,
     domestic violence shelters, sports categories, hospital wards)
     involve explicit disagreement about whether the operative
     category is biological sex or self-declared gender identity.

BD8. The same institutions sometimes use biological sex as the
     operative medical category — e.g., calling screening programs
     "prostate cancer screening" or "cervical cancer screening" — while
     in adjacent reproductive-health communications replacing the
     sex-based term with "people who menstruate" or "people who can
     become pregnant." The operativeness of sex is affirmed and denied
     in overlapping communications from the same source.

BD9. Multiple independent research programs (Schmitt and colleagues on
     personality, Lippa on interests, Falk and Hermle on preferences,
     Stoet and Geary on STEM choices) have found that sex differences
     in personality traits, stated partner preferences, and
     occupational preferences measure as larger — not smaller — in
     more egalitarian societies.

BD10. Cross-cultural comparison: a small set of sex-asymmetric
      patterns appears across nearly all large societies — male
      overrepresentation in physical-risk occupations, female
      overrepresentation in early-childcare roles, sex differences
      in average partner-selection preferences, male concentration
      at the top of political leadership. Other patterns (which
      specific traits are praised, which jobs are coded male or
      female in detail) vary substantially. Sorting "universal"
      from "local" is contested; the empirical case for some
      universality across very different societies is well-established
      for a small core set and disputed for the rest.

BD11. Empathy and prosocial behavior: meta-analyses across
      self-report and behavioral measures find women score higher on
      average on empathy and social-interest indices. Effect sizes
      are small to moderate, with substantial within-sex overlap
      (WG2). The pattern shows up in early childhood, across
      cultures, and in non-human-primate research, suggesting at
      least a partial biological component alongside socialization.
      The finding is uncontroversial as a population average and is
      commonly left out of mainstream gender discourse — possibly
      because it cuts against several intuitions in current framing.

BD12. Diagnostic-prevalence patterns are themselves sex-asymmetric.
      Depression and anxiety diagnoses skew female. ADHD,
      autism-spectrum, conduct-disorder, and substance-use diagnoses
      skew male. Whether the patterns reflect underlying biological
      differences, differences in how the same underlying state
      presents, differences in who seeks care, or some combination,
      is contested in the clinical literature. (See HM12 for the
      health-system-side framing.)
