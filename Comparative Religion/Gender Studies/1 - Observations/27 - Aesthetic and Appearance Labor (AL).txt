--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
AESTHETIC AND APPEARANCE LABOR (AL)
Time, money, and bodily intervention spent meeting appearance standards.
The cost is sex-asymmetric and falls predominantly on women; a smaller
and rising parallel pressure now operates on men.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AL1. Time and money spent on appearance maintenance — hair,
     skincare, makeup, fitness oriented at appearance, wardrobe,
     nail care — falls disproportionately on women. Survey data
     place the difference at multiple hours per week and a
     substantial percentage of disposable income on average across
     the working-age female population.

AL2. The cosmetic-procedure industry — surgical (breast augmentation,
     liposuction, rhinoplasty) and non-surgical (Botox, fillers,
     laser) — is overwhelmingly female in patient base. Male
     cosmetic procedures are rising but remain a small fraction of
     the total. The industry's existence is sustained by the
     appearance-pressure asymmetry in AL1.

AL3. Eating disorders (anorexia, bulimia, binge eating) are
     diagnosed disproportionately in women, particularly in
     adolescence. Body dysmorphic disorder is also more frequent in
     women. Male-specific equivalents (muscle dysmorphia,
     "bigorexia") exist and have risen but are less prominent in
     clinical and cultural attention.

AL4. The aging penalty falls more heavily on women in the dating
     market and in some sectors of the labor market. Survey-based
     attractiveness ratings of women decline with age more steeply
     than ratings of men; the labor-market evidence is more mixed
     and depends on sector. The aesthetic asymmetry compounds across
     decades of life and intersects with the late-life poverty
     pattern (HM11).

AL5. Appearance pressure produces a double bind. Effort spent on
     appearance is critiqued from one cultural vantage (as
     conformity to patriarchal standards or to "the male gaze");
     refusing the effort is penalized from another (in the dating
     and labor markets). Mainstream advice does not resolve the
     bind; it offers individual coping, not release from the
     underlying pressure.

AL6. Male appearance pressure exists and has measurably risen,
     particularly around height, hair retention, and muscularity,
     with corresponding increases in male body-image distress and
     in male cosmetic-procedure demand. The pressure operates
     primarily through the dating market (SR1) more than through
     the professional market. Mainstream framing treats it as
     relatively new and minor; the female-side equivalent is
     treated as long-standing and major. The asymmetry in framing
     is larger than the asymmetry in measured distress.

AL7. The aesthetic-labor cost on women (this section) and the
     bodily-risk cost on men (workplace fatalities, combat,
     stranger violence — see RB7's framing) are not the same kind
     of cost and are not directly tradable. Both are real, both
     sex-asymmetric, and listing one does not cancel the other.

AL8. The vocabulary of "self-care" has been mainstreamed for the
     female-coded appearance and bodily-maintenance routine, framing
     it as care of the self rather than as labor performed for an
     external standard. The same routines re-described in their
     external-purpose register — appearance-market signaling,
     mate-market signaling — are less commonly named, particularly
     in advice content directed at women themselves.
