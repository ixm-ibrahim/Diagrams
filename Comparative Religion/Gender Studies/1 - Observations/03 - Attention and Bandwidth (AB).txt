--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTENTION AND BANDWIDTH (AB)
Cognitive resources required to engage analytically with an emotionally
intense interlocutor, and what happens to discourse if those resources
are scarce. Some entries are well-supported empirically; the connections
between them and discourse outcomes are flagged where speculative.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

AB1. Carefully engaging with a person who is emotionally intense
     costs bandwidth separate from the work of evaluating what they
     said. The listener has to track the other person's feelings,
     control their own reactions, and manage tone — alongside, not
     instead of, evaluating what was said. This is a standard
     finding in cognitive-load and emotion-regulation research and
     holds regardless of who is talking to whom.

AB2. The bandwidth cost of managing feelings in a conversation is
     not paid equally by the two parties. The calmer party carries
     the emotion-management work; the more-intense party does not
     pay the same cost. Over a long exchange this becomes a real
     one-sided drain, separate from any question of who is correct.
     The party paying the cost has less left over for the substance.

AB3. Survey measures of attention behavior in Western adults — average
     uninterrupted task time, screen-shifting frequency, depth of
     single-source reading — have moved in the direction of less
     sustained attention over the past two decades. Most measures
     point in that direction; how big the drop is, is contested
     across studies, and a minority of researchers argue that what
     looks like a drop is attention being moved to new things rather
     than capacity falling. The trend appears in both sexes; some
     measures show it more pronounced in younger men.

AB4. (Speculative.) Sustained-attention capacity has dropped over the
     past two decades while the bandwidth cost of carefully engaging
     with someone who is emotionally intense has not. If both are
     true, that cost is harder to pay; likely consequences include
     more leaving conversations, more reactive responses, and less
     careful engagement on emotionally charged topics — with the
     costs falling harder on the calmer party. The link between the
     focus data and the discourse outcomes is plausible but hasn't
     been tested directly; flag it as a guess rather than a finding.

AB5. Activities that eat up long stretches of focus — and so
     compete with time spent talking with people or thinking
     carefully — differ by sex in what form they take. Long-form
     video games (action, competitive, role-playing,
     massively-multiplayer online) are a male-skewed category and
     have been accepted in the mainstream as a focus or attention
     concern for two decades. Female-skewed scrollable feeds
     (Instagram, TikTok) joined the named-concern list around
     2023–2024, primarily on youth mental-health grounds. Other
     female-skewed equivalents — reality TV, parasocial creator
     content (feeling close to YouTubers, streamers, and podcasters
     one only watches), true-crime media, romance and serial
     fiction — eat up comparable hours per week in survey data and
     remain outside the mainstream focus/addiction conversation.
     The asymmetry has narrowed but not closed.

AB6. In a charged disagreement, today's discourse norms add a rule
     on top of the bandwidth cost difference: when one party is much
     more emotionally intense than the other, the calmer party is
     expected to do the actual reasoning AND to carry the emotional
     management — and also expected not to say they are doing both.
     Naming the split ("I notice you're upset and I'm trying to stay
     analytical and I'm being asked to handle both") reads as
     dismissive or detached (see DD1).
