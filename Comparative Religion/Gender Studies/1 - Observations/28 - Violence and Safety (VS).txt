--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VIOLENCE AND SAFETY (VS)
Who gets hurt, who gets protected, and who is expected to do the
protecting.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

VS1. Men are the majority of victims of violent crime overall, and
     the overwhelming majority of victims of stranger violence
     (violence committed by someone the victim doesn't know).

VS2. Public safety campaigns and urban safety design (lighting,
     patrol patterns, hotline infrastructure) are built around
     reducing harm to women. Male victimization, which is numerically
     larger in absolute terms, receives little equivalent attention.

VS3. "Women and children first" persists as a norm in evacuation
     framing and informal culture (who gets onto the lifeboat, who
     gets shielded in a crisis). No parallel norm prioritizes men's
     survival in emergencies.

VS4. Men are culturally expected to intervene in violent situations
     at risk to themselves — confront the intruder, break up the
     fight, stand between the threat and bystanders. Women are not
     held to the same expectation.

VS5. "Protect women and children" is a common political and
     rhetorical phrase. No parallel phrase — positioning men as a
     category requiring protection — has comparable mainstream use.

VS6. US combat deaths across post-WWII conflicts are overwhelmingly
     male (single-digit percentages female or lower). This reflects
     both historically restricted female combat roles and continued
     male overrepresentation in front-line positions after those
     restrictions were formally removed.

VS7. In survey-based estimates, women report higher rates of
     sexual assault and intimate-partner sexual violence than men,
     particularly in younger cohorts. Rates of female-perpetrated
     sexual violence against men are documented but smaller in
     scale and less institutionally addressed (PF6).

VS8. Women are the substantial majority of victims of stalking and
     of sustained one-on-one harassment by an identifiable
     individual. Men are the substantial majority of victims of
     stranger physical violence (VS1). The two harm sets differ in
     kind — sustained-targeted vs. opportunistic-impersonal — not
     in severity. Both are real; mainstream coverage emphasizes
     the female-skewed set.

VS9. Women report substantially higher levels of fear in public
     spaces — fear of being followed, assaulted, or sexually
     harassed — particularly at night or in unfamiliar locations.
     The fear is asymmetrically distributed even where the
     stranger-violence victimization data (VS1) cuts the other
     way; both observations are real, and they describe different
     things (felt risk vs. statistical incidence). The felt-risk
     side is heavily addressed by public-safety policy (VS2); the
     incidence side is not.

VS10. Veteran-specific outcomes (see also HM13): post-service
      homelessness in the US is overwhelmingly male; post-service
      suicide is overwhelmingly male; PTSD diagnosed in veterans is
      male in absolute numbers (with female-veteran per-capita rates
      sometimes higher because of military sexual trauma). The
      veteran-specific male disadvantage is large in scale and
      receives mainstream coverage that is not proportional to its
      size — coverage tracks female-veteran-specific concerns more
      heavily than the absolute-numbers picture would predict.

VS11. Physical intimate-partner violence is bidirectional in a
      substantial fraction of violent relationships. CDC NISVS data
      and the Whitaker et al. (2007) analysis of large CDC datasets
      find that roughly half of relationships involving physical IPV
      are mutually violent; in the unidirectional remainder, women
      appear as the sole perpetrator more often than men in some
      cohorts (notably younger and dating samples). Severe injuries
      still skew toward female victims, and that part of the picture
      is what mainstream coverage addresses. The bidirectionality
      finding — well-established across the empirical literature
      (Straus, Whitaker, Archer's meta-analyses) — is largely absent
      from mainstream IPV coverage, which presents the pattern as
      predominantly unidirectional male-on-female. Both can be true
      simultaneously: women bear the heavier injury burden in
      physical IPV, and the underlying perpetration pattern is more
      bidirectional than the public framing acknowledges.

VS12. Domestic-violence service infrastructure is built around the
      unidirectional male-on-female model (FL7, FL8). Male victims
      of physical IPV — whether in mutually violent relationships
      (VS11) or unidirectionally female-perpetrated ones — face
      shelter, hotline, and law-enforcement responses that often do
      not contemplate them as victims. Survey data on male IPV
      victims reports being laughed at, disbelieved, or themselves
      arrested when they report. The asymmetry is not only in
      coverage but in the operational design of the response system.
