--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
COMMUNICATION STYLES (CS)
Average sex differences in conversational and conflict-handling style,
and which styles are valorized as the standard versus pathologized as a
deficit in mainstream advice and therapy discourse.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CS1. Survey and observational research finds average sex differences
     in conversational style. Women on average use more
     emotion-naming vocabulary, more rapport-building moves, and more
     processing-aloud speech. Men on average use more direct
     request-making, more content-focused speech, and more parallel
     (side-by-side) rather than face-to-face conversation. The
     differences are averages with substantial overlap (WG2).

CS2. Mainstream relationship advice and therapy discourse valorize
     the female-coded style and treat the male-coded style as a
     deficit to be corrected. Men are widely coached to "open up,"
     "share feelings," "name your emotions," and "stop withdrawing."
     Women are not commonly coached to "say things directly," "stop
     processing aloud at your partner," or "make a clean ask." The
     asymmetry treats one style as the standard and the other as a
     problem.

CS3. "Stonewalling" — withdrawing from a difficult conversation — is
     named as a negative male relational pattern in widely-cited
     couples-therapy material (Gottman's "four horsemen"). The
     mirror pattern — pursuing a partner who has signaled they are
     flooded and need to disengage — is less commonly named and is
     rarely treated with comparable weight in the same material.

CS4. Indirect communication — hinting, expecting context to be read
     — is more common on average among women in survey data. When
     the hint is missed, the typical mainstream framing is that the
     listener should have caught it ("if he loved me he'd know").
     The reverse framing — that an unstated request was the
     speaker's responsibility to make explicit — is less standard.

CS5. Conflict-resolution styles diverge. Mainstream advice ("name
     your feelings, stay engaged, talk it through now") aligns with
     female-coded handling. Male-coded handling (pause, cool down
     separately, return to the topic later) is often described as
     avoidance even where it produces resolution. Both can resolve
     conflict; the second is less often presented as a legitimate
     approach.

CS6. The rules for whose conversational behavior is appropriate in
     group settings have shifted (see WE9 on the workplace version).
     Women interrupting, taking floor time, and pushing back are
     commonly coded as appropriate self-advocacy. Men doing the same
     are commonly coded as dominating the room. Whether this reflects
     a needed correction to a prior asymmetry or a new asymmetry
     running the other way is contested; both readings have
     proponents.

CS7. The interaction of CS1, CS2, and AB1–AB2 produces a specific
     dynamic: the male-coded style of pausing under emotional
     intensity is named as a pathology (CS3) while the bandwidth-cost
     of staying analytical under intensity (AB1) is unacknowledged.
     A man who pauses is read as withholding; a man who stays and
     can't keep up with the emotional bandwidth demand is read as
     defensive or shut-down. Both moves are foreclosed in mainstream
     advice; the only acceptable move is to match the emotional
     register, which is not all listeners' available register.
