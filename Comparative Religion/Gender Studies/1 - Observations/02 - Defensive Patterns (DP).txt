DEFENSIVE PATTERNS (DP)
Moves a person tends to make when an uncomfortable claim lands, instead
of engaging the claim on its merits. Not specific to gender, but very
active on gender topics. These moves are not always invalid — sometimes
the pushback is correct — but each one is also available as a way to
avoid engaging, so the move shows up whether or not the original claim
was right.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DP1. When someone offers an analogy ("A is like B in this one respect"),
     an accepted response is to list differences between A and B
     that are true but don't speak to the specific point the
     analogy makes. Example: "going five miles per hour over the
     speed limit and going twenty-five over are alike in one
     specific way — both break the speed limit." Reply: "no — five
     over is what everyone does, twenty-five over is reckless, and
     the consequences if you're stopped are completely different."
     All three differences are real; but none of them speak to the
     specific point the analogy was making, which was that both
     break the speed limit. The real differences are used to block
     engagement with the narrow point.

DP2. When a specific asymmetry is raised (e.g., "men are 92% of
     workplace fatalities"), an accepted move is to bring up a
     different asymmetry that favors the other side ("but women earn
     less"). The second may be true, but it doesn't address the
     first, and bringing it up keeps the first from being examined
     at all. Called "whataboutism" when the other side does it;
     unnamed when one's own side does it.

DP3. When a group-level statistic is raised, an accepted move is to
     offer up a single case that doesn't fit and treat that case as
     if it disproves the statistic. "Men are about 78% of US homicide
     victims" met with "my aunt was killed by her husband." The
     individual case is real, the statistic is still real, and the
     conversation moves on as if the statistic has been answered —
     even though one case and one aggregate can both be true at once.

DP4. Tying a claim to a disliked group counts, on its own, as a
     reason to dismiss the claim. "That sounds like something a
     men's rights activist would say" or "that's an incel talking
     point" passes as a full refutation in many settings without
     the claim itself being engaged.

DP5. When a claim is made about a group someone identifies with, an
     accepted move is to reframe the claim as a personal attack.
     Instead of engaging with "you're describing a pattern in which
     some men behave badly," the response shifts the topic to "so
     you're saying I'm the bad guy?" The conversation moves from the
     pattern to the hurt, and the pattern stops being discussed.

DP6. Attacking the source of evidence passes as a rebuttal of the
     evidence itself. "That study was funded by X" or "that
     researcher has said problematic things elsewhere" is offered as
     a reason not to engage with what the study actually found.

DP7. An accepted response to an uncomfortable observation is to
     ask for a fix on the spot before the observation itself has
     been acknowledged. "What do you propose we do about it?" shifts
     the question from "is this true?" to "is there a fix?" If the
     speaker doesn't have a fix at hand, the observation is read as
     discredited by the missing fix.

DP8. In group conversation, sidelining the question of whether a
     claim is true in favor of worry about how raising it will make
     people feel is an accepted move. "This is upsetting people in
     the room" is treated as taking priority over "is what you said
     accurate?" — and the question of whether the claim is true is
     allowed to lapse, never settled.

DP9. Counter-cases to group-level statistics get different
     acceptability depending on direction. Naming "not all men" on
     entry and dismissing it as deflection — when it's offered
     against a statistic about male behavior — passes as the
     legitimate response. The mirror move — "my wife out-earns me"
     against the pay-gap figure, "I've known women who abused their
     husbands" against domestic-violence-against-women statistics
     — is not accepted as a move in mainstream discourse, so is
     rarely offered; and when offered, dismissing it as deflection
     before the underlying statistic is examined is the accepted
     response. Same rhetorical move, different acceptability by
     direction.

DP10. The "fragility" charge (see LR11) works as a closed loop in
      argument. Accepting the charge confirms it. Pushing back on it
      is read as confirming it. The structure leaves no way to
      engage the underlying claim without first accepting the
      framing. The same closed-loop charge is not in active
      mainstream use against women in mirror form: a woman
      responding defensively to a comparable claim about women is
      not standardly met with "that response just shows female
      fragility."

DP11. The bodily-integrity analogy between routine infant circumcision
      and female genital cutting is a recurring concrete instance of
      DP1. The analogy claims a narrow point — both involve cutting a
      child's genitals without the child's consent — and is reliably
      met by listing real differences (FGM causes more physical harm,
      has no medical rationale, is done to suppress female sexuality)
      that don't speak to the consent point. The differences are
      real; they don't engage the specific analogical claim. The
      response is predictable enough to be flagged in advance
      whenever the analogy is raised. (See FL10 for the
      legal-framework version of the same asymmetry.)
