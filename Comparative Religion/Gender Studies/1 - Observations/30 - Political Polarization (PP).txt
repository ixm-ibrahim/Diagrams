--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
POLITICAL POLARIZATION (PP)
The recent and unusually large divergence in political identification
and voting behavior between young men and young women across multiple
Western countries, and what it implies for whether gender topics can be
discussed without partisan coding.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PP1. In US polling and equivalent surveys across Western Europe and
     South Korea, young women (under 30) have moved sharply to the
     political left over the past decade, while young men in the
     same cohort have not moved or have moved slightly right. The
     resulting sex gap in young-cohort political identification is
     the largest on record in available survey data.

PP2. The polarization tracks issue clusters. Female-coded issues
     (abortion, harassment, representation) and male-coded topics
     (boys falling behind, custody fairness, draft equality)
     increasingly correlate with the political left/right cleavage
     rather than crossing it. Bringing up either set now reads as
     partisan signaling rather than as a substantive observation —
     even when the speaker intends neither party affiliation.

PP3. The voting-pattern divergence has begun to show up in election
     results. In several recent Western elections, the sex gap among
     young voters substantially exceeds the gap among older voters
     and exceeds the gap any prior cohort showed at the same age.

PP4. The political coding of gender topics affects which
     sex-asymmetry observations a media outlet, employer, university,
     or institution is willing to publish. The female-disadvantage
     set is more publishable in left-coded venues; the
     male-disadvantage set is more publishable in right-coded venues
     (RC1, RC2). The framework's structural claim — that both sets
     exist and should be examined together — is itself politically
     miscoded as a result, and gets read by partisans on both sides
     as a covert version of the other side's argument.

PP5. The dating market and the political market interact. Younger
     women's left shift (PP1) combined with their continued
     preference for higher-status partners (SR2) produces a
     partner-search problem if higher-status men shift right or
     remain centrist. The mismatch is reported in dating-app data
     and attitudinal surveys; its consequences for marriage
     formation are unfolding (see SR, RS).

PP6. Both major US parties, and equivalent formations elsewhere,
     have increasingly framed campaign messaging in sex-targeted
     terms — treating men and women as discrete electoral segments
     with separable interests. Whether this reflects an underlying
     separation of interests or constructs one is contested. The
     messaging itself is observable.

PP7. Class is a major within-sex moderator (WG3). The young-male
     right shift is concentrated among working-class and
     non-college-educated men more than among college-educated men.
     The young-female left shift is more uniform across class.
     Aggregate-by-sex framings can hide this and produce a misread
     of the underlying pattern.

PP8. The polarization makes some kinds of gender discourse harder
     to conduct. A claim that requires holding both evidentiary
     sets simultaneously (this framework's claim) is now read by
     listeners on both sides through their partisan filter, which
     compresses the claim into the closest one-sided position the
     filter recognizes. Stating that both sets are real and both
     should be examined is increasingly an unstable position not
     because it is wrong but because it has no stable home in the
     current political configuration.
