--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EMOTIONAL NEEDS (EN)
How each sex's emotional needs are named, treated, and used — both in
private relationships and in public claims.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EN1. "Unmet emotional needs" in relationship discourse is almost
     exclusively applied to women. When a woman reports her emotional
     needs aren't being met, the framing treats the unmet need as a
     real condition that should be addressed. When a man reports the
     same — his emotional needs aren't being met by his partner —
     the framing more often treats him as needy, entitled, or as
     someone who should work on himself.

EN2. The assumption that men have simpler or fewer emotional needs
     ("men just want sex and food") circulates widely in pop
     psychology and mainstream advice. It both underestimates what
     men actually report wanting — connection, being seen, being
     respected, physical affection — and makes the vocabulary of
     "unmet needs" less available to them when those needs go unmet.

EN3. The vocabulary for naming emotional needs is not equally usable
     across sex. A woman saying "I need more emotional intimacy" or
     "I need to feel seen" is typically taken at face value. A man
     using the same phrasing is often read as weak, needy, or
     effeminate. A man naming the same underlying need in a more
     traditionally-masculine register ("I need to feel respected,"
     "I need physical affection from my partner") is often read as
     making demands. The register he can use to be heard without
     penalty is narrower.

EN4. Need-claims function politically as well as personally. "I need
     to feel safe" — said by a woman, or by a member of a designated
     victim group — has become a load-bearing basis for policy
     changes, speaker cancellations, and institutional concessions;
     the speaker's felt-safety is treated as sufficient justification
     for action. The same need-claim from men (or from designated
     non-victim groups) does not carry the same load-bearing weight
     and is more often treated as unreasonable or as a demand for
     special treatment.

EN5. Men's emotional struggles are commonly narrated about them in
     the third person by outside observers (media, therapy discourse,
     relationship columns) before men themselves speak to the
     struggles, and the third-person narration often carries a
     critical tone (emotionally stunted, avoidant, fragile, checked
     out, toxic). Women's emotional struggles are more often narrated
     in the first person by women in mainstream media using
     vocabulary women supply themselves, and that first-person
     narration is more often received sympathetically.

EN6. In children, girls' emotional needs are more likely to be
     treated as requiring adult attention and response (naming the
     feeling, talking it through, validating it). Boys' emotional
     needs are more likely to be treated as requiring self-regulation,
     toughening up, or a redirect to activity. The pattern is
     observable in parenting advice, teacher response patterns, and
     popular depictions of child-rearing.

EN7. "Emotional unavailability" — the charge that a partner is closed
     off, not offering emotional presence — is a mainstream complaint
     leveled at men. The equivalent complaint about women — that a
     woman is unavailable for the emotional content her male partner
     wants (presence, attention, sexual desire, affirmation) — has no
     widely-used name, and is more often framed as him having an
     unreasonable demand than as her being emotionally absent.
