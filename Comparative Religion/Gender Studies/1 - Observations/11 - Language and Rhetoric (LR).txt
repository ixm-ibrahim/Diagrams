--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
LANGUAGE AND RHETORIC (LR)
Which phrases about men and women are mainstream-acceptable, and which
parallel phrases are not.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LR1. Expressing frustration with men as a group is socially acceptable
     in mainstream settings: "ugh, men," "men are exhausting," "men
     are the worst" pass without sanction. The equivalent statements
     about women — "ugh, women," "women are the worst" — are not
     acceptable and are typically flagged as misogyny.

LR2. "Men are trash" and "kill all men" circulate as acceptable
     humor in mainstream online and cultural spaces. Structurally
     identical phrases — "women are trash," "kill all women" — do not
     circulate as humor and are treated as threats or hate speech.

LR3. "Mansplaining" (a man condescendingly explaining something to a
     woman), "manspreading" (sitting with legs open), "male
     fragility," and "toxic masculinity" are mainstream words with
     wide circulation. No parallel shorthand naming a typical female
     behavior (e.g., for a woman interrupting a man she assumes knows
     less, or for a common pattern of female social aggression) has
     comparable currency in everyday speech.

LR4. "Believe women" is offered as a principle applied to
     accusations made by women. There is no parallel principle
     "believe men" applied to accusations made by men.

LR5. "Men need to do better" works as mainstream collective framing.
     "Women need to do better," used the same way about women as a
     group, does not.

LR6. When an individual man describes suffering, a common response
     is to point out that men as a group still benefit structurally
     ("but you have privilege"). When an individual woman describes
     suffering, she is not typically answered by pointing out domains
     where women as a group benefit (e.g., longer life expectancy,
     lower homelessness rates, preferential custody outcomes).

LR7. "Patriarchy" — meaning a system in which men hold dominant power
     — is used as a current-tense description of society even in
     specific domains where men are now underrepresented or
     underperforming (higher education, early-career earnings in
     certain cities, school achievement). There is no parallel
     structural term ("matriarchy" or similar) applied to those
     domains.

LR8. Calling a claim "what women think" is commonly contested as an
     illegitimate generalization about women. Calling a claim "what
     men think" is rarely contested on the same grounds.

LR9. Women writing, speaking, or making content about the inner lives
     of men — what men want, what men fear, why men are the way they
     are — is a mainstream, uncontested genre. Men writing, speaking,
     or making content about the inner lives of women is commonly
     contested as presumptuous, as "mansplaining women to women," or
     as an illegitimate claim to speak for a group he isn't in. The
     same cross-sex authorial move is licensed in one direction and
     challenged in the other.

LR10. Male-default generic terms (chairman, mankind, generic "he/him")
      have been flagged as exclusionary in institutional style guides
      and widely replaced (chairperson, humanity, they/them, singular
      they). Female-coded generic terms at comparable frequency have
      not been flagged or replaced. In current writing, generic
      "she/her" is commonly treated as progressive; generic "he/him"
      in the same contexts is treated as sexist.

LR11. The vocabulary of "fragility" — "male fragility," "fragile
      masculinity," and the adjacent "white fragility" — names a
      defensive emotional response to a critical claim and treats that
      response as further evidence of the underlying flaw being
      charged. The structure is self-sealing: agreeing with the charge
      confirms the flaw; pushing back is read as a display of the
      fragility being charged. There is no mainstream parallel term
      ("female fragility" or equivalent) for an analogous defensive
      reaction by women, even where the response pattern is
      structurally identical. The vocabulary is licensed to flow in
      one direction.

LR12. "Implicit (gender) bias" — the claim that a person harbors
      sex-based prejudicial attitudes they cannot themselves
      perceive — is a mainstream vocabulary item applied to men in
      workplaces, hiring committees, and professional settings.
      Denial of the bias is treated as evidence of the bias being
      denied. Self-report is treated as unreliable on the grounds
      that the bias is by definition invisible to the holder. The
      Implicit Association Test, the standard behavioral instrument
      associated with the term, has documented test-retest
      reliability problems and weak prediction of individual
      behavior; the term remains in mainstream use despite this. No
      parallel vocabulary in equivalent mainstream circulation
      attributes implicit gender bias to women as a class.

LR13. "Privilege" — applied as a designation to specific living
      individuals via "male privilege" and adjacent forms — names an
      unearned advantage held by group membership and treats
      acknowledgment of the privilege as a precondition for further
      conversation. Acknowledging the privilege is read as
      confirmation; denying it is read as the unawareness that
      constitutes the privilege. The term is not applied as a
      mainstream designation to women on dimensions where women are
      advantaged (life expectancy, custody outcomes, lower workplace
      fatality rates, lower homicide victimization, lower
      incarceration rates, lower combat exposure). No parallel
      "female privilege" vocabulary item operates with comparable
      mainstream circulation, even in the specific domains where
      women hold the advantaged position.

LR14. "Patriarchy" as a current-tense explanatory term is applied
      to opposite outcomes simultaneously. Men holding senior
      positions confirms patriarchy; women holding senior positions
      is described as tokenism within patriarchy or as patriarchy
      operating in modified form. Men's overrepresentation in
      dangerous occupations is described as male disposability under
      patriarchy; women's overrepresentation in safer occupations is
      described as protective sexism under patriarchy. Men's higher
      suicide rate is described as patriarchy harming men; men's
      historical longer lives in some societies is described as
      patriarchy benefiting men. The same term covers the outcome
      and its mirror image with the framing's applicability
      preserved in both directions.

LR15. "Microaggression," in the term's Sue (2007) formulation widely
      cited in subsequent training materials, defines a harmful act
      by the recipient's experience of the act rather than by
      external features of the act. Whether a given comment counts
      as a gendered microaggression turns on whether the woman
      receiving it experienced it as one. The term is in mainstream
      institutional circulation (HR training, university programming,
      corporate DEI). No mainstream parallel term defines harm to
      men by male recipient experience in the same form, even in
      domains where observer-defined-harm framings could plausibly
      apply (classroom characterization of male students as a class,
      dismissive responses to men's grievances, "men are trash"
      circulation in mixed-audience settings).
