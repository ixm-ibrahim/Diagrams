--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FATHER ABSENCE AND BOYS' OUTCOMES (FA)
Concrete observations about the absence of a resident biological
father and what that absence does, on average, to the children — and
how the topic sits in different research and policy traditions.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FA1. Father-absent households (no resident biological father) make
     up roughly a quarter of US households with children, with
     substantial variation across populations. The trend over the
     past several decades has been toward higher rates of father
     absence.

FA2. Boys raised in father-absent households show, on average,
     higher rates of school dropout, behavioral problems, juvenile
     criminal-justice contact, and adult incarceration relative to
     boys raised with present fathers in otherwise similar
     circumstances. The effect remains substantial after controlling
     for income and education in many studies, though the magnitude
     varies and the causal pathway (selection vs. effect of absence
     itself) is debated. Girls raised in father-absent households
     also show negative effects, with a different pattern (earlier
     sexual activity, higher teenage-pregnancy rates).

FA3. The mainstream policy framing of single-parent households is
     "support the parent" — typically the mother. The framing
     "address why the father isn't there" or "intervene to keep
     fathers present" has substantially less institutional support
     and less developed policy infrastructure. Father presence as a
     child-welfare intervention is not in the standard policy
     toolkit.

FA4. Custody policy (FL1, FL2) and family-law incentives interact
     with father absence. When custody disputes systematically award
     primary physical custody to mothers, the practical role
     available to non-custodial fathers narrows. Whether the custody
     pattern produces more father absence, or whether father absence
     produces the custody pattern, is contested; both directions are
     likely operating.

FA5. The "father deficit" research is more institutionally developed
     in some sub-disciplines (sociology of family, criminology, child
     development) than in others (gender studies). The same
     observation about a sex-asymmetric child-rearing arrangement
     reads as central in one literature and marginal in another.

FA6. The cultural prestige of fatherhood as an identity has declined
     in mainstream Western depictions over the past several decades.
     Fathers in popular media (MR1) are routinely shown as
     incompetent at domestic tasks; the affirmative case for what a
     father uniquely contributes to a child's development is less
     common in current child-rearing advice than the equivalent case
     for what a mother uniquely contributes.

FA7. Boy-specific developmental data — later-on-average maturation
     of executive function, higher rates of behavioral and
     learning-disorder diagnoses (BD12, ED3), higher infant-mortality
     rates, higher dropout rates — does not have a parallel
     boys-as-population research and policy infrastructure equivalent
     to the girls-as-population infrastructure (Title IX programs,
     girls-in-STEM initiatives, "girl power" branding, MR4) that
     responded to the female-side data of an earlier era.

FA8. Where father-absence research has been engaged in mainstream
     coverage, it has more often been treated as a class or race
     story than as a gender story. The framing matters: when the
     story is told as gender, it is a story about boys not having
     fathers; when told as class, the same boys appear as a class
     statistic. The gender framing is the one currently
     under-represented.
