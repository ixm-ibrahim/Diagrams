--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
READERSHIP AND COVERAGE (RC)
Where gender-asymmetry topics get covered, by whom, and for which
readers. How the two evidentiary sets this framework catalogues are
distributed across reading locations.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RC1. Mainstream left-coded general-audience publications (New York
     Times, Washington Post, The Atlantic, Guardian, NPR) mostly
     cover gender-asymmetry topics from the set that documents
     female disadvantage and male-misbehavior patterns — pay gap,
     MeToo, reproductive rights, leadership representation,
     harassment. Coverage of the other set — sentencing gap, custody
     outcomes, male-only draft registration, workplace-death sex
     ratio, male suicide rate, male educational underperformance —
     is rare in the same venues. When it does appear, it is more
     often framed as a problem because of its effect on women
     ("men falling behind" treated as a problem for women looking
     for marriageable partners) than as a problem about men
     standing on its own.

RC2. Right-coded publications (Wall Street Journal op-ed, New York
     Post, The Free Press, Quillette, National Review) and
     explicitly men's-issues venues (fathers-rights organizations,
     large parts of online men's discourse, books like Warren
     Farrell's "The Boy Crisis" or Richard Reeves's "Of Boys and
     Men") mostly cover gender-asymmetry topics from the set that
     documents male disadvantage. Coverage of the female-
     disadvantage set is thin in these venues and is more often
     contested than reported.

RC3. Academic gender studies programs focus their curricula on the
     female-disadvantage set. Courses engaging the male-disadvantage
     set are rare inside gender studies departments, though related
     material does appear elsewhere in the university — criminology
     (sentencing, incarceration), family law (custody, alimony),
     labor economics (workplace deaths, occupational segregation),
     and education research (boys falling behind in school). The
     topics exist in academia; they just tend not to be housed in
     the department with "gender" in its title.

RC4. Philanthropic and institutional funding tagged "gender" in the
     US — major private foundations (Ford, Gates, MacArthur,
     Rockefeller), federal programs (EEOC, Title IX enforcement,
     Women's Bureau, the White House Gender Policy Council), and
     corporate DEI spending — flows overwhelmingly to programs
     addressing the female-disadvantage set. Funding tagged for the
     male-disadvantage set (men's mental health, boys' school
     underperformance, custody equality, fatherless-household
     interventions) is a small fraction — orders of magnitude
     smaller at the foundation level.

RC5. Institutionally embedded, well-funded feminist advocacy
     organizations (NOW, Planned Parenthood, UN Women, Time's Up,
     the Women's Law Project) address the female-disadvantage set.
     Men's-issues advocacy (National Coalition For Men, men's-health
     nonprofits, fathers-rights coalitions) is smaller, less well
     funded, and carries lower cultural standing — even within its
     own subject-matter expertise.
