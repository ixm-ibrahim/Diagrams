--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
REPRODUCTIVE AND BODILY BURDEN (RB)
Bodily costs and constraints of reproduction, contraception, and
caregiving — and the unequal distribution of physical risk, time, and
recovery between sexes. Several of these run against women in ways the
rest of this framework does not otherwise capture.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RB1. Pregnancy and childbirth carry physical risks borne entirely by
     women: maternal mortality (small but nonzero in developed
     countries, larger elsewhere), permanent physical changes
     (pelvic-floor damage, cardiovascular strain, dental loss,
     musculoskeletal change), and weeks-to-months of recovery. Men
     face no analogous bodily cost from biological reproduction.

RB2. Reversible contraception is overwhelmingly female-targeted.
     Long-term reversible male methods do not exist at scale; the
     available male methods (condoms, withdrawal, vasectomy as a
     not-fully-reversible option) are limited. Side-effect burden of
     long-term hormonal contraception — mood, weight, libido,
     cardiovascular risk — falls on women.

RB3. Abortion-access decisions, whatever their resolution, fall on
     the pregnant person — virtually always a woman — and concern
     her body in a way they do not concern the male partner's body.
     The political and bodily stakes of access decisions are
     asymmetric in subject position.

RB4. Workplace and economic costs of pregnancy and early childcare
     fall disproportionately on women: career interruption, reduced
     lifetime earnings, slower promotion, and a documented
     "motherhood penalty." A "fatherhood premium" runs in the
     opposite direction but is smaller in magnitude and not
     symmetric. Public policy in most jurisdictions does not fully
     compensate either way.

RB5. Menstruation and perimenopause carry recurring physiological
     costs across decades of life — pain, bleeding, fatigue,
     cognitive effects, mood — and in many workplaces these are
     under-accommodated. Men face no direct physiological equivalent.

RB6. Adult-parent caregiving — the unpaid time and emotional work
     of caring for aging parents — falls disproportionately on adult
     daughters and daughters-in-law. The labor is substantial,
     largely uncompensated, and affects career continuity and
     retirement security. This asymmetry mirrors childcare (RB4) at
     the other end of life and is rarely addressed in policy.

RB7. The bodily-risk asymmetries in this section (women) and the
     bodily-risk asymmetries elsewhere in the framework (men in
     workplace fatalities, combat, stranger violence, suicide) are
     of different kinds but neither is reducible to the other.
     Reproduction-related risk and physical-labor / violence-related
     risk are both real, both measurable, both asymmetric by sex,
     and neither cancels the other out.

RB8. Reproductive autonomy in current law runs in opposite
     directions on different axes. Bodily-autonomy law on continuing
     or ending a pregnancy treats the decision as the pregnant
     woman's; the male partner has no legal standing to compel
     either outcome. The same body of law treats financial
     responsibility for any resulting child as binding on the
     biological father from birth, with no equivalent option to
     disclaim. The two asymmetries run in opposite directions —
     favoring the woman on the bodily-decision axis, exposing the
     man to a unilateral decision on the financial axis.

RB9. There is no legal mechanism in major Western jurisdictions
     equivalent to "financial abortion" — a man's option, before
     some defined point in pregnancy, to disclaim financial
     responsibility for a child he did not intend to father. The
     asymmetry between the woman's option to terminate and the
     man's lack of an equivalent option is rarely framed as a
     matter of reproductive equity in mainstream coverage.

RB10. Paternity fraud (FL4) — a woman knowingly naming a
      non-biological father — and the legal persistence of
      child-support obligations after DNA disproof (FL3) compound
      to produce a category of male financial obligation with no
      female equivalent: a man can be required to pay for years
      for a child not biologically his, with no parallel category
      of obligation running the other way.
