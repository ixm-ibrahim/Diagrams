--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WITHIN-GROUP VARIATION (WG)
A reading instruction for the rest of the framework. Every "men do X"
and "women do Y" claim in this document is a claim about averages
across a population. The within-sex spread is large and matters for how
the claims should be read.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WG1. The framework's claims about "men" and "women" are claims about
     averages across populations, not about every individual. A claim
     of the form "men do X more than women" is consistent with most
     men not doing X and many women doing X. Mistaking the average
     for the individual produces both false generalizations ("men are
     like that") and false rebuttals ("but I know a man who isn't").
     Both errors are common in gender discourse.

WG2. The within-sex variance on most psychological and behavioral
     measures is larger than the between-sex difference. "The average
     man" claims describe a tendency in a distribution, not a
     description of every member. This is a standard finding in
     psychometrics and applies to almost every observation in this
     framework that takes the form of a sex-difference claim.

WG3. Class is a major within-sex moderator. Working-class men and
     college-educated urban men diverge sharply on most outcome
     measures: marriage rates, life expectancy, employment, suicide
     rate, school achievement, friendship counts. Several "men's"
     trends in this framework are concentrated in the lower end of
     the class distribution and are partly masked by averaging with
     the upper end. The same is true within women, with different
     patterns.

WG4. Geographic variation is large. Patterns visible in dense urban
     areas (women out-earning men, female educational majorities,
     late marriage) coexist with rural and suburban areas where the
     older patterns persist. National averages mix the two and
     produce a midpoint that describes neither well.

WG5. Age-cohort variation is large. Several patterns identified in
     this framework — the male attention decline (AB3), the
     political-polarization gap (PP), the friendship decline (RS3),
     the dating-market concentration (SR1) — are concentrated in
     younger cohorts and partly absent from older ones. Aggregating
     across cohorts hides the trajectory.

WG6. Subgroup-level claims and population-level claims are different
     claims. "Men in the bottom income quintile have life expectancy
     X" is a different claim from "men have life expectancy X."
     Several mainstream gender claims and several anti-mainstream
     gender claims switch between these without flagging the move.
     Wherever this framework cites a population average, the
     within-group spread is meaningful and the average is not the
     description of every member.

WG7. The "all men benefit" / "all women suffer" framings the
     framework critiques elsewhere (LR6, VO11) draw much of their
     persuasive force from collapsing within-group variation. The
     critique of those framings is itself weakened if the framework
     making the critique then collapses the same variation in the
     other direction. Honest reading of any sex-asymmetry claim — in
     this framework or any other — requires keeping the distribution
     visible behind the average.
