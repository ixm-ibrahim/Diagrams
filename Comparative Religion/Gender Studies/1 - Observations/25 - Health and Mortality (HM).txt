--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
HEALTH AND MORTALITY (HM)
Concrete statistical asymmetries in how long each sex lives and what
each sex dies from.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HM1. Men die by suicide at three to four times the rate of women in
     most Western countries.

HM2. Men have shorter life expectancy than women in virtually every
     country in the world. In the US the gap is roughly five to six
     years.

HM3. Men are roughly 70% of homicide victims and roughly 80% of
     homicide perpetrators.

HM4. Men make up the substantial majority of homeless populations
     and of overdose deaths.

HM5. Men are less likely than women to seek preventive medical
     care (routine check-ups, early screening) and more likely to
     die of preventable conditions.

HM6. Prostate cancer research receives substantially less public
     and charitable funding than breast cancer research despite
     comparable incidence rates in the population.

HM7. Women's health awareness campaigns are culturally prominent
     (pink ribbons, breast cancer awareness month, heart disease
     in women campaigns). Men's health awareness campaigns (e.g.,
     Movember) exist but are far smaller in cultural footprint and
     institutional backing.

HM8. Mental health services and outreach are mostly designed
     around patterns more common in women: talking about feelings,
     seeking help, describing internal states. Patterns more common
     in men — irritability, physical symptoms, action-oriented
     avoidance, externalizing — are less well served by the design
     of mainstream mental-health services.

HM9. Several diagnostic and treatment gaps run against women.
     Endometriosis is diagnosed an average of seven to ten years
     after symptom onset across many populations. Heart-attack
     presentation in women has historically been underrecognized in
     clinical training (women more often present with atypical
     symptoms and receive delayed intervention). Autoimmune
     conditions, which disproportionately affect women, show
     measurable time-to-diagnosis gaps.

HM10. Pain-management research and clinical practice have
      documented under-treatment of pain in women relative to
      comparable presentations in men. Women's pain reports are more
      often interpreted as psychogenic, anxiety-related, or
      stress-related rather than as evidence of physical pathology;
      the under-treatment is measurable in opioid prescribing data,
      time-to-analgesia in emergency departments, and referral
      patterns.

HM11. Old-age poverty falls more on women than on men. Women's
      longer life expectancy combined with lower lifetime earnings
      and lower retirement savings produces higher poverty rates
      among women in late life. Men's earlier mortality cuts the
      trajectory short; women's longer survival comes paired with
      elevated economic vulnerability in the later years. The two
      asymmetries (HM2 on life expectancy and HM11 on late-life
      poverty) are not in tension — they describe different parts
      of the same pattern.

HM12. Diagnostic-prevalence patterns by sex (parallel to BD12):
      women receive depression and anxiety diagnoses at higher rates
      than men; men receive ADHD, autism-spectrum, substance-use,
      and conduct-disorder diagnoses at higher rates than women. The
      depression gap coexists with men's substantially higher suicide
      rate (HM1), which raises a measurement question: whether men
      under-report depression and are therefore under-diagnosed
      despite the lethal outcomes, or whether the depression and
      suicide patterns are partly distinct phenomena with different
      sex distributions. Both readings have proponents.

HM13. Veteran-specific health outcomes: post-service suicide rates
      among male US veterans are substantially elevated above
      same-age civilian male rates. Veteran homelessness in the US
      is overwhelmingly male in absolute numbers. Female veterans
      face elevated rates of military sexual trauma and
      transition-related distress; per-capita rates can be
      comparable while absolute numbers stay smaller because of
      smaller force participation. Both patterns are real;
      mainstream institutional attention is currently weighted
      toward female-veteran-specific issues despite the male-side
      numbers being substantially larger in scale.

HM14. The "default male" / "invisible women" framing — that medical
      research, drug dosing, crash-test dummy design, body-armor
      sizing, and product ergonomics have historically defaulted to
      male bodies — is a major mainstream gender claim. The
      empirical record is mixed. Female underrepresentation in
      cardiovascular and pharmacological trials through the late
      twentieth century is documented; standard crash-test dummies
      used a fiftieth-percentile male body as reference for decades;
      some PPE and tool ergonomics were sized to male anthropometry.
      FDA and NIH research-inclusion rules from the 1990s onward
      substantially altered the trial-inclusion picture, and several
      women-coded conditions (breast cancer, reproductive health)
      now receive comparable or greater research and funding
      attention than male-coded counterparts (HM6 on prostate vs.
      breast cancer research). The specific diagnostic and
      treatment gaps for women (HM9, HM10) survive the audit; the
      stronger architectural claim — that medicine and design
      generally treat the male body as default and the female body
      as deviation — is partly real, partly outdated by reform, and
      partly overstated. The framing is durable in mainstream
      coverage even where the underlying domain has been reformed.
