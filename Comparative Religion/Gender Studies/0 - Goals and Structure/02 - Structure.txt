STRUCTURE

Sections are ordered from less gender-specific (general discourse
patterns, what can and can't be said about gender) toward more
gender-specific (concrete stats and policy in specific domains
of life).

IDs are stable anchors. Each section has a fixed two-letter prefix
(DD, DP, AB, OD, WG, BD, PR, RC, VO, IG, LR, AC, EN, AP, TA, etc.).
Individual entry IDs (DP10, VO11, DDm1, etc.) survive across versions
even when sections are reordered or file numbers change. The two-
digit file prefix is ordering only and may change; the two-letter
ID is the permanent anchor for cross-references.

The block-by-block roster of sections is in 00 - Section Roster.

================================================================================
STRUCTURE OF AN OBSERVATION
================================================================================

Every entry in the framework — raw observation, meta observation,
or any later layer — meets the same requirements. The requirements
are not about formatting; they are about what makes an entry able
to do its work.

1. Demystification.
   The entry reads in plain language. A smart non-expert understands
   it on first read; a specialist reads it and does not wince. The
   full demystification spec is in 01 - Writing Goal; this rule
   applies at every layer.

2. Accuracy.
   The entry says what is the case, not what is comfortable for
   either side. Where the empirical record is mixed, the entry
   says so directly rather than picking the side that fits a prior
   commitment.

3. Precision.
   The entry says one thing precisely. Two pieces have to be
   bounded.

   Scope — which time period, which population, which domain.
   An entry vague about scope gives an alert reader an easy
   way to dismiss it as overreach.

   Claim-dimension — which aspect of the phenomenon the entry
   is making a claim about. A pattern can be described along
   several dimensions: how often it happens (frequency),
   whether the move is permitted or sanctioned (social
   acceptability), how harmful it is (severity), how widely
   it is believed (uptake). The entry should pick the
   dimension that matches the actual point. An entry on the
   wrong dimension quietly undershoots — the words land but
   describe something one step over from what the writer
   meant.

   The most common dimension-mistake in this framework is
   making an acceptability claim with a verb that only carries
   occurrence. Most of the discourse-move observations in this
   framework are claims about what is socially permitted, not
   about how often the move happens. The asymmetric-treatment
   observations especially: the same move runs in both
   directions, but only one direction is accepted while the
   other is called out and punished. When that is the point,
   the verb has to say so.

   Verbs that carry occurrence only — "is X-ed," "is answered
   with," "is replaced by," "is dismissed as," "happens,"
   "occurs," "tends to" — describe what takes place. They do
   not, on their own, claim the move is permitted. If the
   meta's point is acceptability, the bare passive is
   undershooting.

   Verbs that carry acceptability — "is an accepted move,"
   "passes as," "counts as," "reads as legitimate," "is
   sanctioned," "is permitted," "is allowed to," "is treated
   as fair game" — make the social-permission claim explicit.

   "Common" and "commonly" fall into the same trap: they
   describe frequency when the point is permission. Replace
   with "accepted," "permitted," "treated as legitimate,"
   "standard," or "sanctioned."

   Self-test: read the meta and ask — am I claiming this
   happens, or am I claiming this is allowed to happen? If
   the latter, the verb has to say so. A bare "is answered
   with" makes the weaker claim and quietly undershoots the
   actual point.

4. Soundness.
   The entry's claim follows from the evidence cited or reachable.
   Claims that go beyond what the evidence supports get trimmed
   back or hedged in-place.

5. Defensibility.
   A specialist could look up the cited or implied evidence and
   find no fatal weakness. Entries depending on a single contested
   study are flagged. Entries that would not survive an honest
   cross-examination are revised before release.

6. Grounding.
   Every entry except a raw observation cites the entries it builds
   on, in a "Draws from:" line. Raw observations — direct empirical
   or discourse-pattern claims that are not derived from another
   entry — do not need a draws-from line. Everything else does.

7. Concrete grounding.
   Comparison-words like "mirror," "reverse," "parallel," and
   "asymmetric" do not carry their content on their own. If an
   entry uses one, it illustrates the comparison directly. Not
   "the mirror move is not standard" but "the mirror move — saying
   X about women — is not standard."

8. No bare assertion.
   An observation without an example, a number, or a named instance
   is too weak to release. Discourse-pattern observations get a
   typical-utterance example. Statistical observations get a number
   with a source. Legal-framework observations get a specific
   statute, policy, or case.

   The example has to demonstrate the claim, not just label it.
   Giving the name of a phenomenon ("concrete instance:
   'whataboutism'") is not a demonstration of how the phenomenon
   works — it requires the reader to already know what the name
   refers to. A demonstration shows the move in action: a typical
   utterance, the response it draws, and (for asymmetry claims)
   the parallel move in the other direction going unflagged. If
   the reader has to know the term to grasp the example, the
   example isn't doing its job.

9. Relatable examples.
   An aspect of demystification is that the reader should
   recognize every example, analogy, or named instance on first
   read, without specialized background. The test is stronger
   than "the reader has heard of this." The example should land
   for a reader who has direct or close-second-hand experience
   with the situation it describes, not just one who recognizes
   the words.

   Strong source-pools — things almost every adult has been in
   or argued about: driving, paying bills, eating and cooking,
   chores at home, family arguments, school, working a job,
   texts and emails, mainstream news and TV. Examples drawn
   from here usually land.

   Weak source-pools that look stronger than they are:
   experiences specific to a slice of readers — unpaid
   internships, graduate school, particular industries, court
   proceedings, niche sports, fashion or gaming subcultures.
   These can feel "everyday" to a writer who lives near them,
   while a reader without that background has no purchase on
   them. The unpaid-internship vs. trial-shift analogy is a
   case in point: it sounds universal but the underlying
   experience is narrow.

   When a precise term is needed (e.g. "parasocial"), pair it
   with a plain gloss ("parasocial — feeling like you personally
   know a creator you only watch online"). The same rule applies
   to research-field references: name the finding in plain words
   rather than leaning on the field name to do the work.

   Self-check on any candidate example: would it land for a
   reader who never went to college, never worked in a corporate
   office, lives in a different country? If the example only
   works for readers like the writer, it fails this rule.

A pre-release self-check on each entry runs through 1–9. If any
fail, rewrite.
