META OBSERVATIONS

What they are
================================================================================

A meta observation is a general claim that emerges from reading the
source observations — either by looking at one closely and asking
what's really going on in it, or by noticing the same pattern across
several. The source observations describe specific patterns; the
meta observation names what the pattern is about.

The framework is built in layers. Each layer's meta observations
become the next layer's observations: today's observations are
level 1, the meta observations derived from them become level 2, a
future meta layer would be level 3. The terminology in this doc is
relative — "source observation" means whichever entries are directly
below the layer being built.

Each source observation can possibly carry more than one meta
observation. Reading the source closely and asking "why is this
happening?" surfaces several distinct angles — the form of the move,
the role of the speaker, the role of the listener, what's being
treated as evidence, where the asymmetry sits. Each angle that holds
up on its own becomes a meta observation.

How to derive them
================================================================================

Walk through each source observation clause by clause. Each clause
that introduces a new aspect typically yields a separate meta
observation.

For each clause:
  1. Read it.
  2. Name the angles the clause touches. A clause is rarely about
     one thing. It will name or imply several aspects at once: the
     speaker, the listener, what's being said, how it's being said,
     who is in the room, what is being treated as evidence, what is
     not being asked, where an asymmetry sits, which side a move
     runs in, what time period or population is in scope. Before
     asking why, list the angles. Each angle that holds up on its
     own is a candidate for a separate meta.
  3. For each angle, ask two questions:
       a. "Why is this happening?" — the answer is the underlying
          rule, the implicit assumption, or the social permission
          that makes the move work. This is the move that takes
          you from the source layer to the meta layer above it.
       b. "What's the general form?" — strip the specific noun
          and ask what rule the specific case is one instance of.
          A specific clause about emotional displays in political
          debate may be one case of a more general rule about
          which speakers are credited and which are discounted.
     The two questions are not the same. Why digs for cause;
     general form abstracts to rule. Run both, and a single angle
     can yield more than one meta when they answer differently.
  4. If the answer is not already a meta observation in this
     section, add it as a new one in plain language.
  5. If the answer is already there, add the current source ID to
     that meta observation's "Draws from" line, and note the merge
     in the parenthetical so the merge reasoning travels with the
     meta.
  6. If a later source entry refines an existing meta observation,
     edit the meta observation; keep its ID stable.

"Why" goes one level at a time. Asking why of a source observation
gives you a meta observation. Asking why of the meta observation you
just wrote would give you a meta-meta observation — that's the next
layer up, out of scope while you are still building the current one.

Show the work. While drafting, write the interrogation in parentheses
before each meta observation: which clause is being read, which angle
is being interrogated, what was asked, and (when relevant) why this
is being kept separate from a similar meta or merged into it. The
parentheticals are not just a transparency layer — they are a working
notebook. Treat them as a place to record live thinking, not only
finished derivations. Strip them after release or keep them as a
transparency layer.

Several meta observations per source entry is normal. If a source
entry yields only one meta observation, the interrogation was too
coarse — go back, list the angles, and walk it again.

Things the parentheticals carry beyond the why-derivation
================================================================================

The parenthetical is also where in-flight thinking lives. The
following are legitimate parenthetical content even when they do not
yield a meta on the spot.

- Open questions and surfaced tensions. While reading a clause, a
  counter-case or unresolved question may surface — "but doesn't
  this run into X?" or "this seems to contradict the move in DDm5
  — does it?" Write it down in the parenthetical. It may seed a
  future raw observation, get resolved by a later meta, or expose
  a refinement the current meta needs. Losing the thought because
  the meta itself is "clean" wastes the work.

- Audit flags. If the source observation itself is unclear — the
  example does not land, the quoted utterance is doing more work
  than it should, or the writer is not sure what the example is
  meant to demonstrate — flag it in the parenthetical with a
  short note ("not sure what example this refers to — audit"). A
  shaky source produces a shaky meta. The flag is a signal to
  revisit the source, not to silently force a meta out of it.

- Merge reasoning. When two angles or two clauses converge on the
  same meta, the parenthetical records the convergence ("this is
  the same rule DDm4 named — adding DD2 to its draws-from"). When
  two near-identical metas are kept separate, the parenthetical
  records the distinction ("same shape as DDm12 but the move is
  motive-questioning rather than factual-error treatment, so
  separate"). Without the merge reasoning, the next reader cannot
  tell whether the split is principled or accidental.

Things to avoid
================================================================================

- Academic vocabulary. The writing-goal rules apply at every layer.
  Plain words, low cognitive load, no jargon when a common word
  works. If a sentence reads like a journal abstract, rewrite.

- Compound claims. Each meta observation is one clean claim. If two
  distinct claims are joined by "and," split them.

- Quoting the source observation. The meta stands on its own. The
  source supports it; the "Draws from" line is the link.

- Depending on neighboring metas. The meta also stands on its own
  with respect to the other metas in the same section. A reader
  who lands on a single meta out of order should understand it
  without reading the meta above it. The "Draws from" line links
  back to the source — it does not excuse the meta from being
  readable on its own.

  The orphan-determiner test: scan the meta for any phrase of the
  form "the X," "this X," "that X," "the same X," "such X." Ask
  whether the reader knows what X is from inside the meta alone.
  "The offense," "the cost," "the asymmetry," "the pattern," "the
  decline," "this discomfort," "the same setting," "the mirror
  move" — each one points at something a previous entry defined.
  If the meta is the reader's first contact with X, X has to be
  named inside the meta. "The offense" becomes "enough to be read
  as cold or dismissive." "The cost" becomes "the cost of managing
  the other party's feelings." "The asymmetry" becomes the
  asymmetry, named: "the asymmetric reception of male and female
  emotional displays."

  Read each finished meta aloud as if it were the first sentence
  of an article. If a phrase needs the previous sentence to
  resolve, rewrite.

- Softening. The hard-on-topic / soft-on-reader rule applies. If the
  meta observation states something contested or uncomfortable, keep
  it plain and let the reader meet it directly.

- Restating the source. A meta observation that just rephrases the
  source isn't a meta observation — it's a paraphrase. The meta has
  to add a layer: the underlying rule, the implicit assumption, the
  general form.

  Generalizing the specific to the abstract is still paraphrase.
  Turning "'whataboutism' is named when an opponent does it and
  unnamed when one's own side does it" into "a rhetorical move is
  named when an opponent does it and unnamed when one's own side
  does it" is the same claim with a placeholder swapped for the
  noun. It doesn't answer why the asymmetry exists — and the why
  is what the meta is supposed to add. Self-test on a draft meta:
  if the source's specific noun were swapped back in, would the
  meta become indistinguishable from the source? If yes, it's a
  paraphrase, not a meta. The meta has to say something the
  source doesn't — typically the why behind it.

- Recursing. Asking why of the meta observation you just wrote
  produces meta-meta — the next layer up, out of scope while
  building the current one.

Format
================================================================================

Each meta observation has an ID: the section's two-letter prefix +
lowercase "m" + a number (e.g. DDm1, DPm3, VOm12). IDs are stable
anchors, like the source-layer IDs.

Each meta observation reads as one short prose claim, followed by a
"Draws from:" line listing the source entries that ground it.
Cross-references to other sections use the same convention.

The meta layer is appended to each section file, separated by this
header:

  ============================================================
  META OBSERVATIONS
  Generalizations that surface from reading the observations
  above. Each is a claim of its own; the "Draws from" line names
  the source entries that ground it. When a later source entry
  reinforces a meta observation already on the page, the entry
  is added to its "Draws from" rather than starting a new one.
  ============================================================

Self-check
================================================================================

Before releasing a meta layer:
  - A smart non-expert understands each meta observation on first
    read.
  - A specialist reads it and does not wince — nothing over-claims,
    nothing is sloppy with the source evidence cited.
  - No meta observation hedges without saying anything.
  - No meta observation is just the source rephrased.
  - Each source clause has been walked angle by angle, not asked a
    single generic "why?". If a clause yielded one meta and the
    clause names more than one aspect (speaker + listener,
    speaker + content, move + asymmetry, etc.), the interrogation
    was too coarse.
  - Audit flags raised in the parentheticals are either resolved
    (the source has been revised, or the example sharpened) or
    carried forward as known follow-ups.
If any fail, rewrite.
