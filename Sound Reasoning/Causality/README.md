# What Is "Truth", and How Do We Get to It?

How can we distinguish between truth and falsehood — by the same standards we use to distinguish truth anywhere else?

This site is an interactive map of that question. It presents a structured argument that starts from the ground up — from what we directly observe and experience — and reasons forward, step by step, using the same logic that governs every reliable field of inquiry. Each step narrows the space of possible answers by removing methods that can be used equally well to justify both truth and falsehood. What remains, after all those filters are applied, is the conclusion: Islam is the only candidate that remains distinguishable from falsehood at every level.

The argument is not a linear essay. It's a hierarchy of connected claims, where each claim depends on the ones beneath it, and each can be opened, examined, and challenged independently. The structure is the point — it makes the reasoning transparent so that anyone, regardless of background, can follow it, verify it, or identify exactly where and why they disagree.


## The Point

Discussions about God, religion, and truth tend to go in circles. People talk past each other, argue from different assumptions, and walk away exactly where they started. This usually happens because the actual point of disagreement is buried — hidden behind vague language, emotional reactions, or layers of complexity that neither side has the patience or the tools to untangle.

This map exists to fix that.

The goal is to make discussions productive by making them *accountable*. Every claim here is traceable — it depends on specific prior claims, which depend on specific observations, all the way down to things you can verify from your own experience. When you disagree, you aren't just disagreeing with a conclusion. You're disagreeing with a specific step, and that step has a visible cost: what your rejection commits you to, and whether you're willing to pay it.

This changes the nature of a conversation. Instead of "I believe X" against "I believe Y," it becomes: "I reject this specific filter — here is why — and I accept the consequences of that rejection." That's a discussion worth having. It can actually go somewhere, because both sides can see the structure of the disagreement and address it directly rather than talking past each other.

The map also removes the excuse of complexity. Topics like epistemology, metaphysics, and comparative theology are often treated as inaccessible to normal people — the kind of thing you need a degree to have an opinion about. But the reasoning here starts from everyday observations and builds in plain language. If you can follow a chain of "if this, then that," you can follow this argument. The hierarchy does the organizational work so you don't have to hold the whole thing in your head at once.

Ultimately, the intended use is simple: engage with the argument honestly, locate your position, and take responsibility for it. If you agree with the conclusion, you now understand how it's grounded. If you disagree, you now know exactly where, and what that disagreement requires of you. Either way, the conversation moves forward — and that is the point.


## How the Argument Is Organized

The map works in layers. The top level shows the main claims — the broadest conclusions of each section. These are connected by arrows that show how one conclusion feeds into the next, forming the chain of reasoning from foundational observations all the way to the final result.

Each claim can be expanded by clicking its header. This reveals the internal reasoning behind that claim: observations drawn from everyday experience, inferences built on those observations, and conclusions organized into tabs that break the logic down piece by piece. This is where the actual evidence and argumentation lives.

Many claims also have a **Derivation →** button. Clicking it opens a dedicated page showing the sub-claims that build up to that conclusion. Each of those sub-claims can itself be expanded or derived further. The argument goes as deep as it needs to — some claims resolve in a few observations, others unfold across multiple levels.

The hierarchy mirrors the argument's own logic: fundamental, widely shared observations sit at the bottom, and progressively higher-level conclusions sit above them. A reader who only wants the overview can read the top-level claims and still follow the logical skeleton. A reader who wants to verify can trace any conclusion back to its foundations.


## Navigating the Map

**Breadcrumbs** at the top of the page show where you are in the hierarchy. Each breadcrumb is a link — click any one to jump back to that level. The browser's back and forward buttons also work normally.

On derivation pages, **navigation links** appear above and below the content showing sibling claims — previous steps, next steps, and parallel steps in the reasoning — so you can move through the argument laterally without having to go back up to the parent page.


## Search

The search bar in the header lets you find claims anywhere in the map. Results appear grouped by their parent page, with matching text highlighted in gold. Each group can be collapsed or expanded, and clicking a result takes you to that claim's location.

A filter dropdown beside the search bar controls how search works:

- **Search node contents** — includes the full internal reasoning of each claim, not just headlines. On by default.
- **Search all pages** — searches the entire map. On by default.
- **Search only current page** — restricts results to the page you're viewing.

Results are capped at 100 for performance. If you hit the cap, a notice suggests refining your query.


## Agreement Tracking

This is where the map becomes more than a document — it becomes a tool for thinking through the argument yourself.

Every claim has agree/disagree buttons. When you disagree with a claim, that claim and everything that depends on it turns red — meaning: if this claim is wrong, everything built on it is also in question. Everything before the disagreed claim turns green, indicating you accept the reasoning up to that point.

You can then agree with a claim further downstream to narrow the red zone. This pinpoints the root of your disagreement to the space between the two marked claims — making it precise rather than vague.

This propagation works across pages. If a sub-claim deep in a derivation is marked red, its parent claim turns red too, and the effect cascades upward through the hierarchy.

A **Your Positions** panel appears below the header whenever you have active votes. It lists every claim you've explicitly agreed or disagreed with, each as a link that navigates to that claim. A **Reset All** button clears everything (with a confirmation prompt). Your votes are saved in your browser and persist across sessions.


## Export

The export button in the header downloads the current view as a JSON file — either the claims on the current page (in visual layout order) or the current search results. The file uses the same format as the site's source data.


## Other Features

A **dark/light theme toggle** in the header switches color schemes. Your preference persists across sessions. The layout is **responsive** — parallel claims stack vertically on narrow screens, and the header collapses into a menu on mobile. The interface is **keyboard accessible** — Tab to navigate, Enter or Space to activate, Escape to close an expanded claim.
