## His statement (in his voice, passed 2026-09-09)

**Context, quickly.** I'm building a phenomenological grounding: a record of nodes, each making one minimal claim read directly off experience. PP1 says "Phenomena occur." PP2 says "Distinct phenomena occur." Each node introduces exactly one new concept, must use only terms granted by earlier nodes, and is tested by assuming its opposite and watching that break. PP2 lists PP1 under "Inherits From". The question that's been bugging me is what that link actually *is*.

**1. Both nodes come from reality — so what is the link?** PP1 was grounded in/sourced from reality. PP2 was also grounded in/sourced from reality. But we keep saying PP2 is "grounded in" PP1 — whatever that means. In what sense does PP1 "allow" PP2, if I can just source PP2 in reality anyways? Is the link just conceptual — like the concept of PP2 uses the lego piece of PP1 to be valid? But then where did "distinct" come from, if not from PP1? And here's a wrinkle: in a way, "distinct" is also a thing that PP1 exposes too — PP1's own observation list (red, hot, ouch) already displays distinct things, and noticing a distinction is itself something that occurs, so distinctness was already inside "phenomena" somehow — if that makes sense. Like: "phenomena occur" in PP1 comes from reality/how our minds work; the "distinct" part of PP2 comes from reality/how our minds work; and the "phenomena occur" inside PP2 comes from both PP1 and reality… in different ways? What ways, exactly?

**2. If the links carry no truth, what are they for?** If both nodes come from reality, what's the point of grounding one in another — and what does that even mean, if this action is a *separate* action from accuracy with reality? What benefit do you gain from being able to audit a chain of claims in this structure, if the links between them are empty? What is the algebra of that link supposed to mean?

**3. The containment puzzle.** If each node is the set of all observations that satisfy its claim, then PP1 encompasses all observations, PP2 encompasses a subset of those, PP3 a subset of PP2, and so on — so the parent contains the child. But it's also said that the child "says everything the parent says, plus one more thing" — and that one more thing is still encompassed by the previous nodes, a manifestation of them. My example: if I say "something is in the box" because I see something blurry from far away, versus "an apple is in the box" because I walked closer and the blurriness became defined — the second sentence may encompass the terms of the first plus an additional term, but the first sentence encompasses all observations that could ever be made about what's in the box. And the "newness" of the second isn't really a new word — it's the new context the observation manifests in. So which contains which, and what exactly is the "one more thing"?

**4. Why one difference per node — and why this process at all?** Why one difference in each node, not two or three? And why can't I just start with "distinctions occur", or a later node? The second observation didn't come from the first — it came from reality, just as the first did. So where does this whole approach come from — what makes the one-thing-at-a-time requirement necessary rather than a preference?

**5. The feeling that this is discovery, not construction.** It's almost like, just as we roam the earth and discover new science, we're roaming our own minds and discovering their features. The effort feels like exposing the primitive, fundamental building blocks of all concepts, in some order, starting with the concepts that just appear — where PP1 covers all possible concepts, and everything downstream of PP2 will ultimately require the concept of "distinction". I keep reaching for "conceptual grounding" or "conceptual coverage". Is that right? And what fills the blank in "in ___ order"?

**6. Is the transition to inference seamless?** Projecting ahead: at some point we'll have established what logic, reasoning, will, reality, ignorance, and the rest all are — and then we start making inductions (using what is seen to uncover what is unseen), and then deducing from those seen and unseen observations. Isn't that a seamless transition, if the next node after a set of parents only adds one thing at a time? Does that mean one underlying form of reasoning is used throughout, manifesting as grounding here and inference there — or what? Relatedly: isn't going from a known concept to another exactly what inference is? If inference is "the second statement is true because the first is true", I have a hard time distinguishing that from whatever justifies the jump from PP1 to PP2 — both feel like "because the parent", in the sense that you can't have the child without the existence of the parent. In both cases the parent contains the child: "phenomena" encompasses "distinction".

**7. What I want out of this thread.** A term and a telling. The form of reasoning between nodes, exposed properly: a name that a person new to this project and a seasoned philosopher would both grasp immediately, and a plain, human-friendly explanation of what it is, why the one-concept rule follows from it, why the order is what it is, and how it relates to the induction and deduction that come later. The main thread has offered pieces — "specification" (too light), "exposure", a split between "sourcing" (claim → reality) and "grounding" (concept → concept) — and they help some, but none of it has landed as the exposed thing itself; it feels like my confusion getting labeled rather than dissolved. All these questions are related to each other for sure — I want the one picture they are all views of.
