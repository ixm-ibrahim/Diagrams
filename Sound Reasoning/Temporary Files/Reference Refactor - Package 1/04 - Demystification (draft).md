# 04 Demystification

Why and how everything in this project is written — nodes, prompts to threads, returns, chat.

## Why

Everything here is written to keep the reader's cognitive load low: the reader's effort goes to the point itself, not to decoding the telling — however complex the topic.

Some complexity is genuinely in the topic. The treatment for that is the right breakdown: trace the topic to its roots and present it one graspable piece at a time, so no step asks the reader to hold more than one new thing. And where two stances clash, expose the exact factor that separates them — the reader should be able to say what would make one true and the other false, instead of feeling a vague difference.

But most complexity a reader meets is not in the topic — it is in the telling. It appears when:

- the speaker didn't break the topic down for the listener;
- the speaker can't break the topic down for themselves — they never traced it to its roots, so they don't truly understand what they are saying;
- the complexity is doing work: dressing something up as impossible to tackle, or as deeper than it is — like a contradiction presented (knowingly or not) as profound wisdom, by someone unable to distinguish the ignorance that comes from wisdom from the lack of knowledge that comes from falsehood;
- technical vocabulary is layered over the point until the reader assumes the incomprehensibility is their own failing rather than the speaker's;
- someone wants the audience in the dark, and presents information in the way that keeps them there;
- or the topic arrives pre-wrapped in bad framing from how it is usually told, and getting it right is like translating out of a foreign language — the bad words and framings removed, the correct ones put in their place.

Demystified writing breaks all of that: the topic's own complexity gets broken down, and the telling adds none of its own. The concept is presented so plainly that any reader can grasp it, tackle it productively, and reach a clear, objective outcome. That takes two things at once. First, the right breakdown of the topic itself — its actual structure, traced to its roots. Second, language that transmits that breakdown without loss: precise enough that a specialist would agree it is accurate, and plain enough that the density never persuades the reader to stop reading. Hard on the topic, soft on the reader — everyday words, full flowing sentences, ordinary examples, written as if the reader has never seen the project before. The thinking mistakes that this kind of writing keeps meeting are cataloged in [[05 - Common Biases]].

## The rules

- **Demystify the concept before the language**. Demystification has two layers, and both must pass.
	- First the concept: clear, correct, and in its right place — a wrong or misplaced concept stays a problem in any wording ("we changed the language of this sentence quite a few times in previous threads, but the conceptual issue was still there").
	- Then the language: plain and precise — dense telling makes even a clear concept appear difficult to grasp. Sound, clear logic is the highest priority of the whole effort; the logic helper in [[01 - Processing Queries]] checks this layer, and the worked examples are cases 4, 17, 19, and 21 in [[11 - Sentence Case Studies]].
	- When reporting a problem, say which layer it is in: "the thought is off" (the concept) or "the wording is off" (the language).
- **Everyday words.** If a common word does the job, the fancy word is wrong.
	- Bad: "If your foundation is analytically true, it is unfalsifiable and therefore empirically empty."
	- Good: "A statement that is true by definition gets its truth from how we chose to use words, not from whether or not it aligns with reality — teaching you vocabulary, not facts about anything."
- **A specialized term gets its plain meaning right where it is used.**
	- Bad: "This may be a Carnapian pseudo-statement."
	- Good: "A philosopher named Carnap called sentences like that pseudo-statements — grammatically fine, but conceptually empty."
- **An example appears wherever a point is invisible without one**, in parentheses or a sub-bullet.
	- Bad: "Tautologies carry no information." (The reader nods along without seeing why.)
	- Good: "This isn't a discovery; it's a tautology (a statement with no significant information) — like saying 'a three-sided shape has three sides'."
- **No metaphorical labels, no invented code words, no courtroom or drama phrasing.** Call each thing by its plain description every time, even when that is longer.
	- Bad: "The claim survives the terminus test." (The reader needs a private dictionary — what is "the terminus"? Other examples: "the floor", "the compass", etc. — each one adds to the cognitive load of understanding.)
	- Good: "No rule of reasoning was found that stands without this rule."
- **No sentence points at an earlier item by number or placeholder alone** — restate in a few words what the item said.
	- Bad: "Commitment 3 says a necessary part was stripped away." (Which commitment? What part?)
	- Good: "The third commitment says this node stripped away a necessary part — the experiencer — and kept only the happening."
		- Objections may be referenced by their number, with a brief header as needed, to avoid depending on the reader to memorize them.
- **No ambiguity.** If a sentence can be read as saying something wrong, rewrite it until it can't — check each sentence against the misreading a fresh reader might bring.
	- Bad: "The demand 'give me something that could be proven false' is the right demand for ordinary claims." (Reads as: claims must be provably false to be valid — self-defeating, and not what was meant.)
	- Good: "A claim that can be compared with reality can be caught when it is wrong; a claim that cannot be compared with reality at all cannot be told apart from a made-up one."
- **A misreading caught in conversation gets fixed in the text, not just in the conversation.** The next reader brings the same assumptions the questioner did, and will misread the same spot the same way (the recurring ones are cataloged in [[05 - Common Biases]]).
	- Bad: the conversation clears the misreading up, and the page stays as it was — the fix reached one reader instead of all of them.
	- Good: the misread sentence gets rewritten so the misreading cannot happen. (A question revealed that "Who or what is experiencing these phenomena?" reads as if an experiencer certainly exists; the page line became "Whether an experiencer of these phenomena exists — and if one does, what it is.")
- **Bullets are bite-sized.** One graspable point per bullet; a paragraph-length bullet becomes a parent point with nested sub-bullets. Nesting — and every other piece of formatting — exists to make reading easier, not to decorate.
	- Bad: one bullet running six sentences, where the reader loses the point mid-paragraph and cannot skim.
	- Good: the point as the parent bullet, the steps as sub-bullets — the section can be skimmed by parents alone.
- **Make the relation between joined clauses explicit** — "but" for contradiction, "instead" for replacement, "so" for consequence, etc.
	- Bad: "The road was icy; the bus was late." (Does the ice explain the lateness, or are these two separate complaints? The reader must guess.)
	- Good: "The road was icy, so the bus was late."
- **The drifts to avoid:**
	- academic drift — jargon or Latinate words where a common word works ("utilize" for "use");
	- bland drift — hedging, which means wrapping a claim in so many maybes that it commits to nothing ("it could perhaps be argued that..." followed by nothing);
	- false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would object to ("quantum physics says anything is possible");
	- emotional cushioning that buries the actual point ("with all due respect, and acknowledging the many strengths of this view,..." before a one-line objection);
	- weight drift — a phrase carrying more connotation than the point requires, so the reader spends effort deciding which reading was meant ("gets heard as" → "reads as"; "the standard move is to X" reads as "this always happens" when what is meant is only that the move is accepted).
- **Where accessibility and precision genuinely conflict in a sentence, flag that sentence to the author** rather than silently picking one.

## The watch-list

The words and phrases to keep out of the writing — the AI's habitual tells, the banned and conditionally banned words, and the accepted replacement for each — live in [[10 - Watch-list]], one entry per pattern and per word. In short: banned words and patterns are swapped or deleted on sight; conditionally banned words are swapped directly in chat, and in node text every sentence containing one is listed for the author to rule on.

## The self-check before finishing anything

- The concept itself is clear, correct, and in its right place — no rewording rescues a wrong or misplaced thought.
- A smart non-expert understands it on first read.
- A specialist reads it and does not wince.
- No sentence wraps itself in maybes while committing to nothing.
- No sentence supports a wrong reading — including any misreading already caught in conversation: the fix is in the text.
- Every point that is invisible without an example has one.
- No reference forces the reader to scroll back to decode it.
- Every section can be skimmed by its parent bullets alone.
- No watch-list word is left doing no work.
- Formatting follows the standards — the same kind of thing gets the same mark every time.

If any of these fail, rewrite.
