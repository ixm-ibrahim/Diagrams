# Part 1 — The first message: the four forms, the principles, the regress

## His picture, represented back

He has one path: awareness → induction → abduction → deduction. Observations "just happen in awareness"; "we can't choose what to be aware of", and "there's no 'wrong' or 'false' awareness at this level, only the 'how' you interpret and understand those observations." The most basic form of reasoning, in his words, is that a child "is a manifestation (ie. a new awareness) that satisfies the scope of its parents" — "phenomena occur" parent to "distinctions occur", parent to "qualia occur", parent to "will occurs". Inductions are built from observations, cannot be seen the way red is seen, and come unwilled like red: "my mind is going to automatically say 'those birds are defined as being white'." He caught himself partway through the example: the gravity step "actually looks like deduction", so "the induction here is the 'for all things' part... the projection part", and projections come from awareness — the felt pattern of will moving the body "matches patterns in reality", and the felt pattern of effort is how energy is discovered. The tension: a child is a scoped manifestation of its parents, yet in induction "the child is a scope around its parents", so "either these definitions are wrong, or they are true in different senses." Between parents and child each form has a "mechanism" — projection, selection, and for the first form maybe understanding or meaningfulness, "since 'meaning = concept + context' and 'understanding = occurrence of meaning'" — and for every form he asks where its own principle comes from, offering as a first one "a method of reasoning that justifies a falsehood can't tell what is true from what is not", which "would satisfy itself." The condition on any answer: "whatever I figure out has to align with how I found it, without causing a fatal regress."

## The one picture in everyday words

Awareness comes first and is not picked: red, a wanting, a thought occur, and at that level none of them is false — only what is said about them can be. The first thing reasoning does is what his 1.1 says: a new awareness occurs inside the scope of concepts already held — this movement is one of the falls already known; this red round thing is one of the sweet ones. That is a meaning occurring (a concept in a context), and a meaning occurring is what understanding is, so his candidate for the first form's mechanism is right. It happens before words: the infant has it at the strawberry. Such an awareness gets written down as a statement ("this apple fell"); the statement claims only what occurred, so the one occurrence confirms it whole; and it is at the written statement, where a context is claimed ("a flying pig occurred outside my imagination"), that a falsehood first becomes possible. Looking upward instead of downward, the same move notices what several gathered occurrences share and labels and states the shared pattern: falling occurs. Induction adds one thing to that meaning occurring: it holds the pattern out past what occurred in awareness — to every unsupported thing not yet seen, or from a felt inside (effort, causing) into an outside thing — and only the part held out owes further looking. When two projections fit every gathered observation and part at a case not yet placed, abduction is the holding of one against the other before that case is placed; what could prefer one is open, and no reason that separates the two has been found here. Deduction reads two held statements together for what they say about one case and adds no claim of its own: if every unsupported thing falls toward the earth, and the moon is unsupported, the moon's path bends toward the earth.

Each form's product must pass a standard; for three of the four a standard has been written. Two of those standards have been shown to stop the question "what supports it" by their denials clashing in their own words: his principle (a method that justifies a falsehood cannot tell true from false) and deduction's (a case of P is a case of Q when every P case is among Q's). Each of the two is a defining clause — it teaches what the words ask, the way "a three-sided shape has three sides" does, and states no fact about any occurrence — so each refuses a method only together with a falsehood found among what it accepted (seen at a case, or shown by laying the method's own premise and conclusion side by side), as "bachelors are unmarried" excludes John only together with "John is a bachelor". Placement's and projection's standards read as defining clauses of the same kind and have not been walked (a walk is one worked example laid out in file 0's seven rows); abduction's preference has no standard yet. So what is shown is this: for two principles, the sequence "what supports the principle" stops at a statement whose denial clashes in its own words, as the sequence "what makes the first statement true" stops at what occurs (PP1). What is open is whether the first kind of stop has the standing of the second, or is only what any defining clause's denial does. And the forms were found the way they describe — by noticing what occurs when reasoning happens and testing each statement by its denial — with one projection inside the finding: the claim that these four moves are all the moves, which owes cases like any projection (a fifth move found would break it). That is his alignment condition, and it passes as far as the finding has been shown.

## His questions, each answered

**The opening path** — "observation/awareness --> induction --> abduction --> deduction." It holds as an order of what each form draws from, not as a line walked once. He is right that the first deductions cannot rest on earlier deductions; what they rest on is not induction. "'Triangle' means a three-sided shape" is a defining clause; "this shape has three sides, counted just now" is a placement; together they force "this shape is a triangle", and there is no projection anywhere in that sequence — no premise speaks of a case not yet seen. ## How he gets to these questions, and how to hold them without being overwhelmed

The question pattern, in his terms, in his order:
1. Where does this form's product come from — what are its parents? ("they are built 'from' observations"; "the parents are inductions".)
2. What is the mechanism between parents and child? ("projection", "selection or preference".)
3. How to choose between competing ones? ("how to choose between competing them?")
4. Where does the form's own principle come from — deduced, abducted, or induced? ("where do the principles of abductions themselves come from?")
5. Does the answer to 4 use the form it explains, and does that pass? ("such an induction would satisfy itself"; "align with how I found it".)

Added by the candidates, not his: between 2 and 3, what does the child claim past its parents — which observations does it still owe?

Why the questions feel like too many. First, two questions asked at once: what the form is (steps 1–3, its walk) and where its principle comes from (steps 4–5); his own sentence states both halves — "simultaneously figure out what these forms of reasoning are, as well as where they come from." Second, one question asked four times with a menu that leaves out a source: step 4 at observation (his answer: bad question), induction, abduction, deduction, each time with "deduced, abducted, or induced" — while his own point 1 had already given the missing source, "noticed directly", and PP1 supplies the test for what is noticed (deny it, watch the denial). Third, the fusion of the move with the standard: "where does the doing come from" (it occurs, unwilled — a phenomenon, PP1 covers it) and "what says whether the product is right" (a principle) are two questions about one item; the first has his own answer — a bad question — and only the second regresses.

The working order:
1. One example through all four forms (the apple; the moon's path as the parting case); a second example only where this one cannot show the point — as the case of the wanting and the arm and energy were needed for the transfer.
2. Walk each form through file 0's seven rows, holding every row identical between neighboring walks except the move row, where that is possible; where it is not (rows 0, 1, 1b), say which rows change.
3. Write each form's principle as one line — a claim about when the move comes out true — with true and false outcomes as its gathered observations.
4. Two tests kept apart: does the line claim past the gathered observations? What does its denial do (breaks by itself occurring — PP1's kind; clashes in its own words; is answered by one look; stays open until cases occur — a projection)?
5. Only then ask step 5, once: which form did steps 3–4 use, and does its own denial break itself, and which way. (Applied above: two defining clauses and one projection found in the finding.)

The one example discipline: a distinction with the same example fitting both sides is two labels on one relation; so every distinction is drawn on one item, and the item is the same apple throughout.

### Task 3 — B's two shapes

## Merged answer to his second message

You use "abduction" for preferring among rival accounts that all fit the same observations — "choosing between competing inductions." The video used it for a step with one account in view: humans walk on two legs, Polly walks on two legs, so Polly is a human.

**Is abduction not about preference?** Yours is. By your line that the divide is drawn in awareness, a preferring is an occurrence and needs two things present. The video's speaker held one statement, so no preferring occurred. Parrots and kangaroos are in the circle of two-legged walkers whether or not anyone thinks of them — that is why the step is a guess and not forced, and it fixes what the guess owes: one look at Polly in a property that fixes "human." But it does not make the step a selection. What occurred was a placement made on a property taken as fixing when it does not fix.

**Are you using the word differently?** Yes. A philosopher named Peirce introduced the word (first as "hypothesis") for the video's shape: a general statement plus one seen property, to a case. Later philosophers use it for preferring among rival explanations — your sense — and both uses are current.

**Are these already in your forms?** "Polly is not a human" from the chromosome count is a forced deduction: outside a circle every human is inside, so outside "human." "John is a human" is a placement if two legs and the count fix the label, a guess if they only ride along with it — your fundamental against superficial question. "Polly is a human" from two legs alone is neither forced nor a preferring: one dot moved into a narrower circle whose fixing properties were not seen — a placement said before the fit to "human" was seen. It claims Polly's unseen properties the way "every swan is white" claims unseen swans. It sounds like deduction (general statement plus particular), categorization (a dot under a label), and induction (it claims past the seen) because it shares one part with each.

Where it belongs is the one thing for you to rule on. **Is induction your broad line ("an unseen observation that explains the seen observations") or your refined line ("the 'for all things' part — the projection part")?** If broad: the video's step is your induction, induction has two directions (unseen members; one item's unseen properties), and abduction as preferring among inductions covers both the gravity case and the egg case. If refined: the video's step is a fourth kind of statement — one dot claimed past the seen — needing its own word, and abduction either widens past inductions or gets a second row for preferring among such guesses.

Your bachelor example does not force: "unmarried" holds women, boys, and widowers too. Fixed to "a bachelor is an unmarried man" plus "John is an unmarried man," it is a placement read off a definition; a deduction needs a child circle the dot is in only through a held statement — the cup that will fall, or Polly laying eggs because birds do.
