# 10 Sentence Case Studies

Case studies of sentences that failed the writing standard and how they got fixed (his instruction). The reason this file exists: the same failure shapes recur, and reading a diagnosis is faster than re-deriving it. [[09 - AI Thesaurus]] records which words became which; this file records the anatomy — what was actually wrong, and the lesson that carries to the next sentence. It grows with the work: every fix worth learning from gets a case.

Each case: Before → After, the problem, the lesson.

## Where to look when a sentence grates

The cases keep landing on the same few problems, at different depths. Go through these in order — each one is deeper than the last, and the deeper the problem, the more rewordings fail before it is found, so repeated failure is itself the signal to go a step deeper:

1. A word or phrase from the banned list is hiding inside the sentence (cases 1, 4, 12) — and sometimes the fix is deleting the clause around it, not replacing the word (case 1).
2. A word is standing in for something it does not say — find the ordinary act or the concrete thing it points at (cases 2, 7, 10, 14).
3. The sentence's machinery is broken: a contrast that does not contrast (case 5), a private convention assumed (case 6), an accusation or expectation left implicit (cases 9, 11), praise where content should be (case 12).
4. An image is doing the arguing — unpack it into its claim (case 8).
5. The thought itself is wrong or standing in the wrong place: an unobservable claim stated as a report, or a stand located in the wrong subject (case 4).

And at every depth: after a fix, reread the whole sentence — a replacement must fit the sentence's shape, not just the word's meaning (case 3).

## 1. Delete instead of replacing

- Before: "The demand's honest core — every claim must be checkable — is met here, not dodged."
- After: "…is answered here."
- The problem: a banned-list word ("dodged") inside a contrast that added nothing — the sentence already said the demand is answered.
- The lesson: often the fix is deleting, not swapping. A banned word can mark a clause that never earned its place.

## 2. Replace the private verb with the ordinary act

- Before: "you always meet occurrences as the one experiencing them" (two failed attempts: "reach you", "come across")
- After: "you never notice an occurrence except as the one experiencing it"
- The problem: "meet" had become a private verb for how things arrive in experience. The failed attempts swapped in near-synonyms of the private verb instead of asking what the person actually does.
- The lesson: find the ordinary act the doctrine-verb was standing in for — here, noticing — and say that.

## 3. A fix must fit the sentence's shape

- Before: "The angle is in the looking, not in the object. So the experiencer may be in the meeting, not in the occurring." (failed attempt: "in the experiencing")
- After: "…may be in the noticing, not in the occurring."
- The problem: the sentence lives on a parallel ("looking" / "—ing"); the first replacement broke the rhythm and read awkward even though the word was fine.
- The lesson: after substituting, reread the whole sentence — the replacement has to carry the sentence's shape, not just the word's meaning.

## 4. When rewording keeps failing, the thought is the problem — and the fix may be relocating the claim

- Before (five failed attempts on the objector's first commitment in the made-not-found objection):
 1. "Before attention (or language) does its work, experience carries no boundaries — the boundaries are imposed."
 2. "there are no boundaries in experience"
 3. "experience is one unbroken field — every line in it is imposed"
 4. "Nothing comes divided into this and that — attention (or language) does the dividing."
 5. "There is a way experience is before anyone attends to it: one undivided whole, where nothing is yet this rather than that."
- After (the author's relocation and second pass, polished): "Reality is one undivided whole: without you to tell this from that, nothing is actually this rather than that."
 - His second pass replaced "without your experience, nothing is yet this rather than that": the abstract noun ("your experience") became the concrete act (you, telling this from that), and "yet" became "actually" — "yet" smuggled in a time order (first undivided, then divided) that the objector never claims; the claim is about what is truly so, which "actually" says. The final polish swapped his "notice this from that" for "tell this from that" — "tell X from Y" is the ordinary idiom for telling apart, and it echoes the node's own line about the child who "tells strawberry from ouch".
- The problem had two layers.
 - First layer, found at attempt five by examining the claim instead of the words: the sentence asserts something nobody could ever observe, stated as if someone had seen it — a model of reality put in front of the observations (his diagnosis). A banned-list phrase ("does its work") was also hiding in the original, adding grate without being the source.
 - Second layer, found by him: every attempt located the claim in the wrong place. "Experience before attention" is an experience nobody experiences — a contradiction sitting inside the grammar — so every phrasing of it read garbled no matter which words were used. The objector's true stand was never about a strange kind of experience; it was about reality apart from experience. Relocated there, the sentence became sayable at once — and the reply survived unchanged, cleaner: the claim is still something nobody has ever checked, since all checking happens within experience.
- One micro-fix in the polish: his draft joined the clauses with "as" ("...whole, as without your experience..."), which frames the second clause as a reason for the first; but the second clause restates the first in this-and-that words, so a colon (restatement) fits where "as" (justification) misleads.
- The lesson: when a sentence stays awkward through several rewordings, stop polishing and examine the claim. When the claim reads self-garbled, ask where it actually stands — a stand located in the wrong subject cannot be written well in any words; moved to its true subject, it often writes itself. And once the subject is right, prefer the concrete act to the abstract noun, and watch small words for claims they smuggle in ("yet" carries a time order).

## 5. A contrast must contrast

- Before: "Whether they are found ready-made or arrive already shaped is a later question."
- After: "Whether anything — a mind, a language — helps shape the differences before they show up, or whether nothing shapes them at all."
- The problem: the two alternatives said the same thing ("ready-made" and "already shaped" both mean formed on arrival); the sentence's whole job was the contrast, and it had none.
- The lesson: read each alternative alone and check they actually differ; name the differing part (who shapes, or nobody).

## 6. Name the mechanism, not the convention

- Before: "that question needs words for time and repetition that do not exist yet"
- After: "…that no node has introduced yet"
- The problem: to a fresh reader the claim is absurd — English has words for time. The sentence leaned on the project's private convention (a word "exists" once a node introduces it) without naming it.
- The lesson: where an insider convention is doing the work, say the mechanism in the sentence.

## 7. Replace the metaphor with the concrete thing, not with another metaphor

- Before: "Where boundaries sit…" (failed attempt: "Where boundaries fall…")
- After: "Where one phenomenon ends and another begins, and whether that edge is ever sharp…"
- The problem: the first fix hunted for a better verb and found another metaphor; the abstraction was the problem, not the verb.
- The lesson: ask what the metaphor points at, and write that.

## 8. Unpack the image into its claim

- Before: "That is what makes it a show rather than a blank."
- After: "An illusion with nothing distinct in it would not even be an illusion — there would be nothing to it."
- The problem: the image (show, blank) was carrying the argument; a reader who does not take to the image gets nothing.
- The lesson: state the claim the image was decorating; keep an image only next to its plain statement.

## 9. Say what the accusation would be

- Before: "The steps are …, not padding"
- After: "…— not one thing re-said three times"
- The lesson: an abstract accusation ("padding") lands only when the concrete version is on the page.

## 10. A gloss must be plainer than what it glosses

- Before: "not about what is ultimately real (what is there in its own right, rather than as a form of something else)"
- After: "…(what is there on its own, not just as a form of something else)"
- The problem: an idiom ("in its own right") inside a parenthetical whose only job was to be plainer than the term it explains.
- The lesson: idioms are jargon to a fresh reader; in a gloss, only everyday words.

## 11. "Real X" usually hides an expectation — state it

- Before: "a real report of a difference should say what the difference is"
- After: "a report of a difference is expected to say what the difference is" (his steer: the sentence needed "expected")
- The lesson: "real/genuine X" often means "X that meets an expectation"; naming the expectation says more and drops the filler.

## 12. Name the contribution instead of praising it

- Before: "Naming that position shows the node does separate work."
- After: "Naming that position shows the node adds a claim of its own."
- The problem: "does work" as a verdict — approval without content.
- The lesson: say what the thing contributes; if that can't be said, the praise was hiding an empty spot.

## 13. The replacement can become the next private verb

- Before: eight sentences across one page leaning on "show up" ("before they show up", "distinctions already showing up", "no word shows up on its own authority", "the mind shapes experience before it ever shows up").
- After: each fixed to the actual event — "before you become aware of them", "already occurring", "appears on its own authority", "before you actually become aware of it" (his direction).
- The problem: "show up" was itself the replacement installed when "met" and "attend" were banned — and it quietly took over the same private job (a casual verb standing for "becomes present in awareness"), spreading through the page. A plain word used once is plain; the same word doing the same hidden job in eight sentences has become a term of art.
- The lesson: after adopting a replacement, sweep the page for it. If it is multiplying, it has become the new private verb — go back to the second question in the list above and name the actual event ("you become aware of it") or use the project's own core word ("occurs", "appears").
- Confirmed by the full language audit he ordered: the next two incarnations were already in the pages — "arrive" (five sentences in PP2) and "reach" (five across both pages, although case 2 had already rejected "reach" once as a failed fix for "met"). And the sanctioned replacements themselves had started multiplying: "become aware of" five times in one page, "is simply/plainly/already there" five times. The job keeps hiring new words; the sweep has to run every time.
- Confirmed again, this time before the spread: the "reach" → "notice" fixes would have put five "notice"s in one objection, so the ratified set varies them — "notice" where the act itself is the point, "know of" where the finding-out is (his ok). The sweep now runs on proposals, not just on pages.

## 14. The replacement must carry the meaning, not gesture at it — and match the register

- Before: "without naming what differs" (after "naming" was banned) → failed fix: "without spelling out what differs"
- After (from his candidates — explain, expose, identify, isolate, specify): "without specifying what differs"
- The problem, in his words: "spelling out" is a metaphor for the word's meaning rather than the meaning — the banned "naming" was actually closer, since it used its own meaning directly. And the idiom's register is wrong: "spelling out" is reserved for when explaining something is unusual, inconvenient, or a chore — a connotation the sentence never meant to carry.
- The lesson: when replacing a word, reach for a word whose own meaning is the meaning needed — not an idiom that gestures at it. And weigh the register: an idiom drags its usual situations along with it (this is the weight drift the writing rules already name, caught here inside a fix).

## 15. Mark a mentioned word as mentioned

- Before: "A child tells strawberry from ouch long before it has any words for taste or pain."
- After (his edit): "A child tells "strawberry" from "ouch" long before it has any words for taste or pain."
- The problem: "strawberry" and "ouch" are pointed at as phenomena, not used as words in the sentence's own voice — and the formatting rule already says every mentioned word or label gets double quotes; this spot had slipped past it.
- The lesson: when a sentence points at a word or a phenomenon as an item, quote it; when it uses the word normally, don't. Mentioning without the marks reads as using.

## 16. A restatement and its commitment move together — and watch the page's own terms

- Before: commitment "An observation does not count as complete until you can explain what you observed", restated the same way.
- After (his edit to the restatement, synced back into the commitment): "An observation can't be considered meaningful until you can explain what you observed."
- The problems (as read from his edit): (a) "does not count as" used "count" in a different sense than the page's own introduced term ("count" as a synonym of multiplicity — "no count is taken") — a homonym collision inside the very node that grants the word; (b) "complete" understated the objector's stand — their charge is that the unexplained observation means nothing, which "meaningful" says; (c) once he edited the restatement, the commitment had to follow, or the restate-what-it-said rule breaks.
- The lesson: when one half of a commitment-restatement pair changes, change the other; and avoid using a page's own introduced terms in homonym senses in that page's prose — the reader is learning the granted sense there. Related, from the same pass: he changed "exactly what the next node is for" to "exactly what the future nodes are for" — a deferral should not promise which unmade node answers a question unless that is actually settled.

## 17. If the author misreads it, the phrasing failed

- Before (PP1, the objector's second commitment, ratified): "A starting point has to stake something on how the world is — otherwise it carries no weight."
- What happened: editing PP1 later, the author read the sentence as demanding that a starting point describe the world, and rewrote it that way — which turned the commitment into something PP1 satisfies ("Phenomena occur" does describe the world), breaking the objection its reply answers. The original intent was the falsifiability demand: the starting point must be able to be wrong.
- The problem: "stake something on how the world is" was too oblique to survive its own author's later reading. A sentence that is technically correct but misread by the person who ratified it has failed as writing — the no-ambiguity rule's hardest test case. The same oblique phrase also lived in one of the writing rules' own good examples, spreading the pattern.
- The fix (pending his pick): state the demand so it cannot be read as met by the node — "A starting point has to be able to turn out wrong about the world — otherwise it holds nothing up." — with the reply bullet that restates it re-synced.
- The lesson: the reader whose misreading matters most is the author months later. If a wording needs its intent remembered to be read right, replace it with one that carries the intent on its face.

## 18. When a word gets banned, sweep the standards too

- Found (during his pass over PP1): the writing rules' own good example read "… the world was never consulted, so it reports nothing about the world" — two banned words inside the very example writers copy. Two more good examples carried "carry", "checking", "stakes something", and "in the meeting".
- The problem: an exemplar outranks a rule — the AI reproduces what the standard shows more faithfully than what it says. A banned pattern enshrined in a good example regenerates through every text written to that standard, which is part of why banned shapes kept reappearing in the pages.
- The fix: the good examples were rewritten to his current wordings; bad examples keep their flaws on purpose.
- The lesson: every ban and every new principle gets swept across the reference files' own examples, not just the pages — the standard must be the cleanest text in the project.

## 19. A placeholder object hides the criterion

- Before: "a claim must add something" (proposed as the fix for the banned "earn its keep"); the same shape in "might not be able to hold anything up".
- His diagnosis: a vagueness that is hard to describe and very common in AI talk — don't all claims add something? Even "a 'triangle' is a three-sided shape" adds a new term to use.
- The problem: the sentence is shaped like a test, but the test is missing — a bare placeholder ("something", "anything", "nothing") stands where the kind of thing should be, so the sentence sounds like a criterion while ruling nothing in or out. It is the emptiness of "does work" (case 12) one layer down: there the praise had no content; here the object has none. Linguists call the worn-down words themselves "semantically bleached".
- The fix, first attempt: say what kind, and to what — "a statement that only teaches vocabulary cannot support claims about what occurs". Turned back by him: the language was clean but the concept was wrong — a definition can also state reality-content (his example: "the sky is the atmospheric boundary" says the atmosphere has a boundary, and we call that boundary "sky"), so "only teaches vocabulary" mislocates the emptiness. See case 21 — the concept layer comes first. The third proposal was ratified (changes list, item 26), and with it he stated the principle behind the whole fix: a definition can be correct while the thing defined is not real ("a pegasus is a winged horse" is true by definition, and there are no pegasi), so the definitional structure by itself has no bearing on truth.
- The lesson: when a sentence's object is "something", "anything", or "nothing", ask "what kind?". If the sentence cannot answer, it was hiding an empty criterion. "anything" / "nothing" joined the banned list from this case.

## 20. A replacement must not prove too much

- Before: "choosing words settles nothing about the world" (after "settle" was banned) → failed fix: "choosing words changes nothing about the world".
- His catch: even a non-definition claim changes nothing about the world — "the kettle is hot" changes nothing about the kettle. The fix is true of every claim, so it separates nothing.
- The problem: the replacement widened the claim until the intended point (word-choices cannot make facts) disappeared — a sentence true of everything says nothing, which is the project's own emptiness test, here aimed at a fix.
- The fix: delete the broken clause and keep the concrete half already beside it — "The objector is right that you cannot make something exist by defining a term for it — a definition adds a label, not a fact." (case 1's lesson: often the fix is deleting, not swapping). Ratified by him — "exactly the framing that was missing".
- The lesson: test a replacement by asking what it rules out. If it is true of everything, the sentence stopped saying anything — reread every fix with the same suspicion as the original.

## 21. Demystify the concept before the language

- Before: PP1 objection 1's second commitment, reworded across threads — "A starting point has to stake something on how the world is" → his edit "has to actually describe how the world is" → the proposal "has to be able to turn out wrong about the world" — and still confusing at every pass, including to him.
- His diagnosis: demystification has two layers. If the concept is not clear or correct, you can demystify the language all you want — the same problem is there. If the language is dense and obtuse, even a perfectly clear concept will appear difficult to grasp. Here the concept was the problem, so no rewording could land.
- The concept problem, specifically: the demand that the claim be able to be wrong belongs to the neighboring objection ("what does it actually rule out?") — it is that objection. Split off into objection 1 as a lone commitment, it could only ever be a duplicate saying the same thing worse, so every wording of it read as either obvious or wrong.
- The fix (his choice): structural, not verbal — delete the commitment from objection 1, let objection 2 own the demand, and refer across in the ruled shape ("see ___, which addresses ___"). Exact edits in the PP1 changes list.
- A second example from the same pass: case 19's first fix — clean language, wrong concept ("only teaches vocabulary": a definition can also state reality-content).
- The lesson: before polishing a sentence, ask whether its concept is clear, correct, and in its right place. Related: case 4 (rewording keeps failing — examine the thought) and case 17 (the author's own misreading is the hardest test).
