# 04 Common Biases

The thinking mistakes this project keeps meeting — in objectors, in mainstream framing, in the AI's own training, and in ourselves. The reason this list exists: a bias the writer shares with the reader does not look like a bias — it looks like common sense — so checking against this list is part of checking at all. Each entry explains what the mistake is, shows a list of examples from the everyday to the well-hidden, and ends its explanation with the tell that exposes it. When one of these is caught and corrected in conversation, the fix goes into the text itself, because the next reader will bring the same bias. This page grows: any thread that meets a new bias, or a new example of a listed one, adds it here. (Each bias is a heading, so any note can link straight to it — like [[04 - Common Biases#Hyperskepticism]].)

### Hyperskepticism

The general possibility of being wrong is treated as if it were evidence of being wrong. From the inside it feels like rigor — doubt sounds careful, and more doubt sounds more careful — but no error has actually been found: only the standing possibility of error, charged against the claim as though it were a discovery. In its subtler forms, deniability itself gets treated as a virtue, as if a claim earns credit by being easier to doubt. The tell: no actual evidence against the claim is ever produced; the doubt is doing all the work by itself.

- "You can't really know anything, so this claim stands no better than its rivals."
- "Science has been wrong before — so this result can be dismissed."
- Treating "your senses could deceive you" as if it subtracted from what you actually saw.
- Demanding that a starting point be able to fail — asking for weakness exactly where the job requires firmness.
- The framing itself is contagious: describing a good claim as "one that could have been wrong" sounds like a quality standard, but the only quality it names is the possibility of failure. The honest version of the demand is that a claim stake something on how the world is — and that version no longer counts against a claim the world settles in only one direction.

### The fake-neutral default

One stance — usually an agnostic, physicalist, hyperskeptical one — is treated as the referee of a dispute when it is actually one of the parties. It survives because it rarely states itself as a position: it presents itself as a procedure, a starting point, or plain caution, and so it never gets asked to defend anything. The tell: the "neutral" position is never audited by the standard it applies to everyone else.

- "In the absence of evidence, the default is that no such thing exists" — applied to every rival, never to the default itself.
- "The burden of proof is on you" — said by someone whose own stance never carries one.
- "Methodological naturalism" sounds like a working habit — "when doing science, look for natural mechanisms" — but ask what "natural" means, and the usual answer, "what happens passively", is the same claim as physicalism, now dressed as the referee.
- An AI presenting the agnostic reading of a question as "the balanced view", as if this view were not itself a stance.

### Speculation as proof or refutation

A bare "couldn't it be that..." is treated as if it settles something, though nothing occurring actually supports the alternative. Merely mentioning a rival is treated as if it weakened the claim — it gets demoted to "one view among many" not because anything counts for the rival, but because the rival was uttered. The tell: remove the word "maybe" and the objection has nothing left.

- "Maybe your senses deceive you — so you don't know."
- "The results could be a coincidence" — with no reason offered why they would be.
- A story about how something might have gone wrong, treated as showing that it did.
- An alternative granted equal standing purely for having been mentioned.

### Argument from ignorance

Ignorance is treated as a finding, instead of the starting state that comes before every finding. Not-knowing is the prerequisite of every question; this bias promotes it into the answer. The tell: the conclusion's entire support is an absence.

- "Nobody knows what caused it, so nobody can say anything about it."
- "There is no known mechanism for X" — quietly upgraded from "we have not found how" into "therefore it is not so."
- The mirror image: "we can't rule it out, so it deserves belief."
- An open question nearby treated as if it lowered the standing of settled answers next to it.

### Double standards

One side pays a toll the other rides free of. It hides easily because each demand, taken alone, sounds like good practice — the unfairness only appears when you notice who is exempt. The tell: swap the two sides, and the argument's force changes.

- Demanding proof from a rival view while one's own view is simply assumed.
- The hard-problem move: a rival's unanswered question is fatal, while one's own unanswered questions of the same kind (what is causality, intrinsically? what is energy?) never count against it.
- Accepting testimony when it agrees, demanding corroboration only when it doesn't.
- "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" — where "extraordinary" turns out to mean "not mine."

### Contradiction dressed as depth

An unresolved clash is presented as profundity. What powers this is a confusion between two silences that feel similar: the ignorance that comes from wisdom, which is a real question honestly left open and waiting on knowledge, and the lack of knowledge that comes from falsehood, which is a clash that convicts a claim, dressed up as if it were such a question. The tell: ask what, concretely, would count as resolving it — a real depth has directions to dig in; a dressed-up contradiction has none.

- "It is both true and not true — that is the mystery."
- Technical vocabulary layered over a clash until the reader assumes the incomprehensibility is their own failing rather than the speaker's.
- A self-defeating position presented as humility: "no statement is true — including this one — and that is the deepest truth of all."
- A riddle used to end questioning rather than to sharpen it.

### Exporting a claim beyond its context

A claim true within its scope is carried outside it, and then declared refuted. The claim never failed; the subject was changed under it. The tell: the "refutation" only works after the subject has been quietly swapped.

- "Relativity proved Newton wrong" — yet within everyday speeds and sizes, Newton's physics holds exactly as well as it ever did; relativity widened the context, it did not overturn the claim inside its own.
- "The parallel-lines assumption was doubted, and curved-space geometry won" — the doubt changed the space under discussion; it never touched the claim's truth in flat space.
- "Quantum physics shows that logic fails" — exporting behavior found at one scale into domains where nothing of the kind was observed.
- A rule derived in one domain applied in another where its terms don't reach, then blamed for the wrong answers.

### Semantic drift

One word carries two meanings across an argument, and the conclusion needs both at once. The drift is nearly invisible when the two senses sit far apart in a long text, or when the word is so ordinary that nobody thinks to check it. The tell: force the drifting word to hold one meaning from start to finish, and the argument breaks.

- "Experience is an illusion; illusions aren't real; so experience isn't real" — "real" first means "being what it seems to be", then "occurring at all".
- "Thing" sliding between object and occurrence across a page — caught once already inside PP1's own objections.
- "Evolution is just a theory" — "theory" drifting between "guess" and "well-tested model".
- "Faith" drifting between "trust built on a track record" and "belief without any reason".

### Trained framing

An AI's training treats mainstream philosophy's vocabulary and defaults as neutral ground, so it re-frames plain positions into mainstream terms that subtly shift them — and it does this while sounding helpful. The tell: the reframing always moves toward what the training treats as safe, never toward the author's actual words.

- Hedging both sides of a question that has an answer, to appear balanced.
- Re-confirming a ruling the author already made, as if the question were still open.
- Filing his position under a school's name — "foundationalism", say — which imports commitments he never took on.
- "Improving" the author's wording into standard vocabulary that changes the claim — the way the word "bundle" once entered this very project's record although the author never typed it.
