# 03 Demystification

Why and how everything in this project is written — nodes, prompts to threads, returns, chat.

## Why

Most complexity a reader meets is not in the topic — it is in the telling. It appears when:

- the speaker didn't break the topic down for the listener;
- the speaker can't break the topic down for themselves — they never traced it to its roots, so they don't truly understand what they are saying;
- the complexity is doing work: dressing something up as impossible to tackle, or as deeper than it is — like a contradiction presented, knowingly or not, as profound wisdom, by someone unable to distinguish the ignorance that comes from wisdom from the lack of knowledge that comes from falsehood;
- someone wants the audience in the dark, and presents information in the way that keeps them there;
- or the topic arrives pre-wrapped in bad framing from how it is usually told, and getting it right is like translating out of a foreign language — the bad words and framings removed, the correct ones put in their place.

Demystified writing breaks all of that: it presents the concept so plainly that any reader can grasp it, tackle it productively, and reach a clear, objective outcome. That takes two things at once. First, the right breakdown of the topic itself — its actual structure, traced to its roots. Second, language that transmits that breakdown without loss: precise enough that a specialist would agree it is accurate, and plain enough that the density never persuades the reader to stop reading. Hard on the topic, soft on the reader — everyday words, full flowing sentences, ordinary examples, written as if the reader has never seen the project before. The thinking mistakes that this kind of writing keeps meeting are cataloged in [[04 - Common Biases]].

## The rules

- **Demystify the concept before the language** (his ruling). Demystification has two layers, and both must pass. First the concept: clear, correct, and in its right place — a wrong or misplaced concept stays a problem in any wording ("we changed the language of this sentence quite a few times in previous threads, but the conceptual issue was still there"). Then the language: plain and precise — dense telling makes even a clear concept appear difficult to grasp. Sound, clear logic is the highest priority of the whole effort; the logic helper in [[01 - Processing Queries]] checks this layer, and the worked examples are cases 4, 17, 19, and 21 in [[10 - Sentence Case Studies]]. Shorthand for flagging which layer, kept provisionally (his "for now"): "the thought is off" / "the wording is off".
- **Everyday words.** If a common word does the job, the fancy word is wrong.
 - Bad: "If your foundation is analytically true, it is unfalsifiable and therefore empirically empty."
 - Good: "A statement that is true by definition gets its truth from how we chose to use words, not from whether or not it aligns with reality — teaching you vocabulary, not facts about anything." (Rewritten: the earlier good example itself carried two words he later banned — "consulted" and "reports" — and the standard was reproducing them; when a word gets banned, the rules files' own examples get swept too.)
- **A term of art gets its plain meaning right where it is used, every time.**
 - Bad: "This may be a Carnapian pseudo-statement."
 - Good: "A philosopher named Carnap called sentences like that pseudo-statements — grammatically fine, but conceptually empty."
- **An example appears wherever a point is invisible without one**, in parentheses or a sub-bullet.
 - Bad: "Tautologies carry no information." (The reader nods along without seeing why.)
 - Good: "This isn't a discovery; it's a tautology (a statement with no significant information) — like saying 'a three-sided shape has three sides'." (Rewritten to his PP1 wording; the earlier good example used banned "carry".)
- **No metaphorical labels, no invented code words, no courtroom or drama phrasing.** Call each thing by its plain description every time, even when that is longer.
 - Bad: "The claim survives the terminus test." (The reader must carry a private dictionary — what is "the terminus"? This project's old threads coined "the floor", "the compass", "the terminus", and every one had to be banned.)
 - Good: "No rule of reasoning was found that stands without this rule."
- **No sentence points at an earlier item by number or placeholder alone** — restate in a few words what the item said. (Exception, his ruling: objections may be referenced by their number, with a brief header as needed.)
 - Bad: "Commitment 3 says a necessary part was stripped away." (Which commitment? What part?)
 - Good: "The third commitment says this node stripped away a necessary part — the experiencer — and kept only the happening."
- **No ambiguity.** If a sentence can be read as saying something wrong, rewrite it until it can't — check each sentence against the misreading a fresh reader might bring.
 - Bad: "The demand 'give me something that could be proven false' is the right demand for ordinary claims." (Reads as: claims must be provably false to be valid — self-defeating, and not what was meant.)
 - Good: "An everyday claim can turn out wrong, and testing it against the world can catch it." (Rewritten: the earlier example's "stakes something" was the phrasing whose obliqueness later made the author himself misread PP1's second commitment — see [[10 - Sentence Case Studies]], case 17.)
- **A misreading caught in conversation gets fixed in the text, not just in the conversation.** The average reader carries the same biases the questioner did, so they will misread the same way; a fix that lives only in chat fixes it for nobody.
 - Bad: leaving "Who or what is experiencing these phenomena" after the conversation established the node assumes no experiencer — the line still quietly assumes one exists.
 - Good: the correction written into the record: "Whether an experiencer of these phenomena exists — and if one does, what it is."
- **Bullets are bite-sized.** One graspable point per bullet; a paragraph-length bullet becomes a parent point with nested sub-bullets. Nesting — and every other piece of formatting — exists to make reading easier, not to decorate.
 - Bad: one bullet running six sentences, where the reader loses the point mid-paragraph and cannot skim.
 - Good: the point as the parent bullet, the steps as sub-bullets — the section can be skimmed by parents alone.
- **Make the relation between joined clauses explicit** — "but" for contradiction, "instead" for replacement, "so" for consequence.
 - Bad: "The angle is in the looking, not in the object; the experiencer may be in the meeting." (The reader must work out that the second clause follows from the first.)
 - Good: "The angle is in the looking, not in the object. So the experiencer may be in the noticing, not in the occurring." (Updated to the ratified wording; the earlier version used banned "meeting".)
- **The drifts to avoid:**
 - academic drift — jargon or Latinate words where a common word works ("utilize" for "use");
 - bland drift — hedging, which means wrapping a claim in so many maybes that it commits to nothing ("it could perhaps be argued that..." followed by nothing);
 - false accessibility — oversimplifying in a way a specialist would object to ("quantum physics says anything is possible");
 - emotional cushioning that buries the actual point ("with all due respect, and acknowledging the many strengths of this view,..." before a one-line objection);
 - weight drift — a phrase carrying more connotation than the point requires, so the reader spends effort deciding which reading was meant ("gets heard as" → "reads as"; "the standard move is to X" reads as "this always happens" when what is meant is only that the move is accepted).
- **Where accessibility and precision genuinely conflict in a sentence, flag that sentence to the author** rather than silently picking one.

## Watch-list: words and phrases the AI reaches for

The assistant's habits come from its training and leak in as filler, inflation, or fake weight. Compiled from published lists of AI writing tells and from this project's own catches. The cure is the same everywhere: say the plain thing, or delete.

- Filler openers: "honestly", "to be honest", "basically", "essentially", "simply put", "at its core", "fundamentally" — delete; the sentence says the same without them.
- Inflated verbs: "delve", "dive into", "unpack", "navigate", "leverage", "harness", "foster", "bolster", "underscore", "streamline", "shed light on" — use the plain verb: look at, use, support, show.
- Grand-scenery nouns: "landscape", "realm", "tapestry", "journey", "paradigm" — name the actual thing instead.
- Fake-weight adjectives: "nuanced", "multifaceted", "comprehensive", "pivotal", "crucial", "robust", "compelling", "groundbreaking", "cutting-edge" — if the point has weight, the sentence shows it without the label.
- Stock frames: "it's worth noting that", "it's important to note", "serves as", "plays a crucial role", "a testament to", "not just X but Y" — say the note itself and drop the frame.
- Hedge stacks: "may potentially", "could arguably", "somewhat", "to some extent" — several maybes stacked on one claim; commit, or say plainly what is unknown.
- Sycophancy: "great question", "you're absolutely right" — answer the question; if the person is right, the content shows it.
- Decorative triads: the habit of listing everything in threes ("clear, concise, and compelling") whether or not three things exist.
- Insider shorthand (his instruction): words like "rule", "ground", "floor", "gate", "record", "judgment call", "real (work)", "met" — the words themselves are not banned; what changes is the shape of how they are used. In their plain everyday sense they are fine ("the record shows" about an actual record). The drift is using one as a private technical term a fresh reader cannot decode — "the record" as a name for the project, "met" as a doctrine of how differences arrive ("the distinction is met, not made"), "does real work" as a verdict, "grounds" as a relation the reader was never shown. The fix is the same as everywhere: say the plain thing the word was standing for ("the difference is simply there when both occur" instead of "the difference is met").
- Conditionally banned words and phrases (his ruling): "act" (as a noun for the project's own doings — a node's proof, the method's steps), "anything" / "nothing" (as bare placeholder objects — "adds nothing", "hold anything up" — where the sentence cannot say what kind of thing is meant; "nothing" as the topic itself, as in "Nothing whatsoever is happening", is still listed and he rules), "attend", "arrive" (for how things come into experience), "carry", "check", "choice", "consult", "core", "correction", "cure", "decide", "decoration", "dodge", "does work" / "do work" (the phrase — in the sense "this concept does real work", not in "this solution works"), "earns its place" / "earn its keep", "empty", "failed", "flagged", "gets right", "grade" / "grading", "honest", "instrument", "ladder", "machinery", "matters", "met" / "meet", "naming", "never", "ordering", "ours" / "yours", "padding", "phrasing", "plain", "points" (in the pointing-at sense; "starting point" is a different word — his call whether it is covered), "press", "raw material", "reach" (in the sense "reach an occurrence"), "reports", "rule", "run" / "running" (as the verb for how an order, a test, or reasoning operates — "the order runs one way", "the reasoning runs on"; a person running, or "this solution works"-style plain uses, are outside), "rungs", "settle", "shaping", "shows up", "sits", "splits", "survive", "the world" (as a vague stand-in reached for over and over — for reality, for what occurs, for what makes something true), "underneath", "worry", and terms minted with a hyphen — the structure itself, not any particular phrase (his ruling): words glued into an ad-hoc term, like "difference-claim" or "the telling-apart"; ordinary everyday compounds, like "three-sided shape", are outside the target (thread's boundary reading, his to veto) — and any word or phrase he adds later. This binds all threads. Chat text follows the same standard without the ruling loop: the AI swaps banned words out of its own chat text directly, no per-sentence permission needed (his instruction). ## The self-check before finishing anything

- The concept itself is clear, correct, and in its right place — no rewording rescues a wrong or misplaced thought.
- A smart non-expert understands it on first read.
- A specialist reads it and does not wince.
- No sentence wraps itself in maybes while committing to nothing.
- No sentence supports a wrong reading — including any misreading already caught in conversation: the fix is in the text.
- Every point that is invisible without an example has one.
- No reference forces the reader to scroll back to decode it.
- Every section can be skimmed by its parent bullets alone.
- No watch-list word is left doing no work.
- Formatting follows the standards — the same kind of thing gets the same mark every time.

If any of these fail, rewrite.
