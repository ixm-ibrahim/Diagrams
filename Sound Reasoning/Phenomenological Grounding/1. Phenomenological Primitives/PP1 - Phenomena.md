## Motivation

Something either happens or it does not.

## Observations

- [sight] red
- [touch] hot
- [touch] pressure
- [body] hungry
- [pain] ouch
- [taste] strawberry
- [body] an arm, felt without looking
- [want] pleasure
- [thought] *"cruelty is wrong"*
- [thought] *"the earth is flat"*
- [thought] the number 2
- [thought] *"square circles are impossible"*
- [imagination] a flying pig

## Conclusion

**Phenomena occur.**
- "Phenomenon" is our label for anything that occurs.
    - This includes seeing, tasting, thoughts, emotions, logic, etc. — anything and everything that occurs.
- This is the starting point for everything: something either happens, or it does not.

## If Rejected

- **Without phenomena, there is nothing — not even the rejection of this very claim.**
    - **Proof by Contradiction** (assume the opposite, and show how it breaks itself)
        1. Suppose phenomena don't occur.
        2. Then no act, thought, or awareness of any kind could occur.
        3. But the act of supposing in step 1 itself occurred (let alone the reading, thoughts, emotions, and experience that comes with it) — and supposing is a phenomenon.
        4. Contradiction: supposing can't occur, yet it did.
        
    - **Consequences**
        1. The rejection defeats itself:
            - If nothing occurs, then there is nothing to notice, nothing to point at, nothing to feel. There are no observations, no statements, no thoughts — not even the thought that nothing is happening.
            - But the rejection is itself an occurrence — a thought, a doubt, a denial. So there is no way to reject the claim without that rejection being something occurring.
            - So the denial does not expose a rational alternative — it is itself an example of the very thing it denies.

## Unlocks

- **Concepts:**
    - Something is happening: the most basic discovery, on which all other ideas are built.
    - The next observation: seeing if what occurs is uniform or varied — whether or not distinctions occur.

- **Vocabulary:**
    - **"Phenomenon"** — anything that occurs. *Synonyms: appearance, happening, manifestation, occurrence, thing*
    - **"Occur"** — to happen, to be present. *Synonyms: appear, happen, manifest*

## Eliminates

- "Nothing whatsoever is happening" — since holding this position is itself something happening.
- A more basic starting point — because without "something occurs" there is nothing left to break down: things either occur or they don't.
    - Note that learning how things happen is a secondary step, which can only occur after the initial observation that something is happening.

## Unknowns

- What is occurring — no specific observations have been made yet.
- Whether what occurs is uniform or varied.
- Whether an experiencer of these phenomena exists — and if one does, what it is.
- Why anything occurs at all.

## Objections

### 1. This is trivially true — statements that have to be true by definition can't hold anything up.

**Objection Basis**

- "Phenomena occur" is true by definition — we are simply defining a "phenomenon" as something that occurs. This isn't a discovery; it's a tautology (a statement with no significant information).
	- For example, "triangles have three sides" tells you nothing about any actual triangle — it's like saying "a three-sided shape has three sides".
- A statement that is true by definition gets its truth from how we chose to use words, not from whether or not it aligns with reality — teaching you vocabulary, not facts about anything.
	- How could a report about our word-choices hold up claims about what actually occurs?
- Some philosophers add that starting points aren't needed at all: a belief is actually held up by how well it fits with all your other beliefs  — just like the way bricks in an arch hold each other, with no single brick at the bottom. (These schools are called coherentism and pragmatism.)

**Objection Commitments**

1. A statement that is true by definition tells you nothing about reality, because its truth comes from how words were chosen, not from how the world actually is.
2. A starting point has to actually describe how the world is — otherwise it carries no weight.
3. Starting points are optional anyway — beliefs can hold each other up without one, so long as they are consistent with each other.

**Shared Ground**

- The objector is right that choosing words settles nothing about the world: you cannot make something exist by defining a term for it. This project holds the same — every entry here must trace to something that occurs, not to a phonetic sound or arbitrary label.
- Both sides also agree that a claim must earn its keep: a statement doing no work has no business holding other statements up.

**What's Missing**

- The first commitment says a true-by-definition statement reflects our word-choices, not the nature of reality. So, let's test it: what it would mean for "Phenomena occur" to actually be wrong?
    - Not wrong in some imagined way, but as in: nothing occurring at all.
    - In that situation there are no beliefs, no consistency between beliefs, no arguments, no objections.
    - So even the objector's preferred picture — beliefs holding each other up with no starting point (the third commitment) — needs something for the beliefs to be about before it can be built.
- This also shows the claim is not trivial the way "triangles have three sides" is.
    - That sentence just unpacks a definition someone chose — its guarantee comes entirely from the choice of words.
    - But "Phenomena occur" is not guaranteed by any choice of words: our choice of defining "phenomenon" does not cause anything to occur. What makes it true is that something does actually occur — our word only labels it.
    - So, it identifies the raw material that every claim, every definition, and every argument is made of.
- The second commitment asks the starting point to describe how reality actually is. That gets the job backwards — but not because the world is out of the picture.
    - "Phenomena occur" is settled by the world: something occurs. It just cannot be settled the other way, because a world with nothing occurring leaves nobody to settle anything.
    - And the one place you want a statement that cannot fail is the bottom of the structure — asking the base to be able to fail is asking for weakness exactly where the role requires firmness.
- The valid worry underneath the objection: a claim this thin might not do enough work to support anything.
    - But the test of a starting point is not how much it asserts — it is whether anything at all can be said without it.
    - "Phenomena occur" passes that test: take it away, and even the objector's own objection is gone.

**Correction**

- "Phenomena occur" is not empty the way "triangles have three sides" is: it points at the material every further claim is made of, and taking it away removes the possibility of any claim at all — including the objector's.
- The beliefs-holding-each-other alternative still needs something for the beliefs to be about — and that "something" is some phenomena occurring.
    - The claim's foundational simplicity is its strength: it cannot be denied without the denial itself proving it.

### 2. This looks like a claim, but what does it actually rule out?

**Objection Basis**

- "Phenomena occur" sounds like it says something — but try to describe what things would be like if it were not so... there is nothing to describe.
- And a sentence tells you something only if it draws a line between how things are and how they are not. A sentence that draws no line isn't really saying anything: it has the shape of a claim while carrying nothing inside. (A philosopher named Carnap called sentences like that pseudo-statements — grammatically fine, but conceptually empty. For example: "reality realizes itself" — words arranged like a claim, but actually saying nothing.)

**Objection Commitments**

1. A real claim must rule something out — some way things could have been. ("The kettle is hot" rules out the kettle being cold.)
2. "Phenomena occur" rules nothing out, because "nothing occurring" is not a situation anyone could be in or describe.
3. A claim that cannot be meaningfully denied says nothing, so it cannot serve as a starting point.

**Shared Ground**

- The objector is right that a claim must be checkable, and that a claim nobody could ever check deserves suspicion. This project agrees so strongly that its own test for every claim is denial: consider the opposite, and see what breaks.
- Both sides agree most claims must draw a line between how things are and how they are not.

**What's Missing**

- The third commitment — that an undeniable claim can't be a starting point — mistakes the job.
    - A starting point isn't there to rule out ways the world could be — it is there to hand later claims their first words.
    - "Phenomena occur" does exactly that: everything after it is built out of what it points at (the way this very node hands out "occur" and "phenomenon").
- The second commitment — that "nothing occurring" can't be described — is true, but it counts *for* the claim, not against it.
    - The reason it can't be described is that describing is itself an occurrence.
    - So the objector can't even state the empty situation without using their own thinking and words — which are phenomena.
- And the claim does rule something out — just not a way the world could be. It rules out a position (see Eliminates).
    - The position: someone holding "nothing whatsoever is happening".
    - That position defeats itself the moment it is held, because the holding is itself something happening.
    - So: nothing ruled out among ways the world could be, and something very definite ruled out among positions a person can hold.
- The demand's honest core — every claim must be checkable — is met here, not dodged. This claim does not sit above checking; a claim excused from checking is exactly what a false claim would need in order to sit at the root of a structure, and this one asks for no such excuse.
    - The check is the one this node already ran: consider the opposite and watch what happens (the If Rejected section). The opposite breaks itself — that is a checking result, not an exemption.
    - What this claim lacks is not a check but an "instead": no describable situation sits on its other side. An everyday claim is checked by comparing it against its instead — "the kettle is hot" against the cold kettle. This claim is checked by watching its denial destroy itself. A different route, the same discipline.
    - So the line it draws is real, just drawn among positions instead of situations: consider the opposite, and the opposite cannot even be held.

**Correction**

- The claim's undeniability is not a weakness — it is exactly what fits it for the bottom of the structure.
- To deny "Phenomena occur" you have to think and speak, and thinking and speaking are phenomena occurring. The objector's tools are made of the very thing they are trying to reject.

### 3. This assumes an observer — something can only occur if it occurs *to* something.

**Objection Basis**

- Every occurrence you can point to is one you witnessed. So the idea of "something happening" seems to drag along "something it happens to" — maybe you can't even have the first idea without the second.
- Philosophy and religion have versions of this point (a philosopher named Strawson argued that experience needs an experiencer; a Buddhist teaching holds that consciousness is always consciousness *of* something), and even grammar leans the same way: we cannot say "rains" by itself — we must invent an "it" to do the raining to say, "it's raining".
- So the objection: this node talks about occurrence with nobody there, without showing that such an idea even makes sense.

**Objection Commitments**

1. The idea of something happening first requires the idea of something it happens to.
2. You can never check on an occurrence except by being the one experiencing it.
3. Occurrence with nobody there isn't a simpler picture — it's a broken one: it removes a necessary part and pretends the rest stands on its own.

**Shared Ground**

- The objector is right that every occurrence anyone has ever reached must have come through experiencing it — this project does not dispute that for a moment; it builds on it.
- Both sides agree the question "to whom does this happen?" is real and worth answering.

**What's Missing**

- The second commitment — that you only ever reach an occurrence by being the one experiencing it — is true, but it proves the opposite of what the objector wants.
    - Yes — we do later establish that every occurrence you reach is one you are experiencing.
    - But that shows your *access* to occurrences goes through experience. It does not show that being-experienced is part of what occurring *is*.
    - Those are different things: one is about how you find out, the other is about what it is.
- The first commitment — that happening requires a someone it happens to — gets its pull from the second: because you always meet occurrences as the one experiencing them, it feels like an experiencer is built into occurrence itself.
    - Compare: you always see objects from some angle — but that doesn't mean objects have an angle built into them.
    - The angle is in the looking, not in the object. So the experiencer may be in the meeting, not in the occurring.
- The third commitment says this node stripped away a necessary part — the experiencer — and kept only the happening. But this node isn't denying that there is an experiencer — it is speaking from before the words for one exist.
    - At this point, all that can be said is: this is happening.
    - "Happening to whom?" is still a real question, but it needs words this node hasn't built yet — words for direction, for awareness, for a self — each of which will be discovered in later nodes.

**Correction**

- The node does not claim that occurrence has no one behind it — it records occurrence before the words for "someone" exist. "Phenomena occur" is the observation; "to whom?" is a real question waiting for the required future nodes that build awareness, identity, and self.
- The node's silence about a subject is not a denial of one. It is just the order in which things get built.

### 4. Consciousness is an illusion — nothing is actually happening. (This idea is called eliminativism.)

**Objection Basis**

- Some philosophers and brain scientists hold that consciousness does not really exist — that there is no inner experience at all, and the sense that there is a real "experiencer" is the brain misdescribing itself.
- On that view, what you call "experience" is part of a story the brain tells about itself — and the story is wrong.
- If they are right, then nothing is genuinely "happening" in the sense this node needs.
- And even if they are wrong, all that we see, hear, and experience is the brain *reconstructing* what the senses receive anyway, so we're not even experiencing reality as it truly is.

**Objection Commitments**

1. What you call experience is not raw fact — it is a story the brain tells, and stories can be false.
2. If the story is false, the things it mentions (inner feelings, experience itself) may simply not exist.
3. The true account of what the brain does may contain no experience at all.
4. Even where experience occurs, it is the brain reconstructing what the senses receive — so what occurs is not reality as it truly is.

**Shared Ground**

- The objector is right that the brain's account of itself can be wrong, and that many rich claims about consciousness deserve scrutiny — this node makes no use of those claims.
- Both sides also agree that what the senses deliver is processed rather than raw.

**What's Missing**

- Try to hold the position while reading this sentence: nothing is happening — red, hot, pressure, hungry, a memory: none of these ever happened to you, and the reading of this very sentence is not happening either.
    - Yet you are reading. The reading is something happening, whether you like it or not.
    - Call the reading an illusion if you like — an illusion is *also something happening*.
    - The position destroys itself: denying that anything occurs is itself an occurrence.
- What this idea actually attacks is a much richer idea of consciousness — a unified self, mysterious inner qualities, experience as a special kind of stuff.
    - This node claims none of that. It claims the least that can be claimed: *something is happening*.
    - The bar could *literally* not be lower.
- The added worry — that even genuine experience is the brain reconstructing what the senses receive, so we never meet reality as it truly is — aims past this node.
    - Whether what occurs matches a reality beyond it is a question about accuracy, and this node makes no claim about accuracy — that question waits for later nodes, and sits under Unknowns as "what is occurring".
    - But notice what the worry concedes: a reconstruction is something occurring. Receiving, reconstructing, experiencing — every step it describes is a happening.
    - You can doubt that what you see is a real apple; you cannot doubt that the seeing occurred.

**Correction**

- Eliminativism attacks a rich picture of the mind — beliefs, inner qualities, consciousness as a real thing in reality — and says that picture doesn't describe what really exists. This node makes no use of that picture; it claims only that something is happening.
- And that much eliminativism cannot deny — because the theorizing, the denying, and the arguing are themselves happenings.
- The reconstruction worry, likewise, only relocates the happening — it does not remove it. What the occurrences say about anything beyond them is a later question; that they occur is this node's whole claim.
