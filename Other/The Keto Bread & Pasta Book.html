How this book works

Breads, flatbreads, buns, crackers, pasta and noodles, all built on seven low-carb bases and halal throughout. Every recipe is tagged with the base it's built on. The colour bar on the left of each recipe matches the chips at the top, so you can tell at a glance whether something is a yeasted gluten-blend bread, a cheese dough, an egg bread, or a vegetable standing in for a noodle.

Gluten blendVital wheat gluten, almond flour, flax and oat fiber. Yeasted, kneaded, rises. The closest thing to real bread and real pasta.
FatheadMelted mozzarella, cream cheese, almond flour and egg. No yeast, ready in 20 minutes, pizza-parlour chewy.
EggEggs plus cream cheese or shredded cheese, and whipped whites on their own. Fastest, lowest-carb, softest.
Nut, coconut, seedAlmond flour, coconut flour, psyllium, flax and whole seeds with chemical leavening. Gluten-free quick breads.
VegetableCauliflower breads, and squash, zucchini, cabbage and roots cut into noodles.
Meat & cheeseChicken, beef or chicken cracklings, and cheese doing the job of flour. Near-zero carb.
Konjac & store-boughtShirataki and konjac rice, homemade konjac noodles from glucomannan, plus kelp and heart-of-palm noodles, with the prep that makes them good.

Several kinds of note appear on the cards: plain boxes for tips and variants, green-barred notes for nutrient boosts that carry a real amount per serving, amber-barred notes where the usual way of doing something is worse than a less usual way, red-barred notes for when a recipe didn't turn out (what went wrong, what to change, and what to do with the batch you have), blue-barred notes on taste (how to lose the eggy, musty or coconut notes a base brings with it), purple-barred notes for a low-carb version that isn't keto (a small amount of real flour, starch or sugar where it buys a real improvement, with the carb cost per serving), and dotted notes saying what a recipe leaves behind and where it goes.

Measurements give cups and grams. A scale makes the gluten-blend recipes far more repeatable; the almond flour and egg recipes are forgiving either way. The nutrition strip on each card is computed per serving from typical brand labels for the ingredients as listed (optional add-ins left out), rounded; expect it to move a little with your specific bags and how you slice. Net carbs are total carbs minus fiber. Every recipe assumes large eggs, full-fat dairy, unsalted butter, and halal-slaughtered meat and poultry. Wherever a recipe calls for something you'd normally buy but could make, a homemade link points to the method in the back.

Halal, as written

Every recipe here is halal as printed. Nothing uses pork, lard, gelatin or alcohol, and where a conventional version would, the swap is already in the ingredient list: beef tallow or ghee in tortillas, beef bacon in haluski, halal beef pepperoni in calzones and stromboli, chicken or lamb in dumplings, chicken-skin or beef cracklings where pork rinds would be. A few ingredients still deserve a label check because they vary by brand.

Label checks

Cheese and rennet
Parmigiano-Reggiano, Grana Padano, pecorino, and traditional halloumi and feta are made with animal rennet from calves or lambs that were not halal-slaughtered. For parmesan and other hard cheeses, choose ones labelled microbial or vegetable rennet, or halal-certified. Most American mozzarella and cheddar use microbial enzymes; the label will say "microbial enzymes" or "vegetarian rennet". Cream cheese, ricotta, labneh, sour cream and yogurt are acid- or culture-set with no rennet, but some light or whipped versions add gelatin: use full-fat and read the line.
Extracts
Vanilla, banana and corn extracts are usually alcohol-based. Use alcohol-free extracts, vanilla powder, or the glycerin vanilla in the back of the book. Wherever a recipe says vanilla, that's what it means.
Cream of tartar
A purified salt recovered from grape must in winemaking. It's widely accepted as halal, but every recipe that uses it also gives a lemon-juice alternative.
Soy sauce
Naturally brewed soy sauce carries traces of alcohol from fermentation. Use a halal-certified soy sauce, a tamari checked the same way, or coconut aminos.
Meat products
Halal beef pepperoni, beef bacon, beef cracklings and halal deli chicken or turkey cover every place a recipe wants cured meat or crackling. Where buying them is hard, the homemade section has spiced beef crumbles, chicken-skin crisps and roast-your-own deli chicken.
Fine as they are
Yeast, vinegar, baking soda and powder, sweeteners, psyllium, gluten, oat fiber, and konjac. Sugar-free chocolate is usually fine; check it for alcohol-based flavourings.

The low-carb notes

Most of the gaps in the table above close with a small amount of the real thing: a quarter cup of bread flour in a bagel, a spoon of semolina in the pasta, some rye flour in the rye. Where that's true, the recipe carries a purple note giving the addition and the carb cost per serving, so you can decide when a few grams are worth it. None of those versions are keto, and they're written as departures, not defaults: a 5-gram bagel is still a quarter of a real one.

Breads & bakes

Yeasted breads from the gluten blend

This is the blend from your guide, with the numbers corrected. It is the only keto base with real gluten, which means it kneads, rises, holds a shape and browns (a little). Make the blend in bulk, then every recipe in this chapter starts from the same loaf dough and diverges at shaping.

The master blend

Gluten blend
Makes 3 cups (~310 g)Time 5 min
Per ¼ cup (30 g)130 kcal12 g protein7 g fat2.5 g net carbs6 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (120 g) vital wheat gluten
  • 1 cup (100 g) super-fine blanched almond flour (buy this one; home-ground is too coarse for the blend)
  • ½ cup (52 g) golden flaxseed meal (homemade from whole seed, ground fresh)
  • ½ cup (36 g) oat fiber (not oat flour)

Method

  1. Whisk everything together in a large container for a full minute so the gluten is evenly distributed; clumps of gluten make rubbery streaks.
  2. Store airtight. Room temperature for a month; refrigerate or freeze for longer, because flax meal goes rancid.
  3. Scale it up: a 3× batch (3 cups gluten, 3 cups almond, 1½ cups each flax and oat fiber) fills a 4-quart container and makes about four loaves.

Why these four. Gluten gives structure and chew. Almond flour gives fat, tenderness and flavour. Flax gives a multigrain taste and binds water. Oat fiber adds bulk with zero calories so the crumb isn't dense. Vital wheat gluten carries about 4 g of carbs per ¼ cup, which is where most of the blend's net carbs come from.

Mineral blend, nut-free. Use pumpkin seed flour in place of all the almond flour (same weight). Fat, protein and absorbency are close enough that nothing else changes; the crumb turns grey-green. Each slice of bread made from it picks up roughly 1 mg iron, 55 mg magnesium and 0.8 mg zinc, about a tenth of a day's worth of each. Swapping only ¼ cup barely registers, so it's all or nothing. Fiber: 6 g per serving as written. The blend is already about 6 g of fiber per ¼ cup, mostly from the oat fiber and flax; raising the oat fiber to ¾ cup per batch adds another 1.5 g per ¼ cup with a slightly drier crumb and 2–3 tbsp more water.

Taste. Vital wheat gluten has a flat, faintly beany taste on its own, and brands differ: Bob's Red Mill and Anthony's are clean; some bulk brands taste of cardboard. If a loaf tastes off with everything else right, it's the gluten. The flax and the yeast's sugar carry most of the flavour of anything made from this blend, so don't skimp on either.

Everyday sandwich loaf

Gluten blend
Makes 1 loaf, 16 slicesTime 2½ hr (20 min hands-on)
Per slice95 kcal7.5 g protein6 g fat2 g net carbs3 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 2½ cups (255 g) master blend
  • 1 packet (2¼ tsp, 7 g) instant or active dry yeast
  • 2 tsp sugar or honey (the yeast eats it; under 0.5 g net per slice even if it didn't)
  • 1 tsp (6 g) salt
  • 1 cup (240 ml) warm water, 105–110°F, starting with ¾ cup
  • 2 large eggs, room temperature
  • 2 tbsp (28 g) butter, melted, or olive oil
  • Optional: 1 tsp baking powder as insurance on the rise

Method

  1. Stir the yeast and sugar into ¾ cup of the warm water. Wait 5–10 minutes for foam. No foam means dead yeast; start over.
  2. Put the blend and salt (and baking powder) in the mixer bowl. Add the yeast water, eggs and butter. Dough hook on low for 1 minute to bring it together.
  3. Knead on low (speed 2 on a KitchenAid, which is the highest its manual allows for dough) for 7–10 minutes, and stop when it's smooth and springy; gluten this concentrated develops fast and turns rubbery if you keep going. You want a smooth, springy ball that slaps the bowl and pulls cleanly off the sides. If it tears or looks dry, add the remaining water a tablespoon at a time. If it's a sticky puddle, add blend a tablespoon at a time. It should be tacky, not sticky.
  4. Cover the bowl and rest 10–15 minutes so the oat fiber and flax finish absorbing water.
  5. Flatten the dough into a rectangle as wide as your pan, roll it up tightly, pinch the seam, and set it seam-down in a greased 9×5 pan (8½×4½ for a taller loaf).
  6. Cover loosely and rise somewhere warm for 45–75 minutes, until the dome crowns ½–1 inch above the rim. Don't push past that; overproofed gluten bread sinks.
  7. Brush with egg wash if you want colour. Bake at 350°F for 35–40 minutes, until deep golden and an instant-read thermometer in the centre reads 200°F or higher.
  8. Cool 10 minutes in the pan, then fully on a rack, at least an hour. Cutting warm gives you gummy slices. Slice thin with a serrated knife.

This dough is the base for every recipe in this chapter. When a recipe says "one batch of loaf dough", make steps 1–4 and pick up from there. Egg-free: replace the eggs with an extra ½ cup water; the loaf will be a little denser and paler.

Pullman loaf
Make 1¼× the dough, proof in a greased 9-inch Pullman pan until it's an inch from the lid, slide the lid on, and bake at 350°F for 40–50 minutes, lid off for the last 10 for colour. Square slices, fine tight crumb.
More rise and chew
Swap ¼ cup of the blend for ¼ cup more vital wheat gluten. The blend as written leans tender; gluten-heavier keto doughs rise higher and chew more, up to the point where they turn rubbery.
Softer, rounder flavour
Add 1 tsp apple cider vinegar to the water. It relaxes the gluten slightly and takes the edge off the flax.

Nutrient boost. Make it with the mineral blend, or bake it as the seeded loaf with the seeds doubled. Those are the two changes that move the numbers; a teaspoon of molasses or a pinch of potassium salt in a whole loaf sounds good but comes to almost nothing per slice. Fiber: 3 g per serving as written. Add ¼ cup oat fiber and 3 tbsp water to the batch: about +1 g per slice at zero calories. Two tablespoons of chia does the same with a little crunch.

The usual way, and the better way. Don't wait for it to double, which is what every wheat recipe says; a gluten-isolate dough over-proofs before that and collapses in the oven. Proof it somewhere warm and deliberate: the oven with only the light on, or a turned-off oven with a pan of just-boiled water on the floor, runs about 80°F and is more reliable than a windowsill. And weigh the blend: a scooped cup of almond flour varies by a third depending on how it's packed, which is why the same recipe works one week and not the next.

If it didn't turn out. Dense and short: too little water, weak yeast, or under-proofed. The dough should be tacky and the yeast should foam. Sunken middle: over-proofed (it rose past an inch over the rim) or sliced warm. Gummy centre with a good crust: under-baked; go to 200°F inside and cool a full hour. Rubbery, squeaky crumb: over-kneaded or too much gluten for your flour; next time knead less, add 2 tbsp almond flour and a tablespoon more butter. Salvage: a dense or sunken loaf toasts well and makes the best rusks and croutons; a gummy one, sliced and toasted slice by slice, is fine bread.

Taste. Keto gluten bread reads as flat and under-seasoned at wheat-recipe salt levels; 1 tsp is the minimum here and 1¼ tsp is better. The other flavour levers are the ones real bread uses: the sugar the yeast eats (its by-products are the smell of bread), a cold overnight rest, a tablespoon of butter in the dough, an egg wash for a browned crust, and a toaster. A teaspoon of apple cider vinegar or 2 tbsp yogurt in the water rounds the flavour without tasting sour.

Low-carb, not keto. Replace ½ cup of the blend with ½ cup (60 g) whole-wheat or spelt flour. The yeast has starch to feed on, the crumb softens and browns like bread, and the flavour fills out. Cost: about 2.5 g net carbs more per slice, so 4.5 g total. Bread flour does the same with a lighter crumb.

Sourdough-style loaf

Gluten blend
Makes 1 round loaf, 16 slicesTime 3 hr (20 min hands-on)
Per slice95 kcal7.5 g protein6 g fat2 g net carbs3 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 batch loaf dough
  • 1 tbsp apple cider vinegar, or ½ tsp citric acid
  • Optional: 1 tbsp plain full-fat yogurt for lactic roundness

Method

  1. Add the vinegar (and yogurt) to the water in step 1 of the loaf dough. Make the dough through the rest.
  2. For real depth, cover the kneaded dough and refrigerate it 12–24 hours before shaping. The yeast keeps working slowly on the sugar and on the almond flour, and the flavour compounds it makes are most of what your nose reads as "bread". Let the cold dough sit out 30 minutes, then shape and proof as usual; the proof takes longer from cold.
  3. Shape and bake as the Dutch-oven boule below. The cracked, scored crust and the tang do most of the sourdough impression.

Expectations. There's no fermentation here. A real starter needs starch to eat and this blend has almost none, so this is flavour mimicry, and it works better than you'd expect. Skip anything online promising a true keto sourdough culture.

Iron & energy. Same levers as the loaf: the mineral blend for about 1 mg iron and 55 mg magnesium a slice, and half the salt as potassium-chloride lite salt. The yogurt option adds a little potassium and B12. Fiber: 3 g per serving as written. ¼ cup oat fiber and 3 tbsp water in the batch: about +1 g per slice.

Low-carb, not keto. Use a real starter. Replace ¼ cup of the blend with ¼ cup (60 g) active whole-wheat sourdough starter, drop the vinegar, and cold-ferment 12–24 hours. Real tang from real fermentation, for about 1 g net per slice; the culture eats most of the starter's starch.

Dutch-oven boule

Gluten blend
Makes 1 round loaf, 16 slicesTime 3 hr (25 min hands-on)
Per slice95 kcal7.5 g protein6 g fat2 g net carbs3 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 batch loaf dough
  • Oat fiber for dusting

Method

  1. After the 15-minute rest, shape the dough into a tight round: pull the edges under and into the centre, flip, and drag it toward you on the counter a few times to tighten the skin.
  2. Set it on a sheet of parchment, cover with an inverted bowl, and rise 45–60 minutes until noticeably puffed.
  3. Thirty minutes before baking, put a lidded Dutch oven in the oven and heat to 450°F.
  4. Dust the top with oat fiber and score a ½-inch-deep cross or crescent with a razor. Lift the parchment and dough into the pot, spritz the loaf with water, lid on.
  5. Bake 20 minutes covered. Remove the lid, drop to 425°F, bake 12–18 minutes more until deep golden. Internal temperature 200°F.
  6. Cool on a rack for a full hour. The crust will crackle as it cools.

Crust. Without starch there's less to caramelise, so the crust is crisp but not glassy. Brushing with egg white before scoring deepens the colour.

If it didn't turn out. Spread flat instead of rising up: the dough was too slack or the shaping too loose. Hold back 2 tbsp water, tighten the skin harder, and use a smaller Dutch oven so it has walls to climb. Burnt bottom: slide a sheet pan onto the rack below, or lift the loaf off the pot floor with a folded parchment sling. Salvage: a flat boule is a good focaccia; slice it horizontally for sandwiches.

Iron & energy. the mineral blend gives each slice about 1 mg iron and 55 mg magnesium. Brush the shaped boule with water and roll it in pumpkin and sesame seeds before scoring: another 0.5 mg iron and 30 mg magnesium per slice, and a crust that browns better. Fiber: 3 g per serving as written. ¼ cup oat fiber and 3 tbsp water: about +1 g per slice; the seed crust adds more.

Low-carb, not keto. ¼ cup (30 g) bread flour, or 2 tbsp tapioca starch, in the dough gives the open holes and the glossy, shattering crust that isolated gluten can't. About 1.3 g net per slice more.

Baguettes

Gluten blend
Makes 3 baguettes (about 36 one-inch slices)Time 2½ hr (25 min hands-on)
Per 1-inch slice40 kcal3.5 g protein2.5 g fat1 g net carbs1.5 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 batch loaf dough
  • 1 egg white, for brushing

Method

  1. Divide the rested dough into three (about 200 g each). Flatten each into a rectangle, fold the long sides to the middle, roll into a tight 12–14 inch log, and taper the ends.
  2. Set seam-down on parchment or a baguette pan, cover, rise 30–45 minutes.
  3. Heat the oven to 425°F with a metal pan on the lowest rack. Boil a kettle.
  4. Brush the loaves with egg white and slash 3–4 diagonal cuts with a razor. Slide them in, pour the boiling water into the pan, and shut the door fast.
  5. Bake 20–25 minutes until golden and crackly. Cool 20 minutes before cutting.

Iron & energy. the mineral blend, and roll the brushed loaves in sesame: per 1-inch slice about 0.5 mg iron and 25 mg magnesium from the blend and a little calcium from the seeds. Baguette slices are small, so the numbers are too; the real gain is what goes on them. Fiber: 1.5 g per serving as written. ¼ cup oat fiber and 3 tbsp water: about +0.5 g per 1-inch slice.

Low-carb, not keto. ¼ cup (30 g) bread flour in the batch: a crackly crust and a more open crumb. About 0.6 g net per 1-inch slice.

Ciabatta-style loaves

Gluten blend
Makes 2 loaves, 24 slicesTime 3 hr (20 min hands-on)
Per slice65 kcal5 g protein4.5 g fat1.5 g net carbs2 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 batch loaf dough made with ¼ cup (60 ml) extra water
  • 1 tbsp olive oil
  • Oat fiber for dusting, and plenty of it

Method

  1. Make the dough with the extra water. It will be slack and sticky and won't clean the bowl; that's right for this one. Knead 6 minutes, then scrape into an oiled bowl and cover.
  2. Instead of shaping, do two rounds of stretch-and-fold 20 minutes apart: lift one edge, stretch it up and fold it over the middle, turn the bowl a quarter, repeat four times. Rest 20 minutes after the second round.
  3. Tip onto heavily dusted parchment. Dust the top, cut into two rough rectangles with a bench scraper, and nudge them apart without deflating them. Cover and rise 45 minutes until puffy.
  4. Bake at 425°F for 20–25 minutes with a pan of boiling water on the lowest rack, until deep gold and 200°F inside. Cool 30 minutes.

Expectations. You get the flat slipper shape, the flour-dusted chewy crust and a moist crumb, but not the big glossy holes; those are starch structure, and there's none here. Good for panini and for tearing.

Iron & energy. the mineral blend (about 0.7 mg iron and 40 mg magnesium a slice) and half the salt as potassium-chloride lite salt. Split for a panino with roast beef and arugula and it becomes an iron meal: heme iron from the beef, vitamin C and folate from the greens. Fiber: 2 g per serving as written. ¼ cup oat fiber and 3 tbsp water: about +0.7 g per slice.

Low-carb, not keto. ½ cup (60 g) bread flour and 2 tbsp more water, and the big glossy holes appear. About 1.75 g net per slice more.

Pizzeria crust

Gluten blend
Makes one 12–13 inch crust, 8 slicesTime 1 hr (20 min hands-on)
Per slice, crust only95 kcal7.5 g protein6 g fat2 g net carbs3 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1¼ cups (128 g) master blend
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • ½ tsp salt
  • ½ cup (120 ml) warm water, plus more as needed
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tbsp olive oil

Method

  1. Make a half batch of loaf dough with these amounts: bloom the yeast in the water, then knead everything 5–6 minutes to a smooth ball. Rest covered 20–30 minutes.
  2. Heat the oven to 500°F, or as hot as it goes, with a baking steel or stone inside for 45 minutes.
  3. Roll the dough out on parchment to 12–13 inches. It will fight you; when it springs back, walk away for 5 minutes and try again.
  4. Dock the whole surface thoroughly with a fork. Slide the parchment onto the hot steel and par-bake 6–8 minutes until set and lightly coloured, then flip it over for the second bake so the bottom gets the heat.
  5. Top with low-carb sauce, cheese and whatever else, keeping toppings dry. Bake 6–8 minutes more until the cheese blisters.

Fathead (cheese) dough

Melted mozzarella and cream cheese stand in for gluten. It's chewy, browns well, needs no yeast and no rise, and is the fastest route to a pizza that folds. It is best hot; it stiffens as it cools and softens again when reheated.

The dough

Fathead
Makes 1 batch (one 12-inch pizza)Time 10 min
Per ⅛ batch145 kcal9 g protein11 g fat2 g net carbs1 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1¾ cups (200 g) shredded low-moisture part-skim mozzarella (pre-shredded is fine; fresh mozzarella is not)
  • 2 tbsp (28 g) cream cheese
  • ¾ cup (75 g) almond flour
  • 1 large egg
  • ½ tsp baking powder (optional lift)
  • ¼ tsp salt, plus ½ tsp garlic powder or Italian seasoning for savoury doughs

Method

  1. Microwave the mozzarella and cream cheese in a bowl for 60 seconds, stir, then 30 seconds more until fully melted and stretchy. (Or a double boiler.)
  2. Add the egg, almond flour, baking powder and seasonings and stir hard with a spatula, then knead with wet hands until uniform. Work quickly: it stiffens as it cools. If it seizes, microwave 15 seconds and keep going.
  3. Roll or press between two sheets of parchment. If it's too sticky to handle, chill 10 minutes.

Sweet version: leave out the salt and seasonings, add 2 tbsp granular sweetener and ½ tsp vanilla. Coconut flour version (nut-free): swap the almond flour for 3 tbsp coconut flour and add a second egg. Cheese bites (pão de queijo-style): add 3 tbsp grated parmesan to the savoury dough, roll 1¼-inch balls, bake at 375°F for 15–18 minutes until puffed and golden. The tapioca original isn't keto; these are chewy in the same spirit.

Nut-free mineral version. Use pumpkin seed flour for all the almond flour, same weight. Each eighth of a batch then carries about 0.8 mg iron, 50 mg magnesium and 0.7 mg zinc, and the dough turns olive-green. A tablespoon of fortified nutritional yeast tastes like extra cheese and adds a day's B12 across the batch. Fiber: 1 g per serving as written. 1 tbsp psyllium husk powder kneaded into the dough: about +0.8 g per eighth, and the dough handles better. Two tablespoons of ground flax does the same with a nuttier taste.

The usual way, and the better way. Two things the usual recipe doesn't tell you. Pre-shredded cheese is coated with potato starch or cellulose to keep it from clumping, which adds about half a gram of carbs per cup and dulls the melt slightly; it's acceptable in fathead dough, but for cheese crisps and wraps grate a block. And if the dough turns greasy and separates, it was overheated: stop the microwave the moment the cheese is melted and stretchy, and knead any pooled fat back in with wet hands rather than adding more flour.

If it didn't turn out. Oily and separating: overheated. Knead the pooled fat back in with wet hands and 1 tbsp more almond flour, then chill 10 minutes; it comes back. Sticky and unworkable: too warm; chill. Stiff and cracking: too cool; microwave 15 seconds. Tough after baking: too much almond flour or over-baked; measure by weight.

Taste. The failure of taste is cheese fatigue: a pizza that's cheese on cheese. Season the dough itself (garlic powder, oregano, a pinch of chili), use part-skim mozzarella, which is milder, keep the cheese topping to half what you'd put on a wheat crust, and let the crust brown properly; browned edges are what make it taste baked rather than melted.

Low-carb, not keto. 1 tbsp (8 g) tapioca starch or 2 tbsp all-purpose flour kneaded in makes the dough easier to handle and the crust more bready and less cheesy. About 1 g net per eighth of a batch.

Cloud bread

Egg
Makes 6 roundsTime 45 min (15 min hands-on)
Per round60 kcal3.5 g protein5 g fat0.5 g net carbs0 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs, separated, room temperature
  • 3 tbsp (45 g) cream cheese, softened, or Greek yogurt
  • ¼ tsp cream of tartar, or ½ tsp lemon juice
  • Pinch of salt (sweet version: 1 tsp sweetener and a drop of vanilla instead)

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 300°F and line a sheet with parchment.

  4. Spoon 6 mounds onto the sheet and flatten slightly to 4 inches. Bake 25–30 minutes until golden and dry to the touch.

The usual way, and the better way. Many versions call for baking powder as the stabiliser. Cream of tartar or lemon juice does the job properly, because it's the acid that stabilises the foam; baking powder adds a metallic note and does little for the whites. Whip in glass or metal, never plastic, which holds a film of grease that stops whites from climbing.

If it didn't turn out. Flat pancakes instead of puffs: the whites weren't stiff, you folded too long, or a trace of yolk or grease got in. Stuck to the parchment: peeled too soon; cool completely. Taste. Fresh from the oven it tastes like a meringue omelet. That fades overnight in the fridge as the sulfur compounds from the whites dissipate, so bake it the day before. Cream of tartar or lemon juice in the whites helps (acid suppresses the sulfur smell), so does a pinch of sweetener in the yolk mixture even for savoury use, and so does toasting the halves in butter. Garlic powder and herbs in the yolks make a savoury version that doesn't read as egg at all.

Whipped egg whites

Whipping traps air in egg-white protein, and the oven sets it: volume, lift and structure with no flour, almost no fat and about 17 calories per white. It's the tool behind cloud bread and the egg-white protein bread, and it reaches a good deal further, from crisps and omelets to cakes that weigh nothing. Everything here follows the same rules: a completely grease-free glass or metal bowl and whisk (plastic holds a film of grease), room-temperature whites, an acid (cream of tartar or lemon juice) to stabilise the foam, sweetener added only after soft peaks form, and stopping at stiff, glossy peaks. Stiff and glossy means the peak stands straight up when you lift the whisk, the surface is shiny like wet paint, and you can tilt the bowl without anything sliding; dull, dry and clumpy means you've gone past it, and there's no way back. Cauliflower pizza crust

Vegetable
Makes one 11-inch crust, 8 slicesTime 50 min (20 min hands-on)
Per slice, crust only55 kcal4.5 g protein3 g fat2 g net carbs1 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 medium head cauliflower (about 1½ lb), or 4 cups riced (homemade)
  • 1 large egg
  • ½ cup (56 g) shredded mozzarella
  • ¼ cup (25 g) grated parmesan
  • 1 tsp Italian seasoning, ½ tsp garlic powder, ½ tsp salt

Method

  1. Rice the cauliflower in a food processor. Microwave 5 minutes (or steam), then cool until you can handle it.
  2. Pile it into a clean tea towel and wring. Keep wringing until nothing more comes out. Aim to lose at least a cup of liquid; this is the whole recipe.
  3. Mix with everything else. Press onto parchment in a ¼-inch round, building a slight rim.
  4. Bake at 425°F for 15–20 minutes until golden and dry to the touch, then top and bake 8–10 minutes more. Let it sit 3 minutes before slicing.

Make ahead. Par-bake two or three crusts, cool, and freeze between parchment. Top and bake straight from frozen.

The usual way, and the better way. Frozen riced cauliflower, thawed and squeezed, is easier and drier than fresh: freezing breaks the cell walls so the water comes out with less effort. Whatever you start with, flip the par-baked crust before topping so the bottom crisps; a one-sided crust folds.

Left over: The stems, core and inner leaves rice just as well as the florets; use them. The liquid you wring out is bitter and goes down the drain.

If it didn't turn out. Floppy, soggy, or fell apart when lifted: not wrung dry enough, every time. Next time squeeze until nothing comes out, par-bake until the top is dry to the touch, and flip. Salvage: cut a soggy crust into strips, top with cheese, and bake 10 minutes more as breadsticks; eat with a fork.

Taste. The cabbage-family sulfur smell is the problem, and it lives in the water. Squeezing the cauliflower dry removes most of it, roasting the riced cauliflower on a sheet for 10 minutes instead of microwaving it removes more and sweetens it, and the garlic, Italian seasoning and parmesan in the crust cover the rest. A little lemon zest in the mix works too. Don't under-bake: a pale cauliflower crust tastes of steamed cauliflower; a browned one tastes of pizza.

Iron & energy. Cauliflower brings vitamin C and potassium (about 100 mg per slice) but no iron; the topping is the iron. Spiced beef or lamb, spinach and olives, or a pumpkin-seed pesto in place of tomato sauce for magnesium. Fiber: 1 g per serving as written. 1 tbsp psyllium husk powder in the mix: about +0.9 g per slice, and it binds the crust better.

Low-carb, not keto. ¼ cup (30 g) chickpea flour in the mix binds a crust that lifts like pizza and never goes floppy. About 1.75 g net per slice more.

If it didn't turn out. Soft and tearing: spread too thick or not dry enough; wring harder and bake until the edges brown before flipping. Salvage: lay a torn one flat, top it, and bake it as a mini pizza base.

Taste. Same as the crust: the smell is in the liquid, so wring it out, and season the mix (cumin and garlic for the cauliflower, Italian herbs for the zucchini). Charring them in a hot dry pan before serving is what makes them taste like a tortilla rather than a vegetable pancake.

Pasta & noodles

Pasta from the gluten dough

Gluten is what holds pasta together in boiling water, so vital wheat gluten is the base again, with more egg and no flax or oat fiber, which would make it gritty. The dough is stiff and springy, rests before rolling, and cooks in a couple of minutes. It comes out chewier than semolina pasta and holds sauce well.

Master pasta dough

Gluten blend
Makes 4 servings (~450 g)Time 1 hr (20 min hands-on)Lupin version (80 g gluten, 80 g lupin, 2 eggs): 185 kcal, 26 g protein, 3.5 g net.
Per serving300 kcal31 g protein17 g fat6.5 g net carbs2 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (120 g) vital wheat gluten
  • ¾ cup (75 g) super-fine almond flour, or lupin flour for a paler, more traditional pasta
  • ½ tsp salt
  • 2 large eggs + 1 egg yolk
  • 1 tbsp olive oil
  • 2–4 tbsp water, as needed
  • Oat fiber or almond flour for dusting

Method

  1. Whisk the gluten, almond flour and salt. Add the eggs, yolk and oil and mix, then knead 3–4 minutes by hand or with the dough hook until smooth. It will be firm and very springy. Add water a tablespoon at a time only if it's crumbly.
  2. Wrap tightly and rest at room temperature 30–60 minutes. Don't skip this; unrested gluten dough fights the roller and shrinks back.
  3. Divide into 4. Flatten one piece, dust lightly, and roll with a pin or pasta roller to about 1/16 inch (setting 4–5 of 7). Keep the rest covered.
  4. Cut into the shape you want (next recipe). Dust the cut pasta generously so it doesn't stick, and loosely nest it.
  5. Cook in well-salted boiling water for 2–4 minutes. Taste at 2 minutes; it's done when tender with a firm chew. Drain and sauce immediately.

Tuning. Too rubbery: use more almond flour next time, roll thinner, cook shorter. Falling apart: less water, more gluten, don't over-boil. Silkier: use 1 whole egg and 4 yolks. Storage: dusted and covered in the fridge 2 days; freeze nests on a tray, then bag, and cook from frozen adding a minute. The lupin version: if you buy lupin flour, the established formula is equal weights, ⅔ cup (80 g) vital wheat gluten to ⅔ cup (80 g) lupin flour with 2 eggs and no oil. It rolls a little more like semolina dough, comes out paler, and lands around 3–4 g net per serving. Lupin is the keto legume flour (about 1 g net per ¼ cup); buy debittered "sweet lupin", and note it's a peanut relative for allergy purposes.

Pasta verde. Blend ½ cup cooked, squeezed spinach with the eggs and leave out the water; add a tablespoon of almond flour if the dough is soft. Each serving then carries about 110 mcg vitamin K (nearly a day), 0.8 mg iron, 33 mcg folate and 100 mg potassium. Cook time is the same. Fiber: 2 g per serving as written. 2 tbsp oat fiber in the dough with 1 tbsp more water: about +4 g per serving at no calories, with a slightly softer bite.

The usual way, and the better way. Three pieces of standard pasta advice, two of them wrong here. Don't add oil to the water: it does nothing to stop sticking and it coats the pasta so sauce slides off. Don't rinse cooked pasta: rinsing washes away the surface that grips sauce. And do keep the water at a strong simmer rather than a rolling boil; wheat pasta shrugs off a hard boil, but keto pasta tears in it. To stop sticking, dust the cut pasta generously with oat fiber and stir it for the first 30 seconds in the pot. On recipes generally: the popular keto pasta made from almond flour, egg and xanthan gum disintegrates in water. Gluten or lupin is what makes it pasta.

Left over: 1 egg white from the extra yolk, or 4 if you make the silky version. Freeze in an ice-cube tray, or whip 3–4 into cloud bread or crisps.

If it didn't turn out. Fell apart in the water: the water was at a rolling boil, there was too much almond flour or water, it wasn't rested, or it boiled too long. Strong simmer, 2–3 minutes, and more gluten next time. Rubbery: too much gluten, rolled thick, or over-cooked. Tore on the roller: not rested, or too dry; wrap it 30 minutes more and knead in a teaspoon of water. Stuck together: dust with more oat fiber and spread it out. Salvage: broken pasta is soup pasta; rubbery pasta cut into short pieces and pan-fried in butter until crisp is a snack.

Taste. Almond flour makes the pasta faintly sweet and gluten makes it faintly flat; salt the cooking water like the sea (1 tbsp per 4 quarts), which is where wheat pasta gets its seasoning too, and finish every plate with parmesan and black pepper. A pinch of nutmeg in the dough is the old fresh-egg-pasta trick and it works here. The lupin version tastes more neutral and looks more like the real thing.

Low-carb, not keto. Semolina is what pasta is made of, and a little goes far. ¼ cup (42 g) semolina in place of half the almond flour: real pasta colour, taste and al dente snap, about 7 g net per serving more. Semolina for all the almond flour: about 14 g more, still half of wheat pasta's 40.

The usual way, and the better way. The instinct is to boil them like noodles. Don't; they're cooked, and water dissolves them. Warm them in the sauce for a minute or drop them into soup at the table. Chilled sheets cut cleaner than fresh ones, so bake ahead and slice cold.

If it didn't turn out. Broke into pieces in the sauce: the usual failure, and it has four causes: the sheet was spread paper-thin, it browned (over-baked noodles snap), the ribbons were cut narrow, or they were stirred round a hot pan. Pair them with sauces that are already rich and savoury rather than sweet-tomato ones.

Homemade konjac noodles from glucomannan powder

Konjac & store-bought
Makes about 2 lb of noodles, 6–8 servingsTime 3 hr (30 min hands-on)
Per cup (115 g)11 kcal0 g protein0 g fat0 g net carbs3.5 g fiber

Ingredients

  • 1 oz (28 g, about 3½ tbsp) glucomannan powder, or konjac flour
  • 3½ cups (830 ml) warm water, about 90°F
  • ½ tsp (about 1.2 g) food-grade calcium hydroxide, sold as pickling lime for canning
  • ¼ cup water for dissolving the lime

Method

  1. Put the warm water in a blender or a large bowl and, with it running or while whisking hard, sprinkle the powder in slowly. It thickens within a minute. Stop when it's smooth with no dry lumps.
  2. Leave it 1–2 hours, stirring once or twice, until it's a uniform, thick, translucent gel that holds a spoon track.
  3. Stir the lime into the ¼ cup water until cloudy, then pour it into the gel and stir vigorously for 1–2 minutes until it's evenly mixed and turns glossier and firmer. Wear gloves; lime is a strong alkali. This step is what sets konjac permanently; without it you just have thick goo.
  4. For noodles: load the gel into a piping bag with a small round tip, a potato ricer, or a shirataki press, and extrude directly into a wide pot of simmering water. For a block to cut: pour into a loaf pan, smooth, let set 30 minutes, cut into strips or cubes, and slide those into the simmering water.
  5. Simmer 20–30 minutes. This finishes the set and cooks off the alkaline taste. Drain, rinse, then use exactly like store-bought shirataki: dry-fry and sauce. Store submerged in fresh water in the fridge up to a week.

Should you bother? The result is the same near-zero-carb, near-zero-calorie noodle you can buy for a few dollars, and getting the texture right takes a couple of tries. It's worth it if you want noodles with no additives, want to cast shapes (sheets for lasagna, thick udon-style rounds) that aren't sold, or just like the project. Don't taste the gel before the boil, keep the lime away from kids, and stick to food-grade pickling lime. More lime gives a firmer, bouncier noodle with a stronger konnyaku smell; less gives a softer one that may not hold.

If it didn't turn out. The gel never set: too little lime or not mixed in long enough; stir 2 full minutes, and go to ¾ tsp next batch. Alkaline taste: under-boiled; simmer the full 30 minutes and rinse in water with a splash of vinegar. Salvage: an unset gel can be re-stirred with a little more limewater and cast again.

Iron & energy. A texture, not a food. Build the bowl around it with egg, meat and greens, as for shirataki. Fiber: 3.5 g per serving as written. About 3 g soluble fiber per cup, and nothing else.

Glucomannan powder as an ingredient

Konjac & store-bought
Makes a jar goes a long wayTime 5 min
Per teaspoon (3 g)0 kcal0 g protein0 g fat0 g net carbs2.5 g fiber

Uses

  • Binder in gluten-free doughs: ¼–½ tsp per cup of almond or coconut flour in tortillas, crackers and quick breads, in place of xanthan gum. It holds moisture and adds flex. Past ½ tsp it turns gummy.
  • Stretch in psyllium tortillas and egg noodles: ½ tsp whisked into the liquid first makes both more pliable.
  • Egg replacer for binding only: 1 tsp glucomannan whisked into ¼ cup water, rested 5 minutes, stands in for one egg in crackers and flatbreads. It won't replace an egg's lift or structure in a loaf.
  • Thickener: ⅛–¼ tsp per cup of liquid for gravy, pan sauce or soup. Far stronger than cornstarch.

How

  1. Never add it dry to a wet dough; it clumps into slimy beads that won't dissolve. Whisk it into the dry ingredients thoroughly, or whisk it into cold liquid or fat before heating.
  2. For sauces, whisk it into the cold liquid, then heat. It thickens as it warms and keeps thickening for 10 minutes off the heat, so stop early and wait. It doesn't break with reheating and freezes fine.
  3. Store airtight and dry. A small bag lasts a year at these quantities.

Cautions. It's a soluble fiber that swells enormously, which is the point in a sauce and a hazard in a throat. Never swallow it dry (this is why konjac supplements come with drink-a-full-glass warnings and why konjac jelly candies were pulled from shelves). In the teaspoon quantities here it's ordinary food; in large amounts it can cause bloating. What not to try: recipes that use konjac as the main "flour" for bread. It doesn't bake into anything but rubber.

Iron & energy. As a thickener it adds fiber and nothing else, but it's a good carrier: a gravy thickened with glucomannan over beef delivers the beef's iron without starch. Fiber: 2.5 g per serving as written. Each teaspoon is about 3 g of soluble fiber, which is the whole point of the powder; ½ tsp stirred into a sauce is the easiest fiber in the book.

Bases, techniques & better ways

Bases & techniques

Under the 100-odd recipes there are about a dozen tricks. Each one is a way of getting structure without starch, and once you can see the trick you can make things that aren't written down anywhere. This is the map: what each technique does, where the book uses it, and what else it will do.

Whipped egg whites

Beating unfolds egg-white proteins and stretches them around air bubbles; heat sets the film. Volume and lift from nothing. Acid stabilises the foam; any fat kills it.

Here: cloud bread, egg-white protein bread, and the whole whipped egg whites chapter.

Beyond: fold whipped whites into any almond-flour batter to lighten it; chocolate soufflé; floating islands; macarons; mousse; a marshmallow-style fluff from whites, allulose and gelatin; whipped-white "bread" in a mug for a 60-second bun.

Egg sheets

Egg cooked thin sets into a flexible film. Pure whites give the leanest sheet; yolks and cream cheese make it richer and more tender; a little psyllium or xanthan stops tearing.

Here: egg noodles, egg-white wraps, spinach wraps, cream cheese pancakes, lasagna and cannelloni sheets.

Beyond: egg-sheet "rice paper" for summer rolls; an omelet cut into ribbons as a soup noodle; egg crepes folded around fillings as blintzes; a rolled Japanese omelet (tamagoyaki) sliced as a sandwich layer.

Melted cheese as structure

Hot cheese flows, cool cheese holds. Alone it makes crisps, shells, bowls and wraps; mixed with egg and almond flour it becomes a kneadable dough. Low-moisture cheeses only; fresh mozzarella has too much water.

Here: fathead dough and its chapter, cheese wraps and shells, cheese noodles, chaffles, parmesan crisps, quiche crust, empanadas and danish.

Beyond: cheese "bread bowls" for soup; a lattice of baked cheese as a pie top; grated-and-baked cheese lace as a crumb topping; provolone sheets wrapped around fillings and baked as "cannelloni".

Isolated gluten

Vital wheat gluten is the protein that made wheat bread work, minus the starch. It kneads, rises, holds shape and boils without falling apart. It also over-develops fast and turns rubbery when there's too much of it.

Here: the whole gluten-blend and pasta chapters.

Beyond: seitan-style "meat" is the same ingredient without the almond flour; a small spoonful in almond-flour doughs gives them a little stretch; gluten pasta dough rolled paper-thin makes lavash and cracker sheets.

Psyllium gel

Psyllium husk swells in hot water into an elastic gel that mimics gluten's stretch in a gluten-free dough and stands in for eggs as a binder. Too much and the crumb turns gummy and grey.

Here: psyllium buns, psyllium pizza crust, almond and coconut tortillas, roti, soda bread, nut & seed loaf, egg-white wraps.

Beyond: a teaspoon in almond-flour cookie dough stops crumbling; ½ tsp in a smoothie thickens it; psyllium and water alone set into a gel that binds veggie burgers; a psyllium-almond dough rolled thin makes egg-free crackers.

Flax and chia mucilage

Ground flax and chia release a mucilage that gels in water, binding without egg, cheese or gum. Slower and softer than psyllium, with omega-3 and a nutty taste.

Here: flax wraps, seed crackers, flax focaccia, nut & seed loaf, the flax in the master blend.

Beyond: a "flax egg" (1 tbsp flax + 3 tbsp water, 5 minutes) replaces an egg as a binder in crackers, flatbreads and quick breads that don't rely on egg for lift; chia soaked overnight is a pudding; flax meal and water pressed flat makes egg-free crispbread.

Squeeze-dry vegetables

Grate or rice a vegetable, cook or salt it, and wring out the water; what's left binds with egg and cheese into a dough that holds a shape. The wringing is the recipe.

Here: cauliflower crust, breadsticks and buns, cauliflower gnocchi, cauliflower and zucchini tortillas, broccoli bread and tots, zucchini bread.

Beyond: zucchini fritters; spinach-and-cheese "bread" from a 10-oz block of frozen spinach squeezed dry plus 2 eggs and a cup of mozzarella; cauliflower "rice cakes" pressed and pan-fried; a cabbage-and-egg pancake (okonomiyaki without the flour).

Alkaline treatment

A baking-soda bath or an alkaline salt raises the surface pH, which speeds browning reactions and changes texture: pretzel skin, bagel chew, ramen spring.

Here: pretzels and pretzel buns, bagels, ramen, kelp noodles (softened with baking soda), homemade konjac (set with lime).

Beyond: the browning wash on any pale loaf; a pinch of baking soda in the fathead dough for deeper colour; alkaline noodles cut wide as "udon".

Protein powder as flour

Egg white protein, and to a lesser extent whey isolate, sets when baked and can carry a whipped-white structure the way flour carries a cake. Nearly zero carb and fat.

Here: egg-white protein bread, its pizza crust and waffles, angel food cake, protein fluff.

Beyond: a tablespoon in the almond pancakes makes them hold together better; protein "bagels" from the bread batter piped in rings; a scoop whipped into the chaffle egg for a leaner chaffle.

Twice-baking and drying

A second, low bake drives out the moisture that makes these high-fat breads stale and mould quickly, and turns soft into crisp.

Here: biscotti, rusks and melba toast, all the crackers, seed crispbread, croutons and crumbs, egg-white crisps, meringues.

Beyond: dry any leftover loaf at 250°F into rusks that keep a month; bake the fathead dough thin and long for a cracker that stores; a low oven turns cheese slices into shelf-stable crisps.

Konjac gel

Glucomannan swells enormously in water and, set with an alkali, becomes a permanent gel: a noodle that is 97% water.

Here: shirataki, konjac rice, homemade konjac noodles, glucomannan as a binder and thickener.

Beyond: cast the gel into sheets for a zero-calorie lasagna layer; konjac "pearls" for drinks; a pinch in tortilla dough for flex.

Rendered fat and cracklings

Rendering separates fat from connective tissue: the fat becomes a cooking medium and the crisp solids become a zero-carb crumb.

Here: crackling rolls, chicken-skin crisps and beef cracklings, tallow for the tortillas, the coating and binding notes.

Beyond: cracklings as a pie-crust base pressed with egg; chicken-skin "taco shells"; cracklings ground with parmesan as a cracker dough.

Taste: where the off-notes come from

Every base in this book has a signature flavour that wheat flour doesn't, and most of what makes keto bread taste odd traces back to a handful of causes, each with a lever. The blue notes on the cards apply these to specific recipes; this is the general theory.

The two universal fixes

Toasting and time. Nearly everything in this book tastes better toasted, because browning supplies the flavour that starch would have given, and nearly every egg-heavy bread tastes better the next day, because the sulfur compounds that make it taste of egg leave as it cools and rests. If a loaf tastes odd fresh, don't judge it until tomorrow's toast.

Leftovers & byproducts

Whipped-white recipes leave yolks, yolk-rich ones leave whites, and the vegetable recipes leave stems and cores. The dotted notes on the cards say what each recipe leaves behind; this is where it all goes.

Yolks

Keeping them. In the fridge, covered with a little cold water so no skin forms, 2 days. To freeze, whisk with ⅛ tsp salt per 4 yolks (or 1½ tsp allulose for sweet uses) so they don't turn gummy, and freeze in ice-cube trays for up to 3 months.

Recipes in this book that eat yolks: cheese noodles (1 each), cauliflower gnocchi (1), mozzarella gnocchi (1), enriched dough (2), brioche buns (2), a yolk-rich pasta dough (4 with 1 whole egg), the ravioli filling (1), and every egg wash (1 yolk + 1 tbsp cream is the best browning wash there is).

Allulose lemon curd (4 yolks)
Whisk 4 yolks, ⅓ cup (65 g) allulose, ⅓ cup lemon juice and the zest of a lemon in a small pan over low heat, 8–10 minutes, until it thickens and reaches 170°F; don't let it boil. Off the heat, whisk in 4 tbsp cold butter a piece at a time, strain, and chill. Keeps 10 days. For meringue kisses, pavlova, cloud bread, or a spoon.
Crème anglaise and ice cream (6 yolks)
Heat 2 cups heavy cream (or 1 cup cream + 1 cup unsweetened almond milk) with a vanilla bean or 1 tsp alcohol-free vanilla until steaming. Whisk 6 yolks with ⅓ cup allulose and a pinch of salt, pour the hot cream in slowly while whisking, return to the pan and stir over low heat to 170–175°F, until it coats a spoon. Strain and chill. That's the sauce for the angel food cake; churned, or frozen and stirred every 30 minutes for 3 hours, it's ice cream that stays scoopable because allulose doesn't freeze hard.
Avocado-oil mayonnaise (1 yolk)
In a jar just wider than an immersion blender: 1 yolk, 1 tsp lemon juice or vinegar, ½ tsp Dijon, ¼ tsp salt, then ¾ cup avocado oil or light olive oil poured on top. Blend from the bottom, lifting slowly, 20 seconds. Keeps a week. Store-bought mayonnaise is soybean or canola oil; this one isn't.
Hollandaise (3 yolks)
Whisk 3 yolks with 1 tbsp lemon juice and 1 tbsp water over barely simmering water until thick and pale, then whisk in 8 tbsp melted butter in a thin stream. Salt, cayenne. Eggs on cloud bread or an English muffin.
Carbonara (3 yolks + 1 egg)
Whisk 3 yolks, 1 whole egg, ½ cup grated parmesan and a lot of black pepper. Crisp 4 oz diced beef bacon or halal smoked turkey in a skillet. Toss hot fettuccine with the bacon off the heat, then the egg mixture, stirring fast so it turns to sauce, not scramble. A splash of the pasta water loosens it.
Salt-cured yolks (6–12 yolks)
Fill a container with 2 cups salt mixed with ½ cup allulose, press wells into it with the back of a spoon, drop a yolk into each, and cover with more of the mix. Fridge 5–7 days until firm. Rinse, pat dry, and dry them out on a rack at 150°F for 2 hours (or hang in cheesecloth in the fridge for 2 weeks). Grate over pasta, gnocchi or soufflé like a hard cheese. Keeps a month in the fridge.
Raviolo al uovo (1 yolk per raviolo)
Pipe a ring of the ricotta filling on a pasta sheet, set a whole yolk in the middle, seal a sheet over it, and boil 2½ minutes. Cut open at the table and the yolk runs into the brown butter.

Whites

Keeping them. Fridge 4 days in a covered jar; freezer up to a year in ice-cube trays, one white per cube. Thawed whites whip as well as fresh, some say better.

What they make, by the count you have: 1 white lightens a chaffle or two, or replaces the egg in the almond crackers. 3 whites: cloud bread, psyllium buns, meringue kisses. 4 whites: egg-white crisps, pavlova. 7 whites: egg-white wraps. 12 whites: protein bread or angel food cake. Any number: an egg-white omelet, a white brushed on a loaf for a crisper crust, or two whites in place of one egg in any quick bread for a lighter, less eggy loaf.

Nutrient boosts

Bread and pasta are vehicles, and keto tends to run short on the things that make people feel flat: sodium and potassium first, then magnesium, with iron worth watching if you eat little red meat. Every recipe card carries a green note saying what it contributes and how to raise it. This table says what each addition actually delivers, because a lot of popular "fortifying" tricks come to almost nothing once divided by sixteen slices.

Iron and energy on keto, plainly

Fatigue in the first weeks is usually electrolytes, not calories. Cutting carbs makes the kidneys shed sodium, and potassium and magnesium follow. Most people need 3–5 g of sodium a day on keto, more than the usual advice, plus 3–4 g potassium and 300–400 mg magnesium. Bread is the easiest vehicle for the sodium: the recipes here salt at 1–1¼ tsp per loaf, and salted crusts, pretzels and crackers are doing you a favour. Potassium comes from spinach and other greens, avocado, pumpkin, tomato and, in small amounts, the lite-salt swap; magnesium from pumpkin seeds, hemp, chia, almonds and cocoa, which is why the seed-heavy recipes are the best-fed ones in the book.

Iron is the other half. Adults need about 8 mg a day (18 mg for women before menopause), and keto without much red meat tends to fall short, because the fortified grains that quietly supplied it are gone. Heme iron from beef, lamb and liver absorbs three to five times better than the iron in seeds and greens, so the meat fillings and the liver-in-the-lahmacun trick do more than any fortified flour. Plant iron needs vitamin C alongside and no tea or coffee within an hour. The whipped-white recipes are worth a special note: whites carry no iron at all, the yolks do, and a book that uses a lot of whites has to send the yolks somewhere. The leftovers section does that.

B vitamins come from whole eggs, meat, liver and fortified nutritional yeast, and iodine, which the thyroid needs to run the whole system, from iodised salt, nori and dulse.

Fiber, the one keto reliably runs short on

Wheat, oats, beans, lentils and most fruit are where fiber used to come from, and all of them are gone. Most people on keto land at 10–15 g a day against a target of 25 g for women and 38 g for men, which shows up as constipation in the first weeks and, longer term, as a gut that isn't being fed. Bread is the natural place to put it back, and this book's bases rank very differently: the psyllium buns (8 g), flax wraps (7 g), seed crackers (7 g per 4), nut & seed loaf (6 g a slice) and flax focaccia (5 g) at the top; the gluten-blend breads in the middle at 2–3 g a slice, mostly from the oat fiber and flax already in the blend; the almond and coconut quick breads at 1–3 g; fathead at about 1 g; and the egg, cheese and whipped-white recipes at zero. Every nutrition strip shows the figure, the fiber filter at the top sorts by it, and every green note ends with what a serving carries and the cheapest way to raise it.

The four additions, in order of usefulness. Oat fiber: pure insoluble fiber with no calories or carbs, tasteless, and the gluten-blend recipes already contain it; ¼ cup adds 17 g to a batch of dough for a slightly drier crumb and 3 tbsp more water. Psyllium husk powder: about 6 g per tablespoon, mostly soluble, and it improves the structure of almost every gluten-free dough as a bonus; more than a tablespoon per batch turns things gummy. Chia seeds: 4 g per tablespoon, and they disappear into a batter. Ground flax: 2 g per tablespoon with omega-3 and a nutty taste. Beyond the bread, avocados (10 g each), raspberries and blackberries (8 g a cup), chia pudding, and the cabbage-family vegetables do the rest.

Two cautions. Raise fiber over a couple of weeks, not overnight, and drink more water as you do; psyllium and glucomannan in particular swell, and taken dry or without water they can cause the blockage they're meant to prevent. Fiber counts on labels for sugar alcohols and "net carb" products are often inflated; the figures in this book count only fiber from food.

Absorption, in four lines

  • Plant iron needs vitamin C in the same meal: lemon, tomato, peppers. Tea and coffee within an hour of an iron-rich meal cut absorption by more than half.
  • Toasting seeds and nuts, or soaking them, reduces the phytate that binds minerals; the nut & seed loaf's long rest does some of this by itself.
  • Spinach's calcium is bound to oxalate and largely wasted; its iron, folate and vitamin K are not. For calcium from greens, use kale or chard.
  • Heme iron from liver and red meat doesn't need any of these tricks, which is why 5 g of liver does what 30 g of spinach can't.

Storage

Every bread in this book is higher in fat and moisture than wheat bread and moulds faster. Cool loaves completely, slice, and refrigerate in a bag for up to a week, or freeze slices with parchment between and toast straight from frozen. Buns, bagels and English muffins: split before freezing. Fathead and cheese-based items keep 4–5 days in the fridge and re-crisp in a hot oven or skillet. Cloud bread and egg noodles: fridge, 4–5 days, in a bag with a paper towel. Fresh gluten pasta: dusted and covered in the fridge 2 days, or frozen in nests. The dry master blend keeps a month at room temperature, longer in the fridge; flaxseed meal on its own should live in the fridge or freezer. Crackers: airtight at room temperature, re-crisp at 300°F for 10 minutes if they soften.

What to skip

Ingredients that aren't keto no matter how the recipe is framed

  • Semolina: around 69 g net carbs per 100 g, the same as regular flour. Out of the keto recipes; it appears only in the purple low-carb notes on the pasta cards, where a quarter cup buys real pasta texture for about 7 g a serving.
  • Chickpea flour: around 48 g net per 100 g, roughly 14 g per ¼ cup. Better than wheat, still past a keto budget in any useful amount; lupin flour is the keto legume flour. It appears only in the purple low-carb notes, where a quarter cup binds a cauliflower crust for under 2 g a slice.
  • Oat flour and oat bran: these are not oat fiber. Full-carb.
  • Cassava, arrowroot and tapioca: common in "grain-free" recipes; they're pure starch.
  • Edamame, black bean and lentil pastas: 20-plus grams of net carbs per 2 oz serving.
  • Bananas, honey, maple syrup, dates in "healthy" bread recipes. A banana is around 24 g net.
  • Anything sold as "low-carb bread" with modified wheat starch or resistant wheat starch high on the label; read the total carb line, not the front of the package.

Things this kind of baking can't do well

  • Croissants and other laminated doughs. Without starch the layers don't set and the butter runs. Every keto croissant recipe is a compromise you'll be disappointed by. If you want layers and butter, the paratha and feteer-style breads get there without the compromise.
  • True sourdough fermentation. A starter needs starch to feed. Use the vinegar trick.
  • Shattering-crisp thin crackers from the gluten blend. They come out firm and snappy, not delicate. The almond and seed crackers are the ones for delicacy.
  • Sandwich bread that tastes exactly like wheat. The gluten loaf is close. Toasting closes most of the remaining gap.
